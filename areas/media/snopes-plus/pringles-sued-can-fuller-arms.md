# "No, Pringles Is Not Getting Sued Because Its Can Discriminates Against 'Fuller' Arms"


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: In January 2025, a rumor circulated on social media — primarily Facebook, Instagram, and X — alleging that a woman named Joy Ryde-Myaz, aged 42, from York, England, was suing snack food company Pringles. The lawsuit claim alleged that Pringles' iconic cylindrical cardboard-and-foil canister "discriminates against those with 'fuller arms' and those with beautiful curves," because the narrow diameter of the can prevents people with larger arms from reaching snacks stored at the bottom. Ryde-Myaz was quoted as saying: "Fifty six times I bought Pringles last year and not a single time was I able to get to the crisp at the bottom." And further: "I've never had a problem with other varieties of crisp. Enough is enough."

**Structure of the claim**: The claim was presented in the style of a local British news report, complete with a named plaintiff, a specific age and location, a quoted grievance, and a narrative of repeated consumer frustration culminating in legal action. This format closely mimics the visual and textual conventions of real local news content in the United Kingdom, which frequently covers unusual consumer disputes and localized legal stories. The mimicry was close enough that some readers interpreted the story as authentic.

**Spread and reception**: The claim spread across Facebook, Instagram, and X in early January 2025. While some users immediately recognized the satirical intent — particularly those familiar with the source or who noticed the punning plaintiff name — others engaged with it as genuine news, speculating about the legal merits of the claim, debating whether Pringles' packaging was genuinely discriminatory, and sharing the story as a real development. Facebook comments on various reposts included reactions treating the story as real: "When I see it then I will start to buy Pringles!" and similar engaged responses.

**The convenient timing**: The claim appeared at a commercially fortuitous moment for satirists: Pringles had just launched Pringles Mingles, a new product line that came in a traditional bag rather than the cylindrical can. The existence of Mingles gave the fake lawsuit claim an additional layer of superficial plausibility — if Pringles were genuinely concerned about access issues, creating a bag version would be a logical response. This contextual detail, present in the real world for reasons entirely unrelated to any lawsuit, made the satire harder for casual readers to immediately dismiss.

**The plaintiff name as embedded signal**: "Joy Ryde-Myaz" is a phonetic pun: "Joy Rides My A--." This type of embedded satirical signal — a pun name embedded in an otherwise straight-faced format — is common in British satirical content but requires a moment of deliberate attention to catch. Readers who skim headlines or share content based on titles alone would miss it entirely, and many did. The joke name is simultaneously a signal to attentive readers that the content is satirical and, by its very subtlety, a trap for inattentive ones.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The source is an explicitly satirical Facebook page**: The claim's origin was definitively identified as Wakefield News, a Facebook page that states in its own "Intro" section: "A news page for people who like a laugh. Nothing on this page is serious. Parody account." This disclaimer is public, accessible to anyone who looks at the Wakefield News page directly. The critical evidentiary point is that Wakefield News does not obscure its satirical nature from readers who engage with it as a page — but when individual posts are shared outside their original context, stripped of page identity, the satirical framing disappears and only the content-as-news remains.

**No lawsuit was filed**: A search of publicly accessible UK court records, legal news databases, and media coverage yields no record of any legal action filed by a person named Joy Ryde-Myaz, or any similar claim against Kellogg's (the current Pringles parent company following the 2012 acquisition) regarding Pringles can dimensions and arm accessibility. No credible news outlet — local, national, or international — reported on any such lawsuit.

**No legal basis would exist for such a claim**: From a legal perspective, a discrimination claim in the UK or US based on product packaging design would face substantial obstacles. UK equality law under the Equality Act 2010 prohibits discrimination in goods and services on grounds including disability and characteristics relating to physical attributes in defined contexts, but product packaging accessibility claims would require demonstration of discriminatory intent or disproportionate impact in a legally recognized protected context. No UK case law establishes a precedent for consumer packaging dimensionality as a basis for equality litigation of this type. US disability rights frameworks under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) primarily cover public accommodations, employment, and state/local government services — not product packaging design.

**The Pringles can's ergonomic design is a real and documented consumer frustration**: While the lawsuit is fictional, the underlying consumer experience it parodies is real and extensively documented. The Pringles can's cylindrical design — a pop-top metal lid over a paper cylinder of approximately 73mm diameter — is genuinely ergonomically challenging for people with larger hands or arms. The bottom third of a standard Pringles can is inaccessible to most people of average adult hand size without inverting the can or using utensils. This is not a secret: Pringles has been the subject of genuine consumer commentary, product reviewers, and satirical content about this design limitation for decades. The joke premise is broadly recognizable as reflecting a universally relatable consumer experience, which is part of why even the fictional version resonated with people who might not have expected its invention.

**Pringles Mingles as inadvertent satirical context**: Pringles launched Pringles Mingles in late 2024 — a two-flavor blend snack packaged in a conventional snack bag rather than the cylindrical can. The product represents Pringles' deliberate extension of its brand into different packaging formats. Whether this represents any internal acknowledgment of the accessibility and consumption challenges of the can format, or merely an extension into a different market segment's packaging norms, is not documented in any Kellogg's/Pringles press materials. The timing appeared to validate the satirical claim's premise: if Pringles were genuinely concerned about can accessibility, a bag format would solve the problem, and they had just released a bag format. This reinforces a pattern documented in satirical content research: effective satire parasitizes real events to gain plausibility.

**Joy Ryde-Myaz as a pun name**: The plaintiff name "Joy Ryde-Myaz" rewards a second reading. Its phonetic rendering ("joy rides my ass") is a rude British-style pun fully consistent with the humor register of Wakefield News's content, which is oriented toward absurdist and slightly transgressive British humor. Satire researchers have identified embedded joke names as a specific technique in British tabloid parody going back decades, used to signal satirical intent while maintaining surface-level mimicry of serious news formats (Day, A. & Thompson, E., 2012, *The Daily Show and Rhetoric: Arguments, Issues and Strategies*, Lexington Books; while addressing US satire, the pun-name tradition is discussed in cross-cultural context).


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The anatomy of a well-constructed satire that escapes its frame**: The epistemic novelty of the Pringles case lies in the precise mechanism by which clearly labeled satire transforms into believed misinformation when it circulates beyond its original platform context. Wakefield News labels itself clearly as satirical on its own page. A reader who arrives at the Wakefield News Facebook page, reads the intro section, and then reads the Pringles post will understand they are reading a joke. But social media sharing does not transport the page context — it transports only the post content. A screenshot of the text, shared to an Instagram story or X post without attribution, arrives in recipients' feeds as uncontextualized text that looks exactly like a real local news story.

This dynamic — labeled satire becoming unlabeled misinformation through decontextualization — has been theorized as a specific category of information disorder. Claire Wardle and Hossein Derakhshan (Wardle, C. & Derakhshan, H., 2017, *Information Disorder: Toward an Interdisciplinary Framework for Research and Policy Making*, Council of Europe) distinguish between "misinformation" (false information shared without harmful intent), "disinformation" (false information shared with harmful intent), and "malinformation" (true information shared to cause harm). The Pringles case represents a fourth condition their framework does not fully capture: satirical content explicitly labeled as such that becomes misinformation through decontextualization. The original producers are not lying. The sharers may not be intentionally deceiving. But the output is still false information spreading as true.

**The plausibility architecture of contemporary satire**: The Pringles claim worked partly because it was targeted at a real product, a real consumer frustration, a real recent product launch, and a real legal vocabulary (discrimination claims and "body positivity" language are prominent in contemporary public discourse). Research on the "truth bias" in communication — the default psychological disposition to believe received communications unless there is a clear signal to the contrary (Levine, T.R., 2014, *Communicating Deception*, Peter Lang) — suggests that readers without clear contrary signals will lean toward crediting a text as factual. The Wakefield News format removed most of the normal signals that trigger skepticism: there were no obvious logical impossibilities, no implausible statistics, and no tone that obviously differed from legitimate local news.

**Brand parody as a specific high-engagement satirical genre**: Food brand lawsuits have become a recurring satirical genre because they are simultaneously believable (real unusual food lawsuits do occur — McDonald's coffee, Subway footlong measurement, Starbucks underfilled cups) and inherently absurd (applying legal frameworks to beloved snack products). Research on brand-related misinformation by Wojdynski, B. and colleagues (Wojdynski, B., Evans, N., & Hoy, M., 2018, *Journal of Advertising*, "How Doctored Content Can Spread via Social Media") documents that fake brand news circulates with particularly high velocity because of the combination of brand familiarity (which lowers cognitive cost), established prior schema for brand controversies (which increases plausibility), and social signaling value of sharing brand stories (perceived as relevant to consumer-peer networks).

**The "body positivity" framing and political salience**: The specific framing of the Pringles claim — arm size as a form of body type discrimination deserving legal protection — tapped into a discourse environment in which "body positivity" language and expansions of size-related accommodation norms are genuinely contested cultural territory. Some readers who engaged skeptically with the Pringles claim as a legal matter still found its underlying grievance relatable or its cultural politics interesting as a comment on contemporary discourse. The satirical content thus generated engagement from multiple directions simultaneously: people sharing it as funny, people sharing it as an example of absurd social justice rhetoric, and people sharing it as genuine news. This multi-directional engagement is characteristic of high-performing satire that is also political, as documented in research on political satire's reception (Baumgartner, J. & Morris, J., 2006, "The Daily Show Effect: Candidate Evaluations, Efficacy, and American Youth," *American Politics Research*, 34(3), 341–367).


## 4. The Broader Context

**The Wakefield News satire ecosystem and British tabloid parody**: Wakefield News is part of a broader ecosystem of British regional parody news pages on Facebook, many of which mimic the format of local UK papers and websites. These pages range in quality and transparency — some are clearly marked as satire, some are ambiguous, some are locally understood jokes that become confusing when shared nationally or internationally. The UK's strong tradition of tabloid journalism, with its attendant absurdities, creates excellent raw material for parody: the real tabloid headline style is itself frequently indistinguishable from parody.

British media literacy research (Ofcom, 2022, *News Consumption in the UK: 2022*) has documented that fake news awareness is higher in the UK than in some comparable countries but that awareness of specific satirical sources varies dramatically by age group, with older Facebook users less likely to recognize regional social parody pages as non-credible sources.

**Pringles can design history and the path to Mingles**: The Pringles can was designed by Fredric Baur in the 1970s (Baur, F., US Patent 3,498,508, 1970) as a solution to the problem of stacking saddle-shaped chips — technically called "hyperbolic paraboloid" chips — without breakage. The cylindrical canister solved a packaging problem (fragility of curved chips) while creating a new consumption problem (depth inaccessibility). Baur was reportedly proud enough of his invention that his ashes were buried in a Pringles can at his request in 2008. Kellogg's acquisition of Pringles from Procter & Gamble in 2012 for $2.7 billion maintained the brand identity including the canister format, which had become inseparable from brand recognition. The introduction of Mingles in bag format in 2024 represents the first mainstream Pringles product to abandon the canister format, though the reasons are market positioning rather than accessibility accommodation.

**The recurring pattern of fake food lawsuits in satirical content**: The Pringles case is not an isolated incident. Snopes has documented multiple recent instances of fake food and brand lawsuits originating from satirical sources: fictional lawsuits against sperm donors, TikTok stars, and Elon Musk, among others. What makes food brand fake lawsuits particularly resilient as a satirical genre is the deep public familiarity with the McDonald's hot coffee case (Liebeck v. McDonald's, 1994), which established in popular consciousness that product-related personal injury lawsuits can succeed even when they appear on their face to be absurd. Subsequent similar lawsuits — the Subway footlong, the Starbucks underfill, the Ben & Jerry's "all-natural" flavor — have reinforced the public's awareness that companies regularly face and sometimes settle or lose seemingly small product complaints. This reality makes fake versions of the genre extremely hard to rule out on the basis of implausibility alone.

**Satire labeling, platform responsibility, and information integrity**: The Pringles case renewed questions about platform responsibility for satire that escapes its labeled context. Meta (Facebook) and X have both implemented content labeling systems for satire in some circumstances, but these labels are applied inconsistently, do not travel with content when it is screenshot and re-shared as an image, and are not applied to content from pages that self-identify as satirical. A finding by the Reuters Institute for the Study of Journalism (Reuters Institute, 2023, *Digital News Report*) noted that platform satire-labeling systems reach a minority of the audience that encounters the unlabeled version of the same content. The gap between labeling capability and labeling reach is a structural feature of the current social media architecture.


## 5. The Verdict

**Snopes rating — LABELED SATIRE**. The claim that a woman named Joy Ryde-Myaz was suing Pringles for can-dimension discrimination is fabricated satire. The story originated with Wakefield News, a Facebook page that explicitly identifies itself as a parody account producing humorous non-serious content. No lawsuit has been filed, no credible news outlet reported the claim, and "Joy Ryde-Myaz" is a fabricated pun name. The claim spread because it was well-constructed satire that mimicked the format of real local news, tapped into a genuinely relatable consumer frustration, and was decontextualized when shared beyond its original labeled satirical source. Pringles Mingles, the bag-format Pringles variant, exists but was not launched in response to any lawsuit or accessibility complaint — it is a product diversification effort.


## 6. Knowledge Gaps and Open Questions

**Gap 1 — Satire decontextualization rates across platforms**: No publicly available research quantifies what fraction of a satirical Facebook post's secondary shares occur without the original source context visible to the receiving audience. Understanding the rate at which labeled satire becomes unlabeled misinformation would be valuable for platform policy, media literacy education, and fact-checking resource prioritization. Methodologically, this would require cooperation from platform data teams or large-scale manual auditing of share chains — neither of which is currently available for systematic research.

**Gap 2 — Pringles can accessibility research**: Despite widespread consumer commentary on arm/hand accessibility challenges with the Pringles canister, no peer-reviewed ergonomics or human factors research appears to have formally studied the issue. Consumer product ergonomics research (Cushman, W.H. & Rosenberg, D.J., 1991, *Human Factors in Product Design*, Elsevier) provides frameworks for evaluating packaging accessibility across population hand-size distributions, but these frameworks have not been applied publicly to the Pringles can. Such research would have both practical product design value and could resolve whether the distribution of hand sizes relative to can diameter genuinely produces disproportionate access difficulty for specific demographic segments.

**Gap 3 — British regional satirical news ecosystem**: The Wakefield News page and its peer satirical local news pages represent an understudied layer of the UK digital information ecosystem. How many such pages exist, what their total combined reach is, what fraction of their content escapes into wider information circulation as decontextualized misinformation, and how their track records compare across transparency, accuracy, and community perception are all open questions. UK-focused media literacy research tends to focus on national and international outlets; the regional satirical news genre has not received systematic academic attention.

**Gap 4 — Consumer behavior post-satire correction**: Following Snopes and other fact-checkers labeling the Pringles claim as satire, what fraction of users who had originally believed and/or shared the claim updated their understanding? Research on belief revision and fact-checking in social media contexts (Walter, N. & Murphy, S.T., 2018, *Political Communication*, "How to unring the bell: A meta-analytic approach to correction of misinformation") documents significant variance in correction uptake depending on the format of the correction, the platform on which it is delivered, and whether the corrected belief aligns with prior attitudes. No study specifically examining satire-correction dynamics for food brand claims has been published.
