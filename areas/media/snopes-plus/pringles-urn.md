## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In December 2014, a rumor began circulating—apparently transmitted through casual school-age social networks—that the inventor of Pringles potato chips had been cremated and his ashes mixed into cans of Pringles and sold to unsuspecting customers. A parent reported to Snopes that their daughter came home from school stating she would "never eat Pringles brand chips because the 'creator' of Pringles was cremated when he died and his ashes were put into cans of Pringles and sold to unknowing customers."

Snopes staff writer Dan Evon (2014) investigated the claim and rated it False. The verdict is based on a straightforward factual correction: while it is true that Fredric J. Baur—the man who designed the Pringles packaging system—had a portion of his cremated remains buried inside a Pringles can, that can was never sold to customers. The can was purchased from a Walgreen's store by Baur's son Larry on the way to the funeral home and used as a burial vessel in Baur's grave at a cemetery in suburban Springfield Township, Ohio. The remaining ashes were distributed between an urn buried alongside the Pringles can and a second urn given to a grandson.

The story of Baur's burial preference was publicly reported by the Associated Press on June 8, 2008—more than six years before the false rumor began circulating in late 2014. The AP report was the source of public knowledge about the Pringles can burial, and the Snopes investigation's core task was to trace the rumor's transformation of a genuine and publicly documented human-interest story (inventor buried in his creation) into a consumer contamination scare (inventor's ashes sold to customers in product cans).

This entry examines Fredric Baur's documented contributions to the Pringles concept, the cultural and psychological dimensions of burial with symbolically meaningful objects, the specific mutation mechanism by which the true story became a false consumer scare, and what the longevity and persistence of this type of urban legend reveals about how information degrades as it passes through informal networks.

## §2 Fredric J. Baur: The Inventor, the Can, and the Man

Fredric J. Baur is a figure who exists at the intersection of industrial chemistry, consumer product design, and the slightly eccentric territory of intentional eccentricity—the zone occupied by people who are, to an unusual degree, defined by a particular thing they made.

Fredric John Baur was born in 1918 and died on May 4, 2008, at the age of 89, at Vitas Hospice in Cincinnati, Ohio. He was a graduate of the University of Dayton and Ohio State University and worked as a food storage technician and organic chemist. He joined Procter & Gamble (P&G) as a research and development chemist, where he worked for much of his professional career.

Baur's specific contribution to what became Pringles was the design of the packaging system: the distinctive saddle-shaped hyperbolic paraboloid chip and the tall cylindrical canister that allows them to be stacked uniformly without breaking. The geometric insight was that conventional potato chips—irregularly shaped and fragile—were poorly suited to mechanical packaging because they could not be neatly stacked or densely but gently packed. A chip molded into a consistent saddle shape could, in principle, be stacked in uniform columns, dramatically reducing breakage and air space in the packaging.

Baur was awarded U.S. Patent 3,498,798 on March 3, 1970, titled "Apparatus for Fabricating Chip-Type Food Products." The patent described the saddle-shaped chip form and the process for manufacturing it—mixing dehydrated potato flakes with water and other starch-based ingredients, rolling the mixture flat, cutting it to shape, and forming it over a curved mold before frying. The cylindrical canister that became the Pringle's signature packaging was subsequent development partly attributed to Baur as well; U.S. Patent 3,596,651, granted August 3, 1971, covered the vacuum-pack canister system.

Several important nuances about Baur's role deserve acknowledgment. The Pringles product as sold is not solely Baur's invention—the flavor development, the brand naming, the marketing strategy, and subsequent manufacturing refinements involved extensive teams of P&G researchers and product developers. The chip itself had a famously contentious FDA regulatory history: in 1975, the FDA proposed that Pringles could not be labeled "potato chips" because they were manufactured from a dehydrated potato product rather than sliced fresh potatoes. P&G ultimately settled on the designation "Pringle's Potato Crisps," a naming compromise that is preserved in the current product branding. Andrew Martin's April 2011 *New York Times* article, "Once a Great Flop, Now Sold for Billions," traced the financial trajectory of Pringles from a troubled launch in 1968 to its eventual enormous commercial success and eventual sale by P&G.

The specific attribution of Pringles credit is also not without complication. Alexander Liepa, another P&G researcher, received a patent in 1975 (U.S. Patent 3,923,145) for a method of enhancing the flavor of Pringles, and subsequent reformulations and global adaptations involved many hands. The "creator of Pringles" attribution that appears in both the urban legend and casual media coverage is a significant simplification of a collaborative industrial innovation process.

Baur's burial request—documented by his children and reported by the AP—reflects both a specific personality and a broader cultural logic. His son Larry Baur said the family "briefly debated what flavor to use" when purchasing the Pringles can at Walgreen's on the way to the funeral home, ultimately choosing Original. "I said, 'Look we need to use the original,'" Larry told the AP. The anecdote is charming precisely because it captures both the seriousness of the desire—Baur genuinely wanted this—and the domestic ordinariness of its execution: a stop at a chain drugstore, a brief debate about flavor, and a can of chips as a memorial vessel.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Urban Legend Mutation and the Contamination Scare Pattern

The epistemically distinctive feature of the Pringles cremation claim is that it represents a specific and well-documented category of urban legend: the consumer contamination scare, which mutates an innocuous or affirmative true story into a frightening false one. Understanding this mutation pattern illuminates something important about how misinformation propagates through informal oral and social networks.

The true story—inventor requests burial in product of his creation, family honors the request, it's heartwarming—was publicly reported and well-documented. It requires no special access to verify. The AP reported it in 2008 with named sources (the Baur family), specific details (Springfield Township, the Walgreen's stop, the debate about flavor), and contextual information about Baur's life and contributions. By any journalistic standard, this is a confirmed, documented, warm-toned human-interest story.

The false story—inventor's ashes were sold to customers in product cans—shares the same two core elements (inventor, cremated, Pringles can) but reverses the consumer impact from none (the can went into a grave) to alarming (the ashes went into snack products eaten by unwitting consumers). This reversal is structurally characteristic of what folklorists call the "contamination" motif in urban legends: the idea that something harmful has been secretly introduced into a consumer product and consumed unknowingly.

Jan Harold Brunvand, the foremost academic collector and analyst of American urban legends, documented the contamination motif across dozens of cases in his landmark collections *The Vanishing Hitchhiker* (1981, Norton) and *The Choking Doberman* (1984, Norton). Brunvand identified several recurring structural patterns: the claim typically involves a named national brand or widely consumed product; the contamination is described as accidental or the result of a shocking but specific incident; the story is told as having happened to "a friend's neighbor" or a recently heard case; and the narrative generates powerful disgust responses that make it highly memorable and retransmissible.

The Pringles cremation story fits this template precisely. The structural move that enables the urban legend is minimal: where the true story has "can purchased at store, used for burial, placed in grave," the legend inserts "can returned to store, mixed into distribution, sold to consumers." The factual change is trivially small—a reversal of the can's destination—but the narrative consequence is enormous, transforming a story of individual family sentiment into a widespread consumer contamination event.

Rozin and Fallon (1987, *Psychological Review*, APA) documented the psychology of contamination disgust, finding that the disgust response to contamination is extremely robust across cultures, highly resistant to logical override, and strongly influenced by even symbolic or trace contact with disgusting agents. Their concept of "magical contagion" describes the pervasive intuition that contaminants transfer from a source to a target and persist there even after physical separation. Under this framework, the mere association of Baur's ashes with the Pringles can manufacturing process—even if logically impossible—generates a powerful visceral response that persists even after the story's falseness is established.

This psychological dynamic explains why the story was effective as a schoolyard rumor. Children encountering the claim would experience an immediate disgust-activation response ("someone's ashes were in the chips I've eaten") that is distinctive, emotionally impactful, and highly memorable. The story has high fidelity to the contamination template precisely because the underlying true story—a person being buried in a Pringles can—provides a plausible starting point for the false modification. It is not a story invented from nothing; it mutates from a recorded fact.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Evon's (2014) investigation accurately identifies the false claim, provides the factual correction, and cites the AP source that documents the actual burial. It is a concise, appropriately targeted fact-check. Several threads merit further development in a more expanded treatment:

**Baur's full patent record and inventive contribution**: The investigation describes Baur as "the man who designed the can used to package Pringles potato chips." This is accurate but incomplete—it omits his role in the chip's shape design, which preceded and enabled the packaging design. A more complete account of Baur's inventive contribution would distinguish the chip shape (the hyperbolic paraboloid) from the canister (the cylindrical airtight packaging) and acknowledge that both were Baur's documented contributions.

**The P&G organizational context**: The development of Pringles was a large P&G corporate project involving hundreds of employees across research, food science, manufacturing, and marketing. Baur's contribution—however genuine and foundational—was embedded in that organizational context. The investigation's focus on Baur as the singular "creator" of Pringles, while appropriate for the rumor's specific claim, perpetuates a somewhat simplified inventor-centric narrative.

**The history of the AP report's public reception**: The AP reported Baur's burial in 2008 and the story received wide public attention. Understanding how the story traveled from warm-toned AP feature in 2008 to consumer contamination scare in 2014 would illuminate the specific transmission pathways—did it appear in a Snapchat chain? A specific WhatsApp group? A text message chain?—and potentially help identify how the mutation occurred. Six years is a long time for a true story to mutate into a false one, and the specific pathway matters for understanding misinformation diffusion.

**Contemporary parallels**: Consumer contamination scams and urban legends involving fast food and snack brands are perennial; examples include recurring false claims about soda cans with rat urine, spiders in hair care products, and human body parts in fast food. Situating the Pringles story within this broader field would help readers recognize the template and develop appropriate default skepticism.

**Baur's legacy**: The investigation does not discuss Baur's life beyond the can and the burial. A fuller account would note his career at P&G, his educational background, and the recognition his contribution received during his lifetime and after his death.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Burial With Beloved Objects and the Semiotics of Pringles

Fredric Baur's burial request—that a portion of his cremated remains be interred in a Pringles can—is not as unusual as it might initially appear. The practice of burial with personal objects of significance reaches back at least 100,000 years in the archaeological record and is documented across virtually every human culture.

Neanderthal burial sites from Shanidar Cave in Iraq (dated approximately 60,000 BP) included what Solecki (1977, *Science*, AAAS) interpreted as evidence of flower burial—offerings of meaning placed with the dead. Egyptian burial practices famously included elaborate grave goods ranging from ushabti figurines to full-scale model boats intended to serve the deceased in the afterlife. Viking ship burials interred warriors with weapons, horses, and goods for the voyage to Valhalla. The objects accompanying the dead are among the most archaeologically and anthropologically revealing artifacts precisely because they reflect what the living believed mattered in the world—what tools, weapons, sacred objects, or personal items were worth taking with you.

In contemporary American funeral practice, personalized burial and cremation choices have become increasingly common as the funeral industry has moved away from standardized ritual toward individualized "celebration of life" frameworks. The National Funeral Directors Association (NFDA) has documented a long-term trend toward personalization in funeral arrangements, including non-traditional urn choices, personalized casket designs, and memorial objects. Cremation rates in the United States exceeded 50% for the first time in 2016 (NFDA, 2017, annual report), and as cremation has become the modal choice, the secondary question of what to do with remains has generated significant commercial and personal creativity.

Baur's specific choice—a Pringles can—is notable for its simplicity and directness. He did not commission a custom urn or a replica engineering artifact. He chose an ordinary retail product, the thing he had helped create and that had become ubiquitous in American snack culture. The choice communicates something about how Baur understood his own contribution: not as an abstract intellectual achievement but as a physical object that millions of people had handled, opened, and enjoyed.

Larry Baur's report of the family debate about flavors—a brief discussion ending with "we need to use the original"—captures the texture of how families make small decisions in moments of ritual significance. The "original" choice is semantically apt: it was his original contribution, and the original flavor represents the un-modified, baseline Pringle, the product closest to its conception.

The false rumor that spread from this true story reflects a particular cultural anxiety about the opacity of industrial food production. Consumers who cannot see the manufacturing process of products they eat are structurally vulnerable to believing that things they cannot verify (the origin of the can, the contents of the product) might be corrupted or contaminated. The contamination scare format exploits this structural uncertainty: because consumers cannot directly observe what goes into food products, the claim that something objectionable was added is not directly disprovable from the consumer's position.

Procter & Gamble sold the Pringles brand to Kellogg's in May 2012 for approximately $2.695 billion in cash, in what Andrew Martin's *New York Times* article described as the culmination of a decades-long commercial success story that had begun as "a great flop." By the time Kellogg's acquired the brand, annual Pringles sales exceeded $1.4 billion globally. The fact that a packaging invention—a can and a saddle-shaped chip—could generate that level of commercial value is itself a testament to how consequential Baur's specific contribution was, even if he was one of many contributors to the Pringles ecosystem.

Baur was survived by his children Larry, Linda (of Mississippi), and one other, as well as at least one grandson who received a separate urn of his remains. His death at 89 came approximately 38 years after the first Pringles products reached the market—a span long enough that he lived to see his packaging innovation become one of the most recognizable consumer product designs in the world.

## §6 Verdict Assessment and Broader Implications

The Snopes False verdict for the claim that Pringles creator Fredric Baur's ashes were packaged and sold to customers is well-founded and clearly supported. The documented facts—the AP report, the named family sources, the specific logistical details of buying the can at Walgreen's—leave no ambiguity about what happened: a man was buried in a Pringles can that his family purchased at a drugstore on the way to the funeral home. No Pringles manufacturing facility was involved.

The "context" box that Evon added in the 2021 reformatting of the article—"While it's true that Fredric J. Baur had his ashes buried inside a Pringles can, that can was never sold to customers"—is an appropriately careful framing that acknowledges the true story underlying the false rumor.

The broader implications illuminate several themes:

**For understanding urban legend mechanics**, the Pringles story is a case study in minimal mutation: the transformation of a true story into a contamination scare requires only a small factual change (the destination of the can) while preserving the emotional impact of the original story. This minimal-mutation pattern is particularly virulent because the core true story provides a credible foundation for the false addition.

**For consumers**, the appropriate response to hearing that "someone's body was found in a commercial food product" is immediate verification rather than behavioral change. The contamination scare format is reliable enough in its falseness that it warrants default skepticism, not because contamination events never occur, but because legitimate contamination events are handled by regulatory agencies and generate official recalls—they are not discovered through schoolyard rumors.

**For the broader history of food packaging**, Fredric Baur's contribution deserves recognition independent of the urban legend. The Pringles can solved a genuine packaging engineering problem—delivering fragile, regularly-shaped snack items at scale without breakage—and its design principles have been applied in numerous subsequent packaging contexts. Baur's decision to want to be buried in his invention is an unusual but genuine expression of how he understood his own life's work.

The story of the Pringles can's inventor and his burial choice is one that works as a warm human-interest story—a chemist so identified with his creation that he wanted to carry it with him into death—and it suffers no diminishment from the fact that the attached false rumor is false. The reality is in some ways more interesting than the rumor: a person genuinely took such pride in packaging design that it became integral to his desired final disposition. That is a kind of creative ownership, and an unusual one.

**EARC classification: A (Assertion)** — The claim is a discrete falsifiable factual claim about a historical event. Gap classification: **distorted-but-grounded** — the underlying true story (burial in Pringles can) is real and well-documented; the false claim (ashes sold to customers) is a minimal narrative mutation that inverts the can's destination. The Snopes investigation accurately handled the false claim but left underexplored the urban legend mutation mechanism, Baur's full inventive contribution, and the broader cultural context of burial with personal objects.
