# "Longer Prison Sentences Deter Crime"


## 1. The Claim

Increasing the length of prison sentences deters potential offenders by raising the expected cost of criminal behavior. Under this model, rational actors calculate the risks and rewards of crime; longer sentences increase the cost calculation, and crime rates decline accordingly. The policy prescription follows directly: to reduce crime, impose harsher sentences.

This claim has been a foundational premise of American criminal justice policy since the 1970s and undergirds a legislative history that includes mandatory minimum sentencing (Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, expanded repeatedly thereafter), three-strikes laws (California Proposition 184, 1994; subsequently adopted by twenty-four states), truth-in-sentencing provisions tying parole to minimum time served, and habitual offender statutes that escalate sentences with each conviction. The U.S. incarcerated approximately 500,000 people in 1980; by 2009, that figure had grown to 2.3 million—the highest absolute and per-capita incarceration rate in the world. The theory underlying this transformation was that severity of punishment would translate into deterrence.

The claim has intuitive appeal grounded in classical economic rationality: if something costs more, people will do less of it. But the specific mechanism—that sentence *length* in particular is the operative deterrent variable—is where the evidence has always been more complicated.


## 2. What's Actually True

The criminological literature has converged on a distinction that is politically inconvenient but empirically robust: **certainty of punishment deters more than severity of punishment**. This distinction, present in the foundational literature and substantially confirmed in subsequent decades, means that the policy emphasis on sentence length is aimed at the wrong variable.

**Daniel Nagin (2013)**, a Carnegie Mellon criminologist who has spent his career synthesizing the deterrence literature, published the most comprehensive recent review in *Journal of Criminal Law and Criminology*. His conclusions were unambiguous: the evidence for a substantial deterrent effect of sentence *severity* is weak and inconsistent, while the evidence that *certainty* of apprehension has a deterrent effect is substantially stronger. The probability of getting caught matters; the additional prison time conditional on being caught matters much less, especially at the margins relevant to most policy debates.

The mechanism is not difficult to understand once you disaggregate the rational actor model. Most crimes are not committed by people with accurate information about sentencing guidelines who are running cost-benefit calculations. They are committed by people in states of acute intoxication, emotional arousal, or situational pressure; by people whose future discounting is steep enough that long-horizon punishment is barely registered; or by people operating in social environments where crime is normative and the probability of apprehension is assessed—often accurately—as low. The effective deterrent for such actors is the immediate risk of being caught, not the sentence that follows arrest.

**The cross-national evidence is damaging to the severity model.** The United States has the highest incarceration rate in the world—roughly 639 per 100,000 population as of 2023, compared to 146 for the United Kingdom, 67 for Canada, and 59 for Germany (World Prison Brief). If longer sentences deterred crime, the U.S. should have among the lowest crime rates. It does not. U.S. violent crime rates—particularly homicide—remain substantially higher than peer nations with far lower incarceration rates. The Nordic countries, which rely on shorter sentences, restorative approaches, and rehabilitation-oriented prison design, consistently rank among the safest in the world. No legitimate criminologist attributes this entirely to incarceration policy, but the pattern is the opposite of what the severity-deterrence model predicts.

**The incapacitation effect is real but bounded.** Even critics of the deterrence model acknowledge that incarceration reduces crime through a separate mechanism: incapacitation. A person in prison cannot commit crimes in the community while they are there. This is not a trivial effect—studies of high-rate offenders, particularly in property crime, find meaningful crime reduction attributable to incapacitation. But the effect diminishes with scale. Imprisoning the highest-rate offenders produces the greatest crime reduction per incarcerated person; continuing to incarcerate beyond that point—including the substantial expansion driven by drug offenses and low-level property crimes—produces diminishing returns. The marginal incarcerated person in an overcrowded system is not the same as the high-rate violent offender.

**Crime displacement complicates the accounting further.** Incarcerated individuals are often replaced in drug markets, gang hierarchies, and street economies by others—displacement of criminal activity rather than elimination of it. Levitt's influential 2004 *Journal of Economic Perspectives* paper, "Understanding Why Crime Fell in the 1990s," found that incarceration did contribute to the crime decline of that decade, but attributed only about a third of the decline to prison growth; he assigned roughly equal importance to the legalization of abortion (Donohue and Levitt's controversial hypothesis), the decline in crack cocaine markets, increases in police numbers, and other factors. The incarceration contribution was real but embedded in a causal network.

**Recidivism data** challenge the rehabilitation premise embedded in the severity claim. Longer sentences increase time served but do not consistently produce lower recidivism. Bureau of Justice Statistics studies tracking released prisoners have consistently found five-year reearrest rates of 75–80%, with no significant variation corresponding to sentence length in most analyses. The U.S. prison experience appears neither to rehabilitate nor to deter through personal experience—the "specific deterrence" that would be generated if the experience of prison convincingly discouraged future offending.

**Drug offenses** provide the sharpest test case. Mandatory minimum sentences for drug trafficking were designed explicitly on the deterrence model—raise the cost of the activity, reduce the activity. Decades of drug war enforcement at unprecedented scale has not eliminated or substantially reduced drug use and trafficking; drug markets have persisted, adapted, and in some periods expanded despite mass incarceration of participants. The demand-side of drug markets is largely inelastic to supply-side interdiction and punishment.

The honest summary: incarceration reduces crime through incapacitation in a real but bounded way; longer sentences deter some crimes in some circumstances; but the claim that sentence length is the primary lever of crime control is not supported by the evidence, and the U.S. has conducted a forty-year natural experiment in severity that has failed to validate the underlying theory.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The deterrence model has a long intellectual lineage and deep moral intuitions supporting it.

**Classical roots:** The claim traces to the Enlightenment reformers—Beccaria's *On Crimes and Punishments* (1764) and Bentham's utilitarian calculus. Beccaria actually argued for *certainty* and *promptness* over *severity*, and explicitly warned that excessive severity would undermine the deterrent effect by making juries reluctant to convict. But his successors tended to emphasize the economic logic of pain avoidance and to build from it a theory of calibrated punishment in which severity is the primary dial. The academic pedigree gave the model intellectual legitimacy.

**Intuitive rational actor model:** The logic "if it costs more, people do less of it" is compelling because it is true in many domains of human behavior. Price increases do reduce consumption. Penalties do reduce tax evasion among careful planners. The error is in the generalization—in assuming that the populations committing violent street crime, impulsive property crime, or addiction-driven drug offenses are price-sensitive in the way that the model requires. The deterrence model was calibrated against rational, future-oriented, information-accurate actors; most crime is committed by people who match that description poorly.

**The politics of severity:** "Tough on crime" has been a durable political identity since the 1960s. Politicians who endorse longer sentences can point to a visible, comprehensible policy action; politicians who argue for certainty-based approaches must explain the difference between certainty and severity to an electorate that experiences the distinction as splitting hairs. The optics of severity—expressing society's condemnation, giving victims satisfaction, demonstrating seriousness—deliver political value that is independent of the empirical effectiveness of the policy.

**The 1990s crime decline:** U.S. crime rates fell sharply in the 1990s, a period that coincided with substantial increases in incarceration. This correlation was widely interpreted as evidence that incarceration worked. The confounds were severe—demographic changes, the decline of crack markets, economic expansion, police force expansion, abortion legalization effects—and econometric disentanglement has attributed only partial and contested credit to incarceration. But the temporal correlation was politically and rhetorically sufficient to reinforce the model among policymakers.

**Moral versus empirical reasoning:** For many supporters, deterrence via severity is as much a moral claim as an empirical one—proportional punishment is intrinsically justified, and rehabilitation or short sentences represent insufficient condemnation of serious crimes. On this view, the deterrence argument is supplementary justification for a conclusion grounded in retributive moral intuitions. When the empirical deterrence argument is challenged, the moral argument survives independently. The conflation of empirical and moral claims means that empirical refutation does not necessarily change positions.


## 4. Verdict

**Classification: Contested Consensus**

The deterrence-via-severity claim is not simply wrong—deterrence effects exist and incapacitation is real—but the strong form of the claim, which has driven four decades of American sentencing policy, is substantially contradicted by the evidence.

**What the evidence supports:**
- Certainty of punishment deters crime more reliably than severity (Nagin 2013, consistent with foundational Beccaria/Bentham distinction)
- Incapacitation—keeping specific individuals from offending during incarceration—produces measurable crime reduction
- Incarceration contributed to part of the 1990s U.S. crime decline, though its share is debated and declining returns have been documented since
- For deliberative, economically rational crimes (tax evasion, some white-collar crime), sentence severity has more plausible deterrent effects
- The first offense of incarceration may have modest specific deterrent effects for some offender profiles

**What the evidence contradicts:**
- That sentence length is the primary deterrent variable—certainty consistently outperforms severity in the empirical literature
- That the U.S. incarceration experiment has delivered crime reduction proportional to its scale and cost—cross-national comparisons do not support this
- That drug trafficking can be substantially deterred by mandatory minimums—forty years of evidence does not support the claim
- That longer sentences reduce recidivism—recidivism rates have remained persistently high across sentence length variations
- That incapacitation at the scale the U.S. has pursued is cost-effective—returns diminish sharply past the highest-rate offenders

**The genuine contest:** This is not a case where one side is right and the other wrong—there are legitimate debates about what crimes are deterrable, what the causal mechanisms are, how to attribute crime trends to individual policy instruments, and how to weigh incapacitation benefits against the documented social costs of mass incarceration. What is not contested is the specific claim that severity is the primary lever: that is where the evidence is most consistently against the dominant policy model.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The sentence-severity debate connects to some of the deepest questions in criminology, social policy, and philosophy of punishment.

**The social costs of mass incarceration** have become a substantial literature in their own right. Bruce Western's *Punishment and Inequality in America* (2006) documented that incarceration concentrates in low-income African American communities at rates that constitute a structural social fact, not an incidental byproduct of crime rates. Todd Clear's *Imprisoning Communities* (2007) argued that incarceration at high rates *increases* crime in the communities most affected, by removing men from family and economic networks, reducing social capital, and returning individuals with diminished employment prospects and social ties into the same environments that produced their offenses. This "coercive mobility" hypothesis—that incarceration disrupts community structures enough to produce criminogenic effects that partially offset incapacitation benefits—remains debated but has substantial empirical support.

**The certainty vs. severity distinction** has had some policy influence in the form of evidence-based policing. David Kennedy's "focused deterrence" strategies—making certainty of apprehension very high for specific violent offenders while offering social services and community support—have shown meaningful crime reductions in cities including Boston, Oakland, and Cincinnati. The approach operationalizes the evidence by aiming at the correct deterrence variable. Its success provides an indirect test of the theory.

**Comparative sentencing research** finds that countries with shorter sentences and stronger rehabilitation orientations—Norway, Germany, the Netherlands—have dramatically lower recidivism rates than the United States. Norwegian recidivism is approximately 20%; U.S. recidivism approaches 80% within five years of release. Multiple explanatory factors are in play, but incarceration length is not the variable driving that difference in the direction the severity model predicts.

**Natural experiments** from mandatory minimum reforms have produced cautiously useful data. Several U.S. states that have reduced mandatory minimums for drug offenses in the 2010s have not seen corresponding increases in drug crime. Proposition 47 in California (2014), which reclassified certain drug and property offenses from felonies to misdemeanors, has been extensively studied; the evidence on its crime effects is mixed and contested, but does not show a clear crime increase attributable to sentence reduction.

**International criminology consensus** does not endorse the U.S. severity model. The United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime, the World Health Organization's guidance on drug policy, and major criminological bodies in Europe and Australasia have moved toward certainty-focused, rehabilitation-oriented, and harm-reduction frameworks that reflect the evidence on deterrence. The U.S. model is an international outlier, not the global standard.

The deterrence-via-severity hypothesis is not discredited—it has a domain of validity for specific crime types and specific offender profiles. But as a general theory of criminal justice that justifies mass incarceration, mandatory minimums, and sentence escalation as the primary crime-control instruments, it has not been validated by the experiment it inspired.

**The racial disparity dimension** cannot be separated from the deterrence policy context. The mass incarceration expansion disproportionately affected Black Americans at every stage—arrest rates, charging decisions, plea bargaining outcomes, and sentence lengths. If the deterrence theory were accurate and the policy effective, this disproportionate application should have produced disproportionate crime reduction in those communities. The evidence does not show this. The communities most saturated with incarceration-based deterrence policy did not experience crime reductions commensurate with incarceration rates, while simultaneously bearing severe collateral costs: children without parents, workforce participation reduced, community social capital depleted. The deterrence model's failure is not evenly distributed.

**Opportunity cost framing** is underused in public deterrence debates. The approximately $35,000–$60,000 annual cost of incarcerating one person in the United States could alternatively fund education, job training, substance abuse treatment, or mental health services—all of which have evidence bases for crime reduction comparable to or stronger than incarceration at the margin. The question is not only whether incarceration deters, but whether incarceration is the most cost-effective instrument available for the deterrence goal. On that comparison, the evidence is unfavorable to continued expansion of imprisonment as the primary policy lever.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes does not have a systematic treatment of the deterrence literature, despite the centrality of deterrence claims to American criminal justice policy. The platform addresses specific crime-related myths—incorrect crime statistics, misleading claims about crime rates in immigrant communities, the "crime and guns" empirical debates—but has not examined the foundational empirical premise underlying mandatory minimum sentencing and three-strikes laws.

This gap has policy consequences. The deterrence-via-severity claim is not a fringe position; it was the animating theory of legislation affecting millions of people's lives. When fact-checkers focus on viral falsehoods rather than the empirical premises of governing policy, they miss a significant category of consequential claim. The distinction between certainty and severity of punishment is well-established in peer-reviewed criminology but has not achieved the cultural visibility that would make it a natural target for popular fact-checking. It lives in academic journals and policy reports rather than in the social media ecosystem where fact-checkers concentrate.

The result is that one of the most empirically contested claims in social science—a claim with direct legislative effects and enormous human stakes—continues to operate without systematic public scrutiny of its evidence base.
