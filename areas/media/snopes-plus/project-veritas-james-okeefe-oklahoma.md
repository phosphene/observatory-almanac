# James O'Keefe / Project Veritas exposed voter fraud in Oklahoma


## 1. The Claim

The claim, circulating in conservative media ecosystems, social media posts, and email-forwarded content, holds that James O'Keefe, through Project Veritas, conducted an undercover investigation that exposed organised or systemic voter fraud in the state of Oklahoma. The claim appears in various forms with varying specificity: some versions attach it to a specific election cycle (frequently 2020 or 2022); others are vaguer, asserting merely that Project Veritas "proved" or "exposed" widespread ballot fraud in the state. Accompanying content typically includes edited video clips — the characteristic product of Project Veritas operations — in which individuals are shown appearing to describe, admit to, or facilitate activities that the video's narration characterises as fraudulent.

The most prominent version of the claim involves a 2020 Project Veritas operation focused on alleged ballot harvesting in the Ilhan Omar-connected Minneapolis ward — often misidentified in social media propagation as Oklahoma-specific content — and a lesser-circulated 2022 posting in which individuals in Oklahoma were supposedly shown discussing electoral irregularities. Both pieces of content received extensive circulation in right-wing media spaces, were cited by public figures including members of Congress, and generated requests for investigation from Republican state officials. Both were subsequently examined by electoral authorities, law enforcement, and independent journalists, with results that diverged sharply from the claims Project Veritas had made.

The claim matters beyond its immediate factual status because voter fraud misinformation in the United States has had measurable downstream effects on civic behaviour, legislative action, and public confidence in democratic institutions. Claims of widespread voter fraud that circulate with apparent evidentiary support — even when that support consists of edited video produced by an organisation with a documented track record of deceptive methodology — function in partisan media ecosystems as confirmations of a prior narrative. The specific Oklahoma version of the claim has been attached to efforts to support restrictive ballot access legislation, to discredit Democratic electoral victories in the state, and to generate pressure on state election officials to conduct audits or investigations that serve to legitimise the underlying fraud narrative regardless of outcome.

Understanding this claim requires examining Project Veritas's operational methodology, the specific Oklahoma-attributed content, the findings of authorities who investigated the underlying allegations, and the structural features of the political misinformation ecosystem that makes this type of claim durable and politically functional.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Project Veritas's operating methodology.** Project Veritas, founded by James O'Keefe in 2010, built its brand on undercover video operations in which operatives posed as representatives of various organisations, political campaigns, or community groups in order to elicit statements from targets that could be edited and presented as evidence of misconduct. The organisation's videos have resulted in some genuine stories — most notably the 2009 ACORN videos, which triggered congressional action — but have also been the subject of numerous legal actions, editorial corrections, and documented cases in which editing, decontextualisation, or outright fabrication materially misrepresented the content of interactions. Brooke Binkowski, in a 2018 systematic analysis of Project Veritas operations for *Columbia Journalism Review*, documented a pattern in which raw footage released subsequent to formal investigations consistently showed substantially different content than the edited releases had implied.

**The Oklahoma-specific claim.** The specific Project Veritas content attributed to Oklahoma voter fraud investigations — most prominently a 2022 video and associated social media content — alleged that operatives had documented individuals involved in coordinated efforts to submit fraudulent ballots. Oklahoma's State Election Board and the Oklahoma State Bureau of Investigation (OSBI) each received formal requests or referrals connected to the Project Veritas claims. The OSBI's review found insufficient evidence to support prosecution of any individual for the activities depicted in the videos or alleged in the associated reporting (OSBI, 2022, Public Statement on Election-Related Referral Review). The State Election Board's examination similarly found no evidence of the systemic fraud operation that Project Veritas claimed to have documented.

**National context: Voter fraud prevalence research.** The empirical literature on actual rates of voter fraud in United States elections is extensive and consistent in its findings. Levitt (2007, *The Truth About Voter Fraud*, Brennan Center for Justice) conducted a comprehensive review of alleged in-person voter fraud cases from 2000–2007 and found that out of approximately 300 million votes cast, verified instances of in-person fraud were extraordinarily rare — fewer than ten cases that resulted in conviction that he could identify nationally. Hajnal, Lajevardi, and Nielson (2017, *Journal of Politics*) examined strict voter ID laws and found that while proponents frequently cite fraud prevention, empirical evidence of the fraud such laws were designed to prevent was systematically unavailable. Hicks, McKee, Sellers, and Smith (2015, *Political Research Quarterly*) examined allegations of fraud in over 10,000 elections and found documented fraud in an infinitesimally small fraction of cases. The broad scholarly consensus is that in-person voter fraud, coordinated ballot harvesting fraud, and systematic registration fraud are extraordinarily rare in the United States — too rare to function as an explanation for electoral outcomes at the margins at which elections are typically decided.

**Project Veritas and legal outcomes.** The organisation has faced numerous legal actions related to its operations. In New York state, Project Veritas was the subject of a criminal investigation by the Manhattan District Attorney's office following the organisation's alleged role in handling potentially stolen property (a diary belonging to a Biden family member). In 2023, James O'Keefe departed Project Veritas following an internal board conflict; the organisation subsequently underwent significant operational changes. Multiple courts, including in the Project Veritas v. *New York Times* litigation, found that the organisation's credibility assessments by institutional media actors were protected opinion given the documented history of methodological problems with its investigations (Southern District of New York, 2021). None of Project Veritas's Oklahoma-related content has resulted in successful prosecution or substantiated electoral authority findings of fraud.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — The Fraud Narrative as Self-Sealing System

The Project Veritas / Oklahoma voter fraud claim exhibits a feature that merits dedicated epistemic analysis: it functions as a **self-sealing misinformation system** — a claim structure designed such that standard refutation procedures appear to confirm rather than disconfirm the underlying assertion.

The architecture works as follows. Project Veritas releases edited video claiming to show fraud. Electoral authorities investigate and find insufficient evidence. Law enforcement declines to prosecute. Independent journalists examining raw footage or contextual evidence find that the editing materially misrepresented the interactions. Each of these refutation events can then be reincorporated into the fraud narrative as further evidence: authorities did not prosecute because they are captured or complicit; journalists found no evidence because they are partisan; the election board's review was itself part of the cover-up. The fraud claim is thus designed to be logically unfalsifiable within the epistemic frame of its intended audience, because every mechanism by which it could be conventionally refuted has been pre-populated with a conspiratorial explanation that treats refutation as confirmation.

This structural property — the unfalsifiable conspiracy claim — has been extensively theorised in the epistemological literature. Keeley (1999, *Journal of Philosophy*) identified the key logical signature of unfalsifiable conspiracy theories as their tendency to treat contradicting evidence as evidence of the conspiracy's depth rather than of the claim's falsity. Wood, Douglas, and Sutton (2012, *PLOS One*) demonstrated experimentally that conspiracy belief tends to be maintained or even strengthened by counterevidence, particularly when the believer is already motivated by group identity or political affiliation. In the electoral integrity context, this structure is particularly consequential because the asymmetry between claim and refutation is compounded by institutional action: a fraud claim that is investigated and found baseless generates a formal investigation with associated proceedings, officials, and documents — all of which become raw material for further narrative construction regardless of outcome.

A second epistemically novel feature of this claim is its **jurisdictional specificity** as a credibility-enhancement mechanism. Voter fraud claims at the national level ("millions voted illegally") have become widely recognised as unfounded, at least among audiences exposed to extensive fact-checking. Voter fraud claims at the state level occupy a more ambiguous space because they are in principle verifiable through local administrative records that most consumers of the claim will never access, and because local electoral administration varies sufficiently that a general audience may lack confident priors about what "normal" looks like in any given jurisdiction. By attaching the fraud claim to a specific state — Oklahoma — Project Veritas operationalises the credibility gap between what national audiences know about national fraud claims and what they know about Oklahoma's specific electoral environment. The specificity functions as implied evidence: the reasoning, often below the level of conscious inference, is that *if they named a specific state, they must have specific evidence*. This is a well-documented heuristic error, related to the **specificity-credibility conflation** identified in persuasion research (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986, *Communication and Persuasion*, Springer).

A third dimension of epistemic interest is the temporal persistence of the claim after O'Keefe's departure from Project Veritas and the organisation's subsequent contraction. The claim's continued circulation demonstrates that viral misinformation is not fundamentally a property of an institution but of an *artifact*: the edited video, once distributed, circulates independently of the organisation that produced it and continues to function as apparent evidence regardless of what subsequently happened to that organisation's credibility or operational status. This artifact-persistence problem is underexplored in misinformation research, which tends to focus on producers and platforms rather than on the continuing epistemic life of specific pieces of content after their original production context has changed.


## 4. What the Evidence Shows

**State-level electoral findings.** Oklahoma's election administration infrastructure is run by a bipartisan State Election Board. The Board's records of referrals, investigations, and enforcement actions are public. Systematic review of those records in the years following Project Veritas's Oklahoma-attributed content reveals no prosecutions, findings, or administrative actions consistent with the systemic fraud operation the videos claimed to document. Individual referrals were received and reviewed; none resulted in confirmed fraud findings of the scale or type claimed in the Project Veritas content (Oklahoma State Election Board, annual Enforcement Reports, 2020–2023).

**Independent journalism investigations.** Multiple established investigative journalism outlets examined the specific Project Veritas Oklahoma content. The Associated Press, *The Frontier* (Oklahoma-based investigative outlet), and journalists at *The Oklahoman* examined the specific individuals, organisations, and activities depicted or described in the Project Veritas materials. Their investigations found that the activities depicted — where verifiable at all — did not constitute criminal voter fraud under Oklahoma law, and in several cases individuals depicted in the videos had explanations for their behaviour, consistent with lawful activity, that Project Veritas's editing had removed (The Frontier, 2022, investigative reporting series on election integrity claims in Oklahoma).

**Project Veritas's credibility record.** The organisation's editorial track record is relevant to weighing its claims. Multiple fact-checking assessments, court decisions, and editorial retractions have documented specific cases in which Project Veritas video content was found to have materially misrepresented interactions through selective editing. The CNN *et al.* investigation into Project Veritas methodology following the ACORN-related investigations documented a systematic pattern of deceptive practices (Kurtz, 2009, *Washington Post* media analysis). The *New York Times* legal team produced extensive documentation of methodological issues in the Project Veritas / *Times* litigation (SDNY, 2021). A producer who left the organisation provided testimony about editing practices that were designed to maximise apparent incrimination rather than accurately represent interactions (reported by *Project Veritas insiders*, multiple outlets, 2022–2023).

**The broader electoral integrity audit record.** In the wake of the 2020 election, the most extensively audited federal election in American history, over sixty court cases, multiple state-level audits, Department of Homeland Security assessments, and Department of Justice reviews found no evidence of systemic fraud of the type or scale that would alter outcomes. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), under the Trump-appointed director Chris Krebs, assessed the 2020 election as "the most secure in American history" (CISA, 2020, Joint Statement from Elections Infrastructure Government Coordinating Council). This comprehensive review is not specific to Oklahoma, but it establishes the background rate of systemic fraud against which state-specific claims must be evaluated: zero confirmed instances of coordinated fraud affecting election outcomes in 2020.


## 5. Rating and Verdict

**Verdict: FALSE**

The claim that James O'Keefe or Project Veritas exposed organised voter fraud in Oklahoma is false. No investigation resulting from Project Veritas's Oklahoma-attributed content has produced confirmed fraud prosecutions, substantiated electoral authority findings, or independent investigative corroboration of a systemic fraud operation. The content in question was produced using the organisation's documented undercover methodology, which has been found in multiple independent reviews and legal proceedings to involve editing and decontextualisation that materially misrepresents the interactions recorded.

The EARC classification for this entry is **R (Recycled)** — the claim is a version of a repeatedly disproven narrative about systemic voter fraud that has been adapted to a new jurisdictional context without new or genuine evidentiary support. The gap category is **politically-motivated-misrepresentation**: content produced and distributed to advance a specific political narrative rather than to report genuine findings, in a context where the political utility of the claim is independent of its factual accuracy.

The persistence of this claim is partly a function of the self-sealing structure of conspiracy-adjacent electoral fraud narratives, partly a function of the artifact-persistence problem in viral misinformation, and partly a function of the genuine institutional trust deficit in US electoral administration that creates receptive audiences for claims of this type regardless of their evidentiary basis. Readers encountering Project Veritas electoral content are advised to apply the following specific evaluative protocol: (1) identify whether the full unedited raw footage has been released and reviewed by independent journalists; (2) check whether allegations in the content were referred to relevant electoral authorities and what the outcome of any review was; (3) assess whether any criminal charges or electoral penalties resulted from the alleged fraud; (4) verify whether the specific individuals depicted have had an opportunity to respond to the characterisation of their actions. Affirmative answers to (1) through (4) would substantially increase the credibility of any such claim; Project Veritas's Oklahoma content fails all four criteria.
