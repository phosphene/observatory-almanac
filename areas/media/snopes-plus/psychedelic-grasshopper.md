## 1. The Claim

On September 14, 2021, a Reddit user posting under the handle "dosangst" submitted a photograph to the subreddit r/NatureIsFuckingLit depicting a strikingly colored grasshopper perched on what appeared to be ordinary suburban vegetation. The title indicated the insect had been spotted in a backyard in southeast Michigan. Within days the post had accumulated more than 77,000 upvotes and hundreds of comments, many of them expressing astonishment that such a vividly pigmented insect could exist in the wild — let alone in the temperate Midwest of the United States.

The photograph showed a grasshopper whose body was decorated in bold, irregular bands of deep blue, crimson red, pale cream, and black. The sheer improbability of the color pattern relative to most people's everyday experience of grasshoppers — typically drab greens and browns — made the image an immediate candidate for skepticism. Some commenters suggested it was a manipulated photograph, a painted specimen, or a tropical species that had been placed in an incongruent setting for internet points. Others countered that many insects are in fact extraordinarily colorful and that the animal was likely genuine.

Snopes investigated the claim and, on September 18, 2021, published a fact-check authored by Madison Dapcevich. The resulting verdict was "Mixture" — a rating the outlet reserves for claims that contain elements of both truth and falsehood. This article unpacks the reasoning behind that rating, extends the analysis into areas the original fact-check did not explore, and examines what the episode reveals about the epistemology of viral wildlife photography in the age of citizen science.

## 2. What's Actually True (Verdict: Mixture)

The grasshopper pictured in the Reddit post is almost certainly a genuine, unmanipulated photograph of a real animal. The species is *Dactylotum bicolor*, commonly known as the rainbow grasshopper, the painted grasshopper, or the barber pole grasshopper. Chris Grinter, entomology collection manager at the California Academy of Sciences, confirmed the identification in an email to Snopes, noting that the distinctive mosaic of blue, red, cream, and black markings is characteristic of the species and would be exceptionally difficult to convincingly fabricate through image editing.

However, the claim that the grasshopper was found "in a backyard in S.E. Michigan" is where the mixture verdict arises. According to the SCAN-Bugs database — a major digitized repository of arthropod specimen records across North American natural history collections — there are no known records of *D. bicolor* east of the Mississippi River. The species' documented range is confined to the shortgrass prairies, desert grasslands, and arid scrublands of the western United States, portions of southern Canada, and northern Mexico. Southeast Michigan, situated firmly in the Great Lakes region east of the Mississippi, lies hundreds of miles outside any credible range boundary for the species.

This geographic discrepancy is not merely a matter of imprecise range maps. *Dactylotum bicolor* belongs to a small number of grasshopper species that are functionally flightless throughout their entire life cycle. While many grasshopper species develop full hindwings at maturity and are capable of sustained flight — some, like plague locusts, notoriously so — *D. bicolor* never develops functional flight wings (Otte, 1981, *North American Grasshoppers*, Harvard University Press). This biological limitation means the species cannot disperse across large geographic barriers by its own locomotion. A *D. bicolor* specimen cannot simply fly from the western plains to Michigan; natural range expansion over such a distance is, for all practical purposes, impossible.

As Grinter explained to Snopes, the most likely explanations are that someone collected the grasshopper elsewhere and photographed it in Michigan, that the Reddit user obtained the photograph from another source and misattributed the location, or — less likely but not impossible — that a human introduction at some point brought a small number of individuals into the region. The Snopes verdict captures this epistemic state precisely: the species is real, the photograph is almost certainly authentic, but the location claim does not withstand scrutiny against available entomological records and the species' known biology.

## 3. Epistemic Novelty: The Biogeography of Viral Wildlife Photography

The Snopes fact-check correctly identified the geographic incongruity at the heart of the claim, but it did not explore the broader phenomenon that the psychedelic grasshopper case exemplifies: the systematic creation of biogeographic misinformation through viral wildlife photography on social media platforms. This is not a marginal concern. It is an emerging epistemological problem with real consequences for biodiversity science, conservation planning, and the integrity of citizen science databases.

The core mechanism operates as follows. When a user photographs or encounters a striking organism, the incentive structure of platforms like Reddit, Instagram, and Twitter rewards visual spectacle and emotional engagement rather than taxonomic precision or geographic accuracy. Posts are optimized for virality — for likes, upvotes, shares, and comment-section astonishment. In this context, a photograph's claimed provenance is essentially an unverified metadata field. There is no requirement that a poster verify their geographic claim, no platform-level validation of coordinates, and typically no exif data accessible to viewers that would confirm or contradict the stated location. The result is a vast, growing corpus of wildlife imagery whose geographic attributions range from scrupulously accurate to entirely fabricated, with no reliable way for downstream consumers of the information to distinguish between them.

This would be a purely sociological curiosity if not for the fact that citizen science platforms increasingly ingest social media observations as data. iNaturalist, one of the largest biodiversity observation platforms in the world with over 100 million observations as of 2023, has sophisticated community-based verification processes, including geographic range checks and expert identification (Seltzer et al., 2021, *BioScience*). But many other aggregators do not. eBird, for instance, has rigorous rare-bird filters, yet even its systems can be fooled by plausible-sounding reports (Sullivan et al., 2014, *Biological Conservation*). The Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF), which aggregates hundreds of millions of occurrence records from thousands of sources worldwide, relies on downstream data-quality flags that may or may not catch a misattributed observation, especially when the claimed location falls within a biogeographically plausible region (Zizka et al., 2019, *Methods in Ecology and Evolution*).

The psychedelic grasshopper case illustrates a particularly insidious variant of this problem: the photograph is genuine, the species identification is correct, and the location claim is not wildly implausible to a non-specialist — Michigan is, after all, in North America, where *D. bicolor* does occur. It is only when one consults specimen databases and understands the species' flightlessness that the discrepancy becomes apparent. This kind of "plausible misattribution" is far harder to detect algorithmically than a photograph of a clearly tropical organism claimed to have been found in the Arctic. If such an observation were to enter a formal biodiversity database without adequate vetting, it could distort range maps, confound species distribution models, and potentially mislead conservation assessments. Distribution modeling studies that rely on occurrence data from aggregated databases are particularly vulnerable: a single out-of-range point can shift a model's predicted range envelope by hundreds of kilometers, especially for species with few total records (Boria et al., 2014, *Ecography*).

Furthermore, the virality of the grasshopper post — 77,000 upvotes and widespread media coverage — means that the Michigan attribution was seen by millions of people, a significant fraction of whom may now believe that rainbow grasshoppers can be found in their Michigan backyards. This kind of folk-biogeographic misinformation is resistant to correction because it is anchored to a genuine and memorable image. Psychological research on the "illusory truth effect" demonstrates that repeated exposure to a claim increases its perceived truthfulness regardless of whether it has been corrected (Fazio et al., 2015, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*). A single Snopes article, however well-researched, cannot fully counteract the reach and staying power of a viral Reddit post.

The implications extend beyond individual cases. As social media becomes an increasingly important source of biodiversity observations — particularly for charismatic, visually striking species that generate engagement — the scientific community faces a growing need for tools and norms that can validate geographic claims attached to wildlife photographs. Exif-based geolocation verification, reverse image search to detect re-posted photographs with altered location claims, and automated cross-referencing against known range databases are all technically feasible interventions. The question is whether platforms and citizen science aggregators will implement them at scale before the signal-to-noise ratio in occurrence data degrades beyond easy remediation.

## 4. Dactylotum bicolor: Biology, Range, and the Science of Aposematism

*Dactylotum bicolor* (Acrididae: Gomphocerinae) is a species of short-horned grasshopper endemic to North America. Adults reach a body length of approximately 20 to 35 millimeters (roughly up to 1.5 inches), making them small to medium-sized by grasshopper standards. The species was first described by Thomas in 1872, and its taxonomic placement has been relatively stable since, though the broader phylogenetics of the Gomphocerinae have undergone revisions as molecular data have become available (Song et al., 2015, *Cladistics*).

The most immediately striking feature of *D. bicolor* is its coloration. The body is covered in an intricate, irregularly patterned mosaic of deep turquoise-blue, bright red or orange, cream or white, and black. This pigmentation is not structural (i.e., not produced by the microscopic surface architectures that generate iridescence in many beetles or butterflies) but rather is derived from integumental pigments deposited in the cuticle and epidermis. The result is a matte, paint-like appearance that is remarkably vivid in life and that, as the Reddit post demonstrated, reads as almost supernaturally colorful to observers unfamiliar with the species.

This coloration serves a well-documented biological function: aposematism. Aposematic coloration is the use of bright, conspicuous visual signals to advertise an organism's unprofitability to potential predators, typically because the organism is toxic, venomous, or otherwise noxious (Ruxton et al., 2004, *Avoiding Attack: The Evolutionary Ecology of Crypsis, Warning Signals, and Mimicry*, Oxford University Press). In the case of *D. bicolor*, the grasshopper is believed to sequester or synthesize chemical compounds that render it distasteful to birds and other vertebrate predators. When disturbed, the grasshopper may also exude a frothy defensive secretion from its thoracic region, a behavior common among aposematic acridids (Whitman et al., 1991, *Journal of Chemical Ecology*). The bold coloration thus functions as a learned or innate signal to predators: "I taste terrible; don't bother."

The evolutionary ecology of aposematism in grasshoppers is an active area of research. Among the Acrididae, conspicuous coloration is relatively uncommon — most grasshoppers rely on crypsis (camouflage) to avoid detection. The transition from crypsis to aposematism represents a fundamental shift in anti-predator strategy and is thought to require specific ecological conditions, including relatively high population densities (to ensure predators encounter the warningly colored prey often enough to learn the association) and reliable access to the plants from which defensive chemicals are derived (Sword, 2002, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*). *D. bicolor*'s restriction to open grassland habitats with specific plant communities is consistent with this ecological framework.

The geographic range of *D. bicolor* extends across the western Great Plains of the United States, from Montana and the Dakotas southward through Colorado, Kansas, Oklahoma, New Mexico, and into western Texas. Peripheral records exist in southern Alberta and Saskatchewan in Canada and in the arid grasslands of northern Mexico, particularly Chihuahua and Sonora. The species is most abundant in shortgrass prairie ecosystems, where it feeds on a variety of grasses and forbs. It also occurs in desert grasslands, alkaline flats, and the edges of cultivated alfalfa fields, where it can occasionally reach pest densities (Pfadt, 2002, *Field Guide to Common Western Grasshoppers*, Wyoming Agricultural Experiment Station). Critically, *D. bicolor* is not known from any locality east of the Mississippi River, and most records cluster west of the 100th meridian — the traditional boundary between the humid eastern United States and the semi-arid western plains.

The flightlessness of *D. bicolor* is a key trait for understanding both its restricted range and the implausibility of the Michigan sighting. The species is brachypterous throughout its life cycle, meaning its hindwings are reduced and non-functional for flight even in the adult stage. Brachyptery in grasshoppers is associated with stable habitats, low dispersal requirements, and, consequently, reduced gene flow between populations (Hochkirch and Gröning, 2008, *Journal of Insect Conservation*). For *D. bicolor*, this means that any expansion of its range must occur through walking dispersal — a glacially slow process measured in meters per generation, not the hundreds of kilometers that would be required to colonize Michigan from the nearest known populations in the western plains.

## 5. Citizen Science, Photo Attribution, and the Limits of Social Media Naturalism

The psychedelic grasshopper episode sits at the intersection of two powerful contemporary trends: the democratization of natural history observation through smartphones and social media, and the growing reliance of biodiversity science on citizen-contributed data. Both trends are broadly positive — more people observing and sharing nature is a net good for conservation awareness, and citizen science platforms have generated datasets of unprecedented spatial and temporal coverage (Dickinson et al., 2010, *Annual Review of Ecology, Evolution, and Systematics*). But the grasshopper case highlights a structural weakness that runs through the entire enterprise: the problem of unverified provenance.

In traditional natural history, a specimen record consists of a physical specimen deposited in a museum collection, accompanied by a label bearing the collector's name, the date, the precise locality, and often ecological notes. The physical specimen can be re-examined by future researchers, its identification can be verified or corrected, and its locality data — while occasionally erroneous — are anchored to a specific individual with a professional or semi-professional reputation to maintain. This system is imperfect, but its error modes are well understood and can be audited.

Social media wildlife photography represents a radical departure from this model. There is no physical specimen. The photograph itself may be the only evidence that an observation occurred, and its metadata — including the claimed location — is trivially easy to fabricate, misremember, or misattribute. The Reddit user "dosangst" may have genuinely believed the photograph was taken in southeast Michigan; they may have been repeating a claim they heard from someone else; they may have found the image online and attached a false attribution for engagement; or they may have physically transported a grasshopper from its native range and photographed it in their Michigan backyard. We cannot distinguish among these scenarios from the photograph alone.

This ambiguity is not unique to the grasshopper case. It is inherent to any wildlife photograph shared on social media without independent corroboration of its provenance. Platforms like iNaturalist address this partly through geographic range flags: if a user reports a species far outside its known range, the observation is flagged for community review and typically not validated as "research grade" unless compelling supporting evidence is provided. This is a sound approach, but it relies on the species' range being well-known and encoded in the platform's database — a condition that holds for conspicuous vertebrates and well-studied insect groups, but that fails for the vast majority of invertebrate diversity, which is poorly mapped at the resolution needed for meaningful range checks (Cardoso et al., 2011, *Biological Conservation*).

The grasshopper case also illustrates the tension between public engagement and scientific rigor in citizen science. The Reddit post's virality was driven entirely by the animal's visual spectacle — the psychedelic coloration that made it look "unreal." This is precisely the kind of content that citizen science platforms want to harness, because it motivates participation and public interest in biodiversity. But the same features that make an observation go viral — visual strikingness, emotional impact, apparent improbability — are also the features most likely to be associated with misattribution, manipulation, or deliberate hoaxing. An ordinary brown grasshopper spotted in a Michigan backyard would never accumulate 77,000 upvotes; it also would never generate a problematic out-of-range record.

The challenge for the biodiversity informatics community is to develop protocols that can harness the observational power and public enthusiasm represented by posts like the psychedelic grasshopper, while filtering out the geographic noise that accompanies unverified social media data. Emerging tools for automated image geolocation, where deep learning models estimate a photograph's location from visual cues in the background scene, offer one potential avenue (Weyand et al., 2016, *European Conference on Computer Vision*). Cross-referencing claimed locations against remote sensing data on habitat type — a photograph purportedly from a Michigan backyard should show temperate deciduous vegetation, not shortgrass prairie — offers another. But these are technical solutions to what is ultimately an institutional and normative problem: social media platforms do not currently treat geographic accuracy as a meaningful quality metric, and until they do, every viral wildlife photograph carries an unquantified location uncertainty that propagates silently into any database that ingests it.

## 6. Verdict and Key Takeaways

The Snopes verdict of "Mixture" is well-supported by the available evidence. The photograph is almost certainly genuine and correctly depicts *Dactylotum bicolor*, the rainbow grasshopper. The species' vivid coloration is a natural product of aposematic pigmentation, not digital manipulation. However, the claim that the photograph was taken in a backyard in southeast Michigan is not consistent with the species' known range, its flightless biology, or any records in major entomological databases.

The key takeaways from this case are:

- **The species is real.** *Dactylotum bicolor* is a well-documented, widely distributed grasshopper native to the western Great Plains of North America. Its dramatic coloration is a genuine biological adaptation — aposematic warning coloration that deters predators.

- **The photograph is almost certainly authentic.** Expert identification by Chris Grinter of the California Academy of Sciences confirmed the species, and the coloration pattern is consistent with known specimens and field observations of *D. bicolor*.

- **The location claim is unsupported.** No records of *D. bicolor* exist east of the Mississippi River in the SCAN-Bugs database. The species' functional flightlessness precludes natural dispersal to southeast Michigan. The grasshopper was most likely collected or photographed elsewhere, or the photograph was obtained from another source with a false location attribution.

- **Viral wildlife photography creates systematic biogeographic misinformation.** The incentive structures of social media platforms reward visual spectacle over geographic accuracy, and the resulting corpus of unverified location claims poses a real threat to citizen science databases and species distribution models.

- **Citizen science platforms need better provenance tools.** Automated range checks, exif geolocation verification, reverse image search, and habitat cross-referencing can all help filter bogus location claims, but widespread implementation remains incomplete.

- **Aposematism works — both biologically and virally.** The same conspicuous coloration that evolved to deter predators is precisely what makes *D. bicolor* go viral on social media. The species' evolutionary strategy for attracting attention in one domain (predator warning) has found an unlikely second life in another (internet engagement), with unintended epistemic consequences.
