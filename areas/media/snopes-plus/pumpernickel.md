## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

A folk etymology widely circulated about the dark, dense German sourdough bread known as pumpernickel claims that the word originated when Napoleon Bonaparte—or, in various versions, Napoleon's groom, or an unspecified Frenchman—was offered the bread while on military campaign in German-speaking territory and, finding it unfit for human consumption, declared it suitable only for his horse Nicol. The French phrase "C'est du pain pour Nicol" ("It is bread for Nicol") or "C'est bon pour Nicol" ("It is good for Nicol"), in this account, was adopted by German locals as the name for the bread, eventually corrupting into "pumpernickel."

Snopes investigator David Mikkelson rated this claim **False**. The folk etymology fails on straightforward chronological and linguistic grounds: the word "pumpernickel" was already part of the German language—as a derogatory term roughly equivalent to "boor" or "blockhead"—more than a century before Napoleon was born in 1769. The word also appeared in English as a bread name before Napoleon's birth. The Napoleon story is therefore impossible as an explanation for the word's origins.

A second proposed etymology—more linguistically plausible though still colorful—derives the word from the New High German "pumpern" (a word for flatulence, onomatopoeically descriptive) and "Nickel" (a form of Nicholas, traditionally a name for a goblin or the devil, as in "Old Nick"). Under this account, "pumpernickel" literally means "devil's fart," a reference to the bread's notoriously indigestible qualities and the gastrointestinal consequences of consuming it. Mikkelson (2001) treats this second etymology as plausible, though he also notes a minority view that "pumpern" refers to the sound of thumping on a loaf—a view he characterizes as "extremely unlikely."

This entry examines the documentary refutation of the Napoleon etymology, the linguistic evidence for the pumpern-plus-Nickel etymology, the broader cultural and biological context of pumpernickel's indigestibility and its known gastrointestinal effects, the folk etymology tradition of explaining funny-sounding food words through heroic anecdote, and the significance of the pumpern-Nickel derivation within the German semantic tradition of demonic and scatological naming.

## §2 The Napoleon Etymology and Its Refutation

The Napoleon etymology's appeal is obvious: it is simple, memorable, and involves a famous historical figure who was actually present in German-speaking territories. Napoleon conducted multiple campaigns in areas now comprising Germany, and the image of the imperious French emperor contemptuously feeding dark bread to his horse while dismissing it as inferior to French cuisine fits a recognizable narrative template—foreign arrogance encountering local food customs, with the locals having the last laugh by preserving the contemptuous phrase as the food's permanent name.

The problem is chronological. Mikkelson (2001), citing Rawson (1994, *Devious Derivations*, Crown Trade Paperbacks, New York, p. 165–166) and West (2000, *The Secret Lives of Words*, Harcourt Inc., New York, p. 221), notes that "pumpernickel" was part of the German language as a pejorative expression well before Napoleon's birth. The *Compact Oxford English Dictionary* (third ed., Clarendon Press, 1993) confirms that the word appeared in English as a type of bread before Napoleon was born. The earliest recorded English use of "pumpernickel" as a bread name substantially predates any possible Napoleonic origin.

This chronological argument is decisive. For a word's etymology to be valid, the origin story must predate or be contemporaneous with the word's first documented appearance. If "pumpernickel" was already established in German (and subsequently borrowed into English) before Napoleon existed, then no act on Napoleon's part could have created the word. The folk etymology has the causal arrow backwards in time.

It is possible—as Mikkelson (2001) allows—that one or more monarchs may have actually made this pun. The general structure of "sir loin" jokes (a king knighting a joint of beef) and "pain pour Nicol" jokes may have been performed historically by witty aristocrats as genuine wordplay. But performing a pun on an existing word is not the same as coining the word. Napoleon or his entourage could have made an amusing jest about "pain pour Nicol" while in Germany without that jest being the origin of the word—and the documentary evidence indicates the word was already established before any such jest could have occurred.

The Napoleon etymology belongs to the broad category of folk etymologies that Rawson (1994) calls "devious derivations"—explanations for word origins that are vivid and memorable but contradict historical linguistic evidence. Such folk etymologies propagate because they satisfy a psychological need for stories behind words, particularly exotic-sounding or funny-sounding ones. A word like "pumpernickel"—with its unusual phonology, its implicit comedy, its foreignness—invites an explanatory story. The Napoleon story satisfies this need even while failing historical scrutiny.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Pumpern-Nickel Etymology, Scatological Naming Traditions, and the Digestive Chemistry of Rye Bread

The more linguistically plausible etymology—"pumpernickel" as "devil's fart"—is itself a case study in the rich tradition of scatological and demonic naming in German linguistic culture, and it can be evaluated against what is known about dark rye bread's actual gastrointestinal effects.

The component "Nickel" derives from a hypocoristic (pet-name) form of the German name Nikolaus. In German folklore and linguistic tradition, the name Nickel—like the English "Old Nick"—was associated with mischievous spirits, goblins, and the devil. The water spirit the Nixie (or Nix), the mine goblin Bergnickel, and various domestic mischief spirits all drew on this naming tradition, in which the familiar name Nicholas was used as a euphemism for supernatural malevolence—similar to the English "Old Harry" or "Old Nick" as ways of referring to Satan without using the name directly (Grimm, Jacob, 1835/1966, *Teutonic Mythology*, Vol. 1, tr. James Steven Stallybrass, Dover Publications, New York). The element "nickel" in pumpernickel is thus linguistically equivalent to calling the bread "devil's" something—a common folk idiom for something with disagreeable or dangerous properties.

The element "pumpern" is a New High German derivation from a root denoting a heavy blow, thump, or—crucially—flatulence. German dictionaries confirm that this form was used onomatopoeically for the sound of breaking wind, similarly to how English speakers might use "trump" or other sound-derived terms for the same phenomenon. Mikkelson (2001) treats this as the intended meaning in the compound, making "pumpernickel" a compound implying that consuming the bread produces demonic-level intestinal gas.

This etymology is not only linguistically coherent but has a plausible biological basis. Pumpernickel is made primarily from whole-grain rye flour, specifically through a long slow-bake process (often 16–24 hours) at relatively low temperatures (below the temperature that rapidly drives water out of the bread). Rye contains significantly higher concentrations of fermentable carbohydrates—including fructooligosaccharides, arabinoxylans, and other dietary fibers—than wheat flour. These fermentable carbohydrates are not digested in the small intestine but are instead fermented by colonic microbiota, producing substantial quantities of short-chain fatty acids, carbon dioxide, hydrogen, and methane gas (Nyman, M., Björck, I., & Asp, N.-G., 1986, "Fermentable Plant Fiber and in-vitro Ileal Digestibility: Short-Chain Fatty Acids and Gases Produced during Fermentation," *Journal of the Science of Food and Agriculture*, Vol. 37, No. 8, pp. 725–737).

Modern nutritional science confirms that pumpernickel has a significantly lower glycemic index than wheat bread—typically around 40–50 on the standard 100-point scale, compared to 70–90 for white wheat bread—precisely because its complex carbohydrates resist rapid digestion (Jenkins, D. J. A., Wolever, T. M. S., Taylor, R. H., et al., 1981, "Glycemic Index of Foods: A Physiological Basis for Carbohydrate Exchange," *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, Vol. 34, No. 3, pp. 362–366). The same structural features that make pumpernickel nutritionally beneficial—its resistant starches and complex dietary fiber—also made it, for populations unaccustomed to high-fiber diets, experientially gassy and uncomfortable. Pre-modern German-speakers eating pumpernickel regularly would have had substantially different gut flora and fiber tolerance than occasional French consumers, which may partly explain how a food eaten daily without incident in Westphalia could be experienced as indigestibly flatulent by visitors.

The devil's fart etymology thus provides a genuine insight: the bread was named for its experiential effects on naive consumers, and those effects have a documented food-science explanation. This represents a rare case where a scatological folk etymology aligns with observable empirical reality.

## §4 The Folk Etymology Tradition and Funny-Sounding Food Words

The pumpernickel case is far from unique in attracting folk etymology about food words. A substantial proportion of food origin stories attach colorful anecdotes to culinary terms whose actual etymology is prosaic or uncertain, and these invented stories tend to share structural features with the Napoleon/Nicol narrative.

Folk etymologies for food words typically take one of several forms: (1) attribution to a named historical figure (king, emperor, chef) who coined the term through a dramatic act or declaration; (2) transformation of a foreign-language phrase through mishearing or back-formation; (3) explanation of an animal or natural phenomenon as the original referent. Category (1) is the most prestigious form and is accordingly the most common vehicle for false etymologies about food. "Beef Wellington" is attributed to the Duke of Wellington; "Caesar salad" is correctly attributed to Caesar Cardini but is frequently embellished with invented details about its creation; "club sandwich" has accumulated multiple implausible origin stories (Ayto, John, 1993, *A Gourmet's Guide: Food and Drink from A to Z*, Oxford University Press, pp. passim).

The mechanism generating these false etymologies is fundamentally the same as that identified by Rawson (1994): funny-sounding or foreign-origin words invite explanatory narratives, and the human mind prefers a vivid story to an uncertain etymology or a dry linguistic derivation. "It comes from Middle French via Latin" is less satisfying than "Napoleon called it this while feeding it to his horse." The Napoleon story circulates because it is better entertainment; the pumpern-Nickel story, while more accurate, circulates less widely because "devil's fart" is funny but lacks the social cachet of a royal origin.

Samuel Johnson's etymological note in his 1755 *Dictionary of the English Language*—defining "oats" as "A grain, which in England is generally given to horses, but in Scotland appears to support the people"—provides a useful comparative case. Johnson's definition is itself nationalistic snobbery encoded as lexicographic wit, structurally identical to the Napoleon/Nicol story: a culturally superior person (or nation) derisively categorizes a food as horse fodder. The parallel suggests that the template for the Napoleon etymology was culturally available and recognizable to English speakers well before the 2001 Snopes article.

## §5 The Word's Pre-Napoleonic History and Semantic Range

The evidence for pumpernickel as a German word predating Napoleon is not simply a matter of the word's earlier appearance in bread names. Mikkelson (2001) notes that the word was part of German as a general pejorative—roughly equivalent to the modern English "jerk" or "boor"—before it was specifically applied to the bread. This suggests a semantic development in which the bread was named after the pejorative (because eating it was associated with lowly status or because its effects matched the implied slur), rather than the pejorative developing from the bread name.

This semantic trajectory—a general term of reproach becoming a specific food name—is documented in German lexicography by the 17th century at the latest. The *Compact Oxford English Dictionary* (1993) entry confirms the word's presence in English (as a loanword from German) before Napoleonic times, and the German sources cited by Rawson (1994) and West (2000) establish its German pre-history. The Westphalian origin of pumpernickel bread—it is traditionally associated with the Westphalia region of northwestern Germany—is consistent with a regional product whose name was established in regional German speech before it entered wider circulation.

The distinction between the two proposed etymologies—Napoleon's horse vs. devil's fart—maps onto the broader philological distinction between folk etymology and scholarly etymology. Folk etymologies favor narrative plausibility (Napoleon was in Germany; he might have said this) over documentary evidence. Scholarly etymology requires dated textual attestation and formal linguistic analysis of component derivations. On all scholarly criteria, the pumpern-Nickel account is superior: it provides dated attestation for both components, proposes a compositional meaning coherent with the word's semantic range as a pejorative, and does not depend on the existence of events (Napoleon in Germany with a horse named Nicol) for which no primary documentary evidence exists.

## §6 Broader Significance: Etymology, Epistemic Humility, and the Pleasure of Word Stories

The pumpernickel etymology case illustrates a persistent tension in popular engagement with linguistic history: the desire for narrative explanation versus the discipline of evidence. Etymology is one of the few academic fields in which non-specialists feel comfortable devising their own explanations for professional puzzles, partly because words are familiar objects and partly because the relevant evidence (historical texts, comparative linguistics) is not immediately accessible to most people.

The consequence of this democratized etymology is an abundance of circulating word-origin stories that are engaging but false—for pumpernickel, sirloin, the word "news" (allegedly an acronym for North, East, West, South), "golf" (allegedly an acronym for "Gentlemen Only Ladies Forbidden"), and dozens of others. The internet has both amplified the spread of false etymologies and created new resources for correcting them, including Snopes and the Online Etymology Dictionary (Douglas Harper, ed., etymonline.com).

The broader epistemic significance is that the popularity of etymological folk tales reveals something genuine about how people relate to language: words feel as if they should have stories behind them, and the instinct to provide those stories—even absent evidence—is deeply human. Linguist David Crystal (2000, *Language Death*, Cambridge University Press) has observed that speakers develop strong emotional attachments to their language's history, including romanticized versions of word origins. The pumpernickel Napoleon story exists not because anyone deliberately manufactured a false etymology but because someone, somewhere, saw the structural similarity between "pain pour Nicol" and "pumpernickel" and experienced the satisfying click of apparent pattern-matching. That the pattern was spurious does not diminish the cognitive experience that generated the story.

The EARC gap designation of **A** (Archival) reflects the need for a more complete historical tracery of the word's documented appearances across German and English texts from the 15th through 18th centuries. While Rawson (1994), West (2000), and the Oxford English Dictionary provide orienting references, a full scholarly etymology would require engagement with Early New High German lexicographical sources and German regional dialect records that are not readily synthesized from the available popular scholarship cited by Mikkelson.
