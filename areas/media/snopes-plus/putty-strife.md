## §1 — Claim & Verdict Overview

The claim under review — that Chick-fil-A chicken sandwiches contain dimethylpolysiloxane, which is Silly Putty — was published by Snopes on 6 March 2015 and carries the rating **MIXTURE**. The piece was authored by **David Mikkelson**. The mixture rating captures a structurally bifurcated claim: the first half (Chick-fil-A uses dimethylpolysiloxane) is true; the second half (dimethylpolysiloxane is, essentially, Silly Putty) is false in the way it is almost universally understood when stated — as an identity claim.

This is a classic example of what food communication scholars call a "shared-ingredient conflation" — a rhetorical device in which two products that share a single common chemical compound are framed as equivalent by identifying them through that shared compound. The logical structure is: (1) Product A contains chemical X; (2) Product B also contains chemical X; (3) therefore Product A contains Product B. The persuasive force of this structure depends on the audience's likely ignorance of how common the chemical is across diverse contexts, how small its concentration typically is in food applications, and what the relevant safety authority has determined about its safety profile.

In this case: dimethylpolysiloxane (PDMS) appears in Chick-fil-A's frying oil formulation as an anti-foaming agent. Silly Putty contains dimethylpolysiloxane as approximately four percent of its composition (the remainder being boric acid, silica, and other polymers). A Snopes analytical note made in the specific article holds that characterizing a substance as "Silly Putty" when it constitutes only four percent of that material, with the other 96 percent being chemically distinct compounds, is "certainly overstated and inaccurate." The identity claim is false even by strict compositional standards.

The case also touches on broader questions about "chemical literacy" — or rather illiteracy — in consumer food communication. The claim's persuasive core, as Mikkelson diagnosed it, is the intimidation factor of an unpronounceable chemical name: dimethylpolysiloxane sounds industrial, synthetic, and alien even to many educated adults. This semiotic quality is weaponized in ingredient-scare content across the food industry.

## §2 — What Is Dimethylpolysiloxane?

Dimethylpolysiloxane (polydimethylsiloxane, or PDMS) is a silicon-based polymer belonging to the silicone family. Its chemical structure consists of a repeating backbone of alternating silicon and oxygen atoms with methyl groups attached to each silicon atom. This backbone gives PDMS its characteristic thermal stability, low surface tension, and non-reactivity with most organic compounds — properties that make it useful in an enormous range of industrial, medical, and consumer contexts.

In food applications, PDMS is used at very low concentrations — typically no more than 10 parts per million — as an anti-foaming agent in vegetable oils used for frying. Heated cooking oils naturally foam as water from food surfaces vaporizes and becomes entrained in the oil; excessive foaming accelerates oil oxidation, causes spattering, and can lead to overflow hazards. Anti-foaming agents work by reducing oil surface tension, destabilizing foam bubbles before they can persist. PDMS is effective at trace concentrations and is chemically inert under frying temperatures.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration has authorized PDMS as a food additive under 21 C.F.R. § 173.340, permitting its use specifically as an anti-foaming agent in food-grade oils at concentrations not to exceed 10 parts per million. This level — one ten-millionth of the total mass — is several orders of magnitude below any dose associated with physiological effects. The toxicology of PDMS has been reviewed by the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA), which established an Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) of 0–1.5 mg/kg body weight, based on studies demonstrating no adverse effects at concentrations far exceeding food-use levels (JECFA, 1970). For context: a 70 kg adult would need to consume approximately 105 mg of pure PDMS per day before reaching the ADI threshold; at 10 ppm in frying oil, and assuming that frying oil is consumed at a rate far below total food mass, actual PDMS intake from fast food consumption is several orders of magnitude below the ADI.

The FDA approval and WHO review are cited in Mikkelson's Snopes article. McDonald's, Taco Bell, Whataburger, Coke (in certain fountain drink applications), and KFC are all identified as additional users of the compound in their menu items. The ubiquity of PDMS in fast food is itself an important corrective to the impression created by "Chick-fil-A specifically uses this industrial chemical": the ingredient is widespread, openly disclosed in ingredient lists, and has been reviewed by multiple independent regulatory bodies with no findings of concern at food-use concentrations.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: Chemical Name Intimidation as a Rhetorical Strategy in Food Communication

The "chemical name intimidation" strategy — using an unfamiliar, polysyllabic, Latinate chemical name to generate anxiety about a food ingredient regardless of its actual safety profile — is one of the most reliable rhetorical devices in viral food-fear content. The Chick-fil-A/PDMS case is a particularly clean example because the compound in question is unambiguously safe at food-use concentrations by the consensus of every regulatory body that has evaluated it. The scare depends entirely on the name's aesthetic qualities (length, technical character, phonological foreignness) and the shared-ingredient conflation with Silly Putty.

The epistemology of this rhetorical strategy has received some empirical study. Siegrist and Hartmann (2020, *Comprehensive Reviews in Food Science and Food Safety*) reviewed the literature on consumer responses to food ingredient names and found that consumers systematically rate "natural" ingredient names as safer and more acceptable than chemically precise names for identical compounds — a finding replicated across multiple studies and cultures. Their review documented that providing consumers with the chemical name of a compound familiar from everyday contexts (e.g., "dihydrogen monoxide" for water) produces measurable anxiety about that substance when the name is unfamiliar, regardless of the substance's actual risk profile. This cognitive pattern is known as the "natural/artificial" heuristic and is reinforced by the observation that unfamiliar names are more cognitively difficult to process (Alter, 2013, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* context; processing fluency literature).

In the PDMS/Silly Putty case, the shared-ingredient conflation adds a second rhetorical layer: the "evidence" offered for the claim is not a hazard study but a category association. The audience is not told "dimethylpolysiloxane causes X harm at Y concentration"; they are told "dimethylpolysiloxane is in Silly Putty," and the harm inference is left unstated, generated by the consumer's prior association of industrial toys with inedibility. This indirect structure is rhetorically powerful because it sidesteps the falsifiability of explicit harm claims while still generating a harm inference. The rhetor can always retreat to "I never said it was harmful — I just said it's in Silly Putty" if challenged.

No published study has, to this author's knowledge, specifically examined the "shared-ingredient conflation" pattern as a distinct rhetorical category within food misinformation. Dietvorst and Logg (2019, *Journal of Marketing Research*) and related work on algorithm aversion provides an adjacent framework — consumers systematically reject technical information presented in abstracted/algorithmic form even when they would accept identical information in naturalistic form — but does not address the specific shared-ingredient logic. A study that systematically catalogued instances of this rhetorical structure in viral food-fear content and tested whether providing the shared-compound's actual safety data (rather than the industry presence of the compound) corrects consumer concern would contribute directly to public health communication strategy.

The Chick-fil-A case is notable for occurring in 2015, well before the era of social media video-based food content, which has subsequently amplified similar claims dramatically. TikTok food content regularly features the "do you know what's actually in X" structure that deploys exactly the PDMS playbook: unfamiliar name, shared presence with a non-food industrial product, no hazard evidence required, correction-resistant because no explicit harm claim was made.

## §4 — The "Banned in the UK" Counter-Claim

Some versions of the Chick-fil-A/PDMS rumor included the additional claim that dimethylpolysiloxane is "banned in the United Kingdom for safety reasons." Mikkelson explicitly addresses this claim and describes it as false. The Food Standards Agency (FSA), the UK regulatory body with jurisdiction over food additive authorizations, has not banned PDMS for use in food; UK regulations permit its use as a processing aid at concentrations consistent with EU and global norms. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) has evaluated PDMS and established an ADI aligned with the JECFA evaluation. There is no basis for the "banned in UK" claim in any regulatory document.

The "banned in [other country] for safety reasons" addendum is itself a recognizable rhetorical flourish in food misinformation, functioning to establish implied external validation for a health concern without providing a specific source. Since the US often has different regulatory frameworks from the EU, and since EU regulations are sometimes more precautionary for categories of food additives and agricultural chemicals, the claim has a surface plausibility that requires regulatory reading to debunk rather than mere common sense. Chapman and collaborators (2019, *Risk Analysis*) identified "regulatory asymmetry arguments" — the claim that a foreign jurisdiction's more restrictive posture validates a domestic safety concern — as a common pattern in anti-GMO and food additive anxiety content, noting that such arguments often mischaracterize the foreign regulatory landscape.

## §5 — The Broader Context: "Eat This, Not That" and Ingredient Alarm Content

The immediate origin of the 2015 viral iteration of this claim was a tweet from @EatThisNotThat on February 2, 2015, which stated: "Chick-fil-A's Chicken Sandwich contains 'dimethylpolysiloxane,' an antifoaming agent also found in shampoo and Silly Putty!" Mikkelson traces an earlier 2013 version targeting multiple large food companies under the headline "You Won't Believe Where Silly Putty Is Hiding in Your Food." The shift from multi-company to Chick-fil-A-targeted content between 2013 and 2015 reflects a broader pattern in food-brand social media dynamics: brand-specific fear content generates more engagement than diffuse industry claims because it maps onto existing brand associations and allows consumers to signal dietary virtue by criticizing a specific brand rather than the industry as a whole.

*Eat This, Not That* is a media brand and book series associated with authors David Zinczenko and Matt Goulding, focused on comparative nutritional advice. Its Twitter account is a legitimate consumer information outlet, but the tweet did not provide context, safety information, or concentration data. The rhetorical economy of the Twitter format — then 140 characters — structurally penalizes the kind of contextual information that would have made the claim accurately informative rather than alarmist.

Freedhoff and Sharma (2010, *Canadian Medical Association Journal*) examined media coverage of food science findings and found that news media presentations of ingredient safety information systematically omit dose, concentration, and regulatory context in favor of identity-level claims (this compound appears in this product). Their finding that the "dose makes the poison" principle is effectively invisible in popular food journalism is directly relevant to the PDMS case: the claim is structured to make the presence of a compound synonymous with the presence of a hazard, suppressing the information — concentration — that is definitively relevant to any safety assessment.

## §6 — Summary and Research Gap Assessment

Chick-fil-A uses dimethylpolysiloxane as an anti-foaming agent in its frying oil at trace concentrations authorized by the FDA and reviewed as safe by WHO/JECFA. Silly Putty contains dimethylpolysiloxane at approximately four percent of its composition, with the remainder being other compounds that are not in Chick-fil-A's food. The claim that Chick-fil-A sandwiches "contain Silly Putty" is false by any compositional standard. The claim that the compound is "banned in the UK" is also false. The underlying true fact — that PDMS appears in both Chick-fil-A products and in Silly Putty — does not support the implied (or explicit) safety concern, because PDMS is a safe food additive at food-use concentrations and is used by numerous food manufacturers.

**EARC Classification: A (Analytical)** — The claim deploys a specific and analyzable rhetorical strategy (shared-ingredient conflation) that is generalizable beyond this individual case and whose mechanisms deserve systematic empirical study.

**Research Gap:** No published study has systematically quantified the "shared-ingredient conflation" pattern across the corpus of viral food-fear content, analyzed what consumer characteristics predict susceptibility to this argument form, or tested what corrective information most effectively addresses it. A study combining content analysis of viral food-fear social media posts with experimental consumer survey methodology would be feasible and policy-relevant, especially given the ongoing prevalence of this rhetorical pattern in TikTok-era food content.
