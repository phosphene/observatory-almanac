# "A Man in Malaysia Was Bitten by a Python in His Toilet"


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: A man living in Malaysia was bitten on an intimate part of his body by a python that had coiled itself inside his toilet bowl. The incident was documented with photographs and received international news coverage, circulating on social media as a striking — and for many readers, viscerally alarming — example of urban wildlife encounters in Southeast Asia.

**The specific incident**: In the version that gained viral traction in 2022, a Malaysian man named Sabri Tazali posted about the encounter on Twitter (now X), sharing photographs of the snake coiled inside the toilet bowl and describing having been bitten during what he clearly intended to be a routine bathroom visit. His account included images of the python wedged inside the bowl and subsequent media coverage noted that he sought medical treatment for the bite, which, while startling, was not life-threatening.

**Where it spread**: Coverage was picked up by major international English-language outlets including Newsweek, The Straits Times of Singapore, and various Southeast Asian regional news services. The images of the snake inside the toilet bowl were particularly striking and shareable — the visual was at once alarming and almost absurdist — and the story propagated rapidly across social media platforms. Many readers encountering the story expressed a mix of horror, dark humor, and renewed anxiety about their own bathroom routines.

**Related earlier circulation**: A broadly similar story circulated in April 2021 involving a home in Johor, Malaysia — again involving a python found in a toilet — though in that earlier incident, a bite was not explicitly reported in all accounts. The 2022 Sabri Tazali incident provided more documentation than the 2021 report and attracted more unambiguous international attention.

**Why the claim requires fact-checking**: Stories of this nature — involving either exotic animals in unexpected domestic settings or dramatic wildlife encounters — are a category that social media amplifies readily, partly because they are emotionally compelling and partly because they are difficult to verify from a distance. Readers encountering such stories might reasonably wonder whether they are fabrications, whether photographs have been staged, or whether the incident involved misidentification of the species involved. The geographic specificity (Malaysia), the named individual, and the photographic documentation all provide more than usual grounds for verification.

**The broader context**: The claim situates itself within a recognized pattern of reticulated pythons (Python reticulatus) entering human habitations in Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and other parts of Southeast Asia. Unlike the more extreme variants of snake-toilet stories that circulate online — some of which have been shown to involve staged or manipulated imagery — this claim had authenticated documentation and was consistent with established natural-historical knowledge about reticulated python behavior in urban environments.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The incident is documented and confirmed**: Sabri Tazali's account, posted on Twitter/X in March 2022, documented a reticulated python bite sustained in his own toilet. Photographs confirmed the snake's presence inside the bowl. He was treated medically for the bite. Newsweek (2022) and The Straits Times (2022) independently reported on the incident based on his social media posts and additional corroboration. Snopes investigated and found the incident to be authentic.

**Reticulated pythons in Malaysia**: The reticulated python (Malayopython reticulatus, formerly Python reticulatus) is the world's longest snake species and is native to South and Southeast Asia, including Malaysia. Adult reticulated pythons regularly exceed 4–5 meters in length, and exceptional individuals have been documented at over 7 meters (Barker, D. G., & Barker, T. M., 2008, *Bulletin of the Chicago Herpetological Society*). The species is highly adaptable to disturbed and urban habitats and is one of the most commonly encountered large constrictors in Malaysian cities, towns, and peri-urban areas. They are opportunistic hunters that exploit any available passage when seeking shelter, food, or thermoregulation.

**How pythons enter plumbing systems**: Reticulated pythons and other large constrictors are capable of navigating drainage and sewage infrastructure. Urban drainage systems in tropical cities are typically designed for water volume management rather than wildlife exclusion, and gaps sufficient for a snake to enter exist at drainage outfalls, manhole connections, and where pipes connect to older or poorly maintained toilet fixtures. The snakes are drawn to these enclosed, humid passages because they approximate the refuge habitats — dense vegetation, rock crevices, hollow logs — that pythons exploit in natural environments (Shine, R., 2010, *BioScience*). Toilet waste pipes in older building stock in Southeast Asia often lack the S-trap configuration that would pose a greater physical barrier, making direct entry into toilet bowls more plausible than might be assumed from Western plumbing standards.

**Temperature as a driver of shelter-seeking behavior**: Tropical Southeast Asia experiences periods of high ambient temperature during which snakes — as ectotherms that cannot regulate body temperature internally — actively seek cool, shaded refugia. Indoor plumbing offers a passage from warm outdoor environments to a cooler, humidity-controlled interior. Research on thermoregulatory behavior in large pythons has confirmed that temperature-seeking drives movement into unusual microhabitats (Slip, D. J., & Shine, R., 1988, *Journal of Herpetology*). During Malaysia's hottest periods, snakes entering buildings through drainage systems are encountered with greater frequency.

**Medical significance of a reticulated python bite**: Reticulated pythons are non-venomous constrictors. Their primary hunting technique is constriction — they grip prey with their jaws and coil their bodies to prevent breathing — and their dentition consists of rows of small, backward-curved teeth designed to grip rather than puncture deeply. A bite from a reticulated python of the size likely involved in a toilet encounter (2–3 meters, smaller than a mature adult) would produce lacerations from the teeth, risk of bacterial infection from oral microbiota, and significant psychological distress, but would not involve venom injection (Warrell, D. A., 2010, *Clinical Medicine*). The backward-curved teeth characteristic of pythons can cause tearing lacerations if the snake clamps and the person reflexively pulls away, which is the likely mechanism of the wounds Tazali sustained.

**Frequency of similar incidents**: While incidents of snakes found in toilets make international news when they occur, they are not unprecedented in Malaysia and the broader region. Fire departments and wildlife rescue services in Kuala Lumpur and other Malaysian cities report multiple calls per year involving snakes in residential plumbing, particularly during hot seasons. Similar incidents have been reported in Thailand, Indonesia, and the Philippines. A 2016 incident in Thailand involving a python in a toilet was widely reported after the snake bit a man's penis — a case eerily similar to the 2022 Malaysian incident — underscoring that this category of encounter, while unusual, is a real and recurring phenomenon in these regions (Bangkok Post, 2016, news report).

**The 2021 Johor incident**: The April 2021 viral circulation identifying a python in a toilet in Johor, Malaysia, may have referred to a separate incident documented by a Malaysian newspaper. Snopes noted at the time that while it could not independently verify all details of that specific incident, the general claim that pythons enter toilets in Malaysia was well-supported by historical wildlife encounter data.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty — the visceral accessibility of toilet-animal encounter stories**:

There is something categorically distinctive about the psychological architecture of toilet-animal encounter stories, and this distinctiveness explains both why they are compelling and why their veracity is often initially doubted.

The toilet occupies an unusual phenomenological position in human consciousness. It is the space of maximum human physical vulnerability — unclothed, physically constrained by the posture required, distracted, and in a location that is both private and therefore unmonitored. The toilet bowl itself, a fixture so familiar as to be background-invisible in normal consciousness, is also — when attention is even briefly directed there — an aperture to a pressurized water system with connections extending throughout a building and into the urban infrastructure beneath it. The conceptual transformation of this aperture from benign fixture to entry point for a large predator produces a degree of visceral alarm that is essentially involuntary. The story is compelling not because people consciously reason about the plausibility of python plumbing; they react with automatic fear before any analytical processing occurs.

This automatic fear response suppresses analytical skepticism. Stories that provoke pre-analytic fear are processed differently than emotionally neutral claims. The fight-or-flight circuit that evaluates "is this a threat?" does not require factual accuracy checks; it operates on pattern recognition. "Python in toilet" is recognized as threat before it is evaluated as claim. This means that people who would normally apply skepticism to unusual animal stories — who would note the absence of corroboration, the improbability of the specific scenario — often fail to apply that skepticism before accepting and spreading the story. The emotional wallop functions as an epistemically destructive shortcut.

The complementary mechanism is that because toilet-snake stories are so physiologically compelling, they are also widely fabricated, semi-staged, and sensationalized in ways that genuinely warrant skepticism. The internet contains multiple viral "snake in toilet" images and videos that have subsequently been identified as hoaxes, staged encounters, or misattributed events from different countries. This history of false claims creates reasonable grounds for skepticism toward new, apparently similar stories. A practised news-reader who has seen previous snake-toilet hoaxes debunked may therefore apply skepticism to authentic incidents as part of a reasonable general policy, producing cases where warranted skepticism incorrectly disbelieves a true claim. This is a real epistemic cost of the hoax ecosystem.

The Malaysian geographic context matters for international audiences, who lack reliable base-rate knowledge about python encounters in Southeast Asian cities. For a reader in North America or Europe, a python in a toilet is an event whose base rate is perceived as effectively zero; for a reader in Kuala Lumpur, it is low but not vanishingly rare. This cross-cultural difference in perceived base rates produces systematic divergence in willingness to believe the story, with Western audiences being more skeptical than is warranted and regional audiences being less surprised than they would appear to outsiders.


## 4. Verdict

**TRUE**: The claim is confirmed. Sabri Tazali was bitten by a reticulated python in his toilet in Malaysia in March 2022, documented by photographs and corroborated by multiple news outlets. The incident is consistent with established knowledge of reticulated python behavior in urban Southeast Asia.

**What the evidence shows**: The photographic documentation, Tazali's own account, his medical treatment, and independent news reporting from Newsweek and The Straits Times collectively establish the authenticity of the core claim. Snopes' investigation found no evidence of fabrication or misrepresentation. The biological and ecological facts are consistent with a genuine incident of this type occurring.

**What actually happened physiologically**: The python likely entered the building through the drainage system, drawn by the cool, humid environment of the plumbing. It coiled in the toilet bowl as a thermally suitable refuge. When disturbed by Tazali, it bit reflexively — a defensive response, not a predatory one. At the probable size of the snake (consistent with a sub-adult reticulated python of 2–3 meters that could navigate the plumbing), the bite produced lacerations requiring medical attention but posed no life-threatening risk from venom. The psychological impact was presumably substantial.

**Scope of the confirmed claim**: The confirmed incident is the 2022 Tazali bite. Related 2021 Johor reports are consistent with realistic background frequency but were less fully documented. The broader claim that such events occur and are not unprecedented is strongly supported independently of the specific 2022 incident.

**What the claim does not imply**: The truthfulness of this claim does not imply that toilet-python encounters are common or that average residents of Malaysia should modify their bathroom routine as a routine safety precaution. The incident is real but rare. Precautionary measures applicable in areas with confirmed wildlife incursion history include checking toilet bowls before use and ensuring drainage pipes are properly sealed, particularly in older buildings in areas adjacent to natural habitats — practical advice given by Malaysian wildlife authorities during high-encounter seasons.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**Reticulated pythons and human cohabitation in Southeast Asia**: The reticulated python is among the best-studied large constrictor species in the world partly because of its close association with human habitations. Unlike many large snake species whose ranges have contracted as human development has expanded, the reticulated python has demonstrated considerable adaptability to urban and peri-urban landscapes. In Southeast Asian cities, pythons exploit several features of the built environment: drainage infrastructure for transit and refuge, human-associated food sources (primarily rats, which throng in urban environments), and the thermal buffering that concrete buildings provide relative to the open environment. Research by Shine, R. et al. (2010, *BioScience*) on the ecology of invasive pythons in Florida — a population derived from released or escaped pets — has informed understanding of how these animals navigate human-built environments, though the Florida Everglades context differs substantially from the original Southeast Asian range.

**The public health dimension of human-snake interface**: Beyond the dramatic toilet-encounter category, the interface between human populations and large snakes in Southeast Asia has significant public health implications. Reticulated pythons are capable of killing adult humans — documented fatalities, though rare, have occurred in Indonesia and the Philippines, and multiple cases have been reported in the scientific and news literature (Langner, C. et al., 2014, *Journal of Forensic Sciences*). The more common medical issue is bite wounds from encounters with defensive or captive snakes, which require wound management and monitoring for infection. Local health authorities in Malaysia maintain awareness of this risk particularly in areas bordering forest reserves and wetlands.

**The drainage and infrastructure ecology of Southeast Asian cities**: Tropical megacities like Kuala Lumpur and Bangkok have drainage systems built over decades that combine modern and legacy infrastructure, creating a network that is physically accessible to large animals in ways that more recently Built cities may not be. Reticulated pythons, monitor lizards, and other large reptiles have all been found in urban drainage systems and building interiors across the region. The ecological drivers — warmth, food availability, refuge quality — are understood, but the built-environment management responses remain inconsistent. Some Malaysian municipalities have begun retrofitting drainage outfalls with exclusion grilles, but comprehensive infrastructure-level wildlife exclusion programs remain rare.

**The role of social media in wildlife encounter documentation**: The Tazali incident is part of a broader shift in how urban wildlife encounters are recorded and shared. Where previously such incidents might be reported to local news with limited photographic documentation, smartphone camera quality and social media ubiquity now mean that even highly transient encounters can produce high-quality visual records that survive for independent verification. This has genuinely improved fact-checkers' ability to assess the authenticity of unusual wildlife encounter claims. The photographic documentation Tazali provided was central to Snopes' ability to confirm the incident.

**The comparative context of other toilet-wildlife encounters**: The toilet-snake category is the most dramatic and widely shared, but encounters with other animals in domestic plumbing are reported globally. Rats and mice commonly enter homes through plumbing; possums and rats are occasionally found in Australian toilet systems; scorpions and large spiders inhabit bathroom drain systems in parts of the American Southwest and Australia. These encounters form a category of "domestic wildlife invasion" that causes disproportionate public anxiety relative to actual epidemiological risk. The specific psychological contribution of the toilet — as the location of maximum exposure and minimum defensive capacity — ensures that plumbing-entry wildlife encounters will continue to generate intense media attention regardless of their objective rarity.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Snopes approach**: Snopes addressed this claim by verifying the existence and identity of Sabri Tazali, confirming the authenticity of the photographs he posted, and seeking corroboration from international news outlets that independently reported the incident. Given the photographic documentation and multiple-source corroboration, Snopes assessed the claim as true. The fact-check noted that while such incidents are unusual, reticulated pythons entering homes via plumbing infrastructure is a documented phenomenon in Malaysia and surrounding countries.

**The earlier 2021 claim**: Snopes' handling of the related 2021 Johor claim was more cautious — the earlier incident was similarly structured but with less robust documentation, and Snopes identified it as "unproven" at that stage while noting that the general phenomenon of pythons in Malaysian toilets was not implausible. The 2022 Tazali incident, better documented, shifted the overall assessment.

**Verification methodology for animal encounter claims**: The general methodology for verifying claims of this type involves (1) geolocating the incident to the claimed region and confirming that the claimed species exists there; (2) assessing whether the ecological scenario described is biologically plausible; (3) verifying photographic evidence for signs of manipulation or misattribution; (4) seeking primary account corroboration from named individuals and secondary confirmation from news outlets with regional access. The Tazali claim met these criteria.

**Media framing vs. factual core**: One challenge in reporting this type of story is that the emotional framing of media coverage tends to inflate the implied risk and frequency of the events. Headlines such as "Man Bitten by Python While Using Toilet" are accurate but encourage readers to update their priors on overall snake-toilet encounter frequency far more than the data justifies. Responsible fact-checking of such claims includes not only verifying the specific incident but contextualizing its frequency — a function that Snopes and similar organizations perform when assessing whether a claim, even when true, is being used to construct a misleading pattern.

**Wildlife authority response**: Malaysian wildlife authorities and the Department of Wildlife and National Parks (PERHILITAN) periodically issue advisories about human-wildlife conflict in peri-urban settings, and these advisories serve as a factual backdrop against which individual incidents can be assessed. The existence of a wildlife rescue infrastructure specifically oriented toward snake extraction from buildings and homes provides institutional evidence of the phenomenon's frequency in the region, confirming that the Tazali incident was not an isolated impossibility but a rare instance of a known category of event.

**Social media verification tools**: Reverse image searching the photographs in the Tazali case and in earlier related claims allows verification that images originated from the claimed time and location and had not been previously circulated in unrelated contexts. This is a standard tool in modern fact-checking, and its application to snake-toilet claims has historically revealed both fabrications (recycled images from different incidents) and authentic new documentation. In the Tazali case, the images were consistent with new and authentic documentation.
