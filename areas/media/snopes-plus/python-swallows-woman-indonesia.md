## §1 — Claim and Verdict

The claim: in June 2018, a 54-year-old woman named Wa Tiba disappeared from a village in Muna regency, Southeast Sulawesi, Indonesia, and was subsequently found inside the belly of a large reticulated python (*Python reticulatus*). The snake was killed and dissected by local residents, and the woman's body was recovered intact.

Snopes rated this claim **True**.

The verdict is supported by: (1) contemporaneous reporting by multiple international wire services and regional Indonesian media; (2) expert authentication of video footage by two leading academic herpetologists; and (3) independent assessment of the physical plausibility of the event based on documented characteristics of large reticulated pythons.

The case is unusual in the category of large-snake-eating-human stories. The overwhelming majority of such claims in online circulation are hoaxes, misidentifications, or exaggerations — so the species' actual capacity and occasional practice of predating humans tends to be dismissed in the same epistemic movement as the fabrications. This verdict demonstrates that the fabrications and the genuine cases belong to different epistemic categories and must be evaluated independently.

## §2 — Origin and Viral Spread

Wa Tiba, a 54-year-old housewife from Muna Island, Southeast Sulawesi, disappeared on the night of Thursday, 14 June 2018 — the last night of Ramadan. She had left home to inspect her corn plantation approximately one kilometer away, where wild boar regularly damaged crops. Her family became alarmed when she had not returned by 6 a.m. the following morning. Her sister went to the plantation site and found Wa Tiba's footprints, a torch, her sandals, and a machete on the ground, but no sign of Wa Tiba herself.

At approximately 9:30 a.m., local residents discovered a large python — reported variously as 7 to 8 meters in length — lying near the plantation with a visibly swollen midsection. Residents killed the snake and dissected its stomach, inside which they found Wa Tiba's body, clothed and largely intact, swallowed head-first.

The local Indonesian news site Tribunnews.com was among the first to report the story, on 16 June 2018, with photographs and video. The case was reported the same day by the *Jakarta Post* (an English-language Indonesian newspaper) and picked up by the Associated Press and Agence France-Presse, reaching international audiences through the *Washington Post*, BBC, and other major outlets.

The story attracted immediate skepticism online, partly because the category of python-eating-human stories is heavily contaminated with fabrications — older viral images of pythons "eating" men in Sulawesi had previously been debunked as misidentifications or manipulated images. Readers familiar with this history of hoaxing applied appropriate skepticism, which prompted Snopes to seek expert verification rather than taking media reports at face value.

## §3 — Epistemic Analysis

This case presents an **authenticity problem that viral animal stories routinely face but rarely resolve in the affirmative**: because so many large-snake-eating-human claims are false, legitimate instances inherit the epistemic shadow of the fabrications. Snopes' approach to this problem — going directly to academic experts in herpetology and asking them to evaluate video footage — is methodologically significant.

The epistemic novelty here is the **expert authentication protocol applied to video evidence from an isolated, non-English-speaking context**. This is a distinctive challenge in global fact-checking:

1. The primary source (Tribunnews.com, regional Indonesian) is in Indonesian, inaccessible to most fact-checkers' audiences.
2. The event occurred in a rural, geographically isolated context (Muna Island) with limited external observers who could corroborate the account.
3. Official statements came from local police (credible in principle but not independently verifiable for an international audience).
4. Video evidence existed but required expert interpretation — specifically, distinguishing genuine python-human ingestion footage from prior fabrications.

Snopes resolved this by contacting Jim McGuire, a professor of integrative biology and curator of herpetology in the Museum of Vertebrate Zoology at the University of California, Berkeley. McGuire reviewed the footage and stated: "There is no doubt in my mind — zero — that this is totally authentic." He added crucial context: such events, while rare, likely occur "every few years" on Sulawesi and its adjacent islands, but went undocumented before mobile phone video capability.

A second independent expert, Harry W. Greene — emeritus professor in the Department of Ecology and Evolutionary Biology at Cornell University and the author of *Snakes: The Evolution of Mystery in Nature* (University of California Press, 2000) — independently confirmed the footage's authenticity. Greene described the video as showing "slitting open an adult — but I would judge not gigantic — Reticulated Python, which initially has a visibly large, but not impressively so, food lump, thereby exposing the freshly ingested (little or no visible effects of digestion) fully clothed female human, swallowed head-first."

The agreement of two independently contacted expert witnesses — neither of whom had reason to misrepresent — constitutes strong epistemic support independent of any institutional media chain. This is the standard of evidence that distinguishes this verified case from the surrounding ecosystem of python hoaxes.

## §4 — Scientific and Biological Context

The reticulated python (*Python reticulatus*) holds the title of the world's longest extant snake species. Verified maximum lengths exceed 7.5 meters; the herpetological literature documents individual specimens approaching and occasionally exceeding 8 meters. The largest specimen on reliable record measured approximately 10 meters, according to Greene's account to Snopes. These dimensions are sufficient — by a significant margin — to physically accommodate an adult human being.

Python feeding mechanics are worth understanding in some anatomical detail, because the claim that a python could swallow a human being strikes most people as implausible on first consideration. Pythons are constrictors: they kill prey by coiling around it and applying pressure sufficient to cause cardiac arrest and respiratory failure, then swallow prey whole. Unlike mammals, snakes lack a fused mandible — the two lower jaw bones are connected by an elastic ligament (the symphysis) that allows the jaws to spread laterally and accommodate prey items far wider than the snake's resting diameter. The esophagus and body wall are highly elastic.

Greene provided the key quantitative context: "Average meals are likely in the neighborhood of 30–60% of the snake's mass." The largest natural prey items on record for giant constricting snakes (boas and pythons) are "right at or slightly more than 100% of the predator's mass." A large reticulated python might weigh 68–90 kg (150–200 pounds); a rural Indonesian woman with a lifetime of agricultural labor, at 54 years of age, might weigh 45–55 kg — roughly 50–65% of a large python's mass. This falls within the documented prey-mass range.

Headland and Greene (2011) conducted a systematic study of python-human interactions in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America*, examining records from the Agta Negritos, a hunter-gatherer people of the Philippines. From a sample observed between 1934 and 1973, 26% of adult men had been attacked by reticulated pythons, and six fatal attacks were documented in 39 years. The study characterized the Agta's relationship with pythons as one of mutual predation: "People and pythons were reciprocally prey, predators, and potential competitors." The Agta both hunted pythons for food and were preyed upon by them.

This study's significance is often underappreciated in popular discourse: it documents systematic predation on humans by reticulated pythons in a context where traditional ecological relationships and lack of firearms meant humans were genuinely within the prey size range of large pythons. The predation frequency among the Agta does not represent typical Indonesian village life today (where firearms, machetes, and habitat fragmentation alter the encounter parameters), but it demonstrates that python predation on humans is not a theoretical possibility — it is a documented historical pattern.

McGuire emphasized a key factor in why such events are underreported: "Snakes typically hide after eating and are therefore not so likely to be detected — to record an instance of python predation, someone would have to witness the actual attack, or subsequently (as in this case) locate and dissect the snake that ate the person." The rarity of documentation is a function of how snakes feed and hide, not an indication that predation is rare.

**What cannot be verified:**

Snopes appropriately noted the limits of its verification: Wa Tiba's precise age, the exact sequence of events on the night of her disappearance, and the precise statements attributed to specific local officials cannot be independently confirmed from outside Indonesia. The verdict relies on the authenticated video footage, expert assessment, and corroborated media reports — not on a complete independent reconstruction of the incident.

**Rating confidence:** High. Expert authentication from two independent academic herpetologists provides the anchor that transforms media reporting into verified fact. The physical plausibility is well-established by the literature; the video evidence is unequivocal to qualified observers.

## §6 — Broader Implications

This case raises important questions about **the epistemology of extreme but genuine events** — events that are so dramatic that they are presumed to be false until proven otherwise, even when evidence supports their authenticity.

The existence of a large ecosystem of python-eating-human hoaxes has a real epistemic cost: it creates what might be called a **false-positive bias in debunking**. Audiences who have learned to be skeptical of large-snake claims become calibrated to dismiss them categorically, even when the evidence warrants a different conclusion. This represents an overcorrection: the appropriate response to a history of fabrications in a category is not to disbelieve all new claims in that category, but to apply rigorous evidence standards and update when those standards are met.

The verified frequency of reticulated python predation on humans in Southeast Asia is itself underappreciated in popular media representations. The Headland and Greene (2011) study — 26% of Agta men attacked, six fatalities in 39 observed years among a small hunter-gatherer group — suggests that in environments where humans and large pythons interact regularly, predation is neither vanishingly rare nor fantastical. This has implications for conservation policy: managing large python populations in areas of human agricultural activity requires understanding the real, documented risk rather than either dismissing it (because the category is contaminated with hoaxes) or inflating it (because dramatic snake images generate social media engagement).

The story also highlights a persistent gap in Southeast Asian wildlife fatality data. Unlike large-predator fatalities in Africa and South Asia — where lion, leopard, elephant, and crocodile attacks are tracked by conservation organizations and international health bodies — reticulated python fatalities in Indonesia, Malaysia, and the Philippines lack a systematic reporting mechanism. McGuire's observation that most events go undocumented because the snake hides after eating is a recognizable obstacle for field researchers: the very mechanism of the predation removes the evidence from public view.

From a human-wildlife conflict management perspective, the most actionable implication is mundane but important: rural communities in the range of large reticulated pythons should possess the knowledge (and ideally tools) to recognize and respond to the scenario that killed Wa Tiba — a missing person, slippers and a machete found at a remote location, a nearby snake with a distended abdomen — as a potential predation event requiring snake dissection rather than continued search.
