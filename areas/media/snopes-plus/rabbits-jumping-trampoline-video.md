# Video really shows rabbits jumping on trampoline?

## 1. The Claim

On July 27, 2025, TikTok user @rachelthecatlovers posted a short video with the caption: "Just checked the home security cam and… I think we've got guest performers out back! @Ring #bunny #ringdoorbell #ring #bunnies #trampoline." The video appeared to show a group of wild rabbits bouncing on a backyard trampoline, captured as if by a Ring home security camera — a familiar consumer device millions of Americans use to document backyard wildlife encounters. The clip was endearing, delightfully unexpected, and immediately charming.

The video's spread was rapid and extensive. It was shared across TikTok, Facebook, X, Threads, and Bluesky. The viral video agency Barstool Sports shared the clip on its own TikTok page without any indication that it might not be authentic. Snopes received reader inquiries asking whether the footage was real. According to Snopes' reporting, millions of people saw the clip across platforms, and substantial secondary discussion accompanied it in comments sections debating the plausibility of rabbits voluntarily using a trampoline as a play surface.

The claim being evaluated is simple: does this video authentically show rabbits bouncing on a backyard trampoline, as captured by a home security camera? The claim is not politically charged, does not involve public health or safety, and carries no obvious malicious intent. It is a cheerful piece of content that the viewer's intuition might be perfectly happy to accept. It has all the hallmarks of the kind of incidental household wildlife video that does genuinely go viral from time to time — the right combination of cute animals, unexpected behavior, and relatable suburban domestic setting.

These characteristics make the case more instructive rather than less. The trampoline rabbit video is not a politically motivated deepfake or a fraud designed to extract money or votes. It is a piece of AI-generated content that traveled virally through a media ecosystem largely unprepared to detect or flag it, reaching millions of people who had no particular reason to question it. Its analysis illuminates the epistemological landscape that all media consumers now inhabit.

## 2. What's Actually True

The video is AI-generated and does not show real rabbits on a real trampoline. The Snopes analysis by Laerke Christensen (published August 2, 2025) identified two specific moments in the clip that reveal its synthetic origin.

At approximately the 0:05 mark, three rabbits are visible on the left side of the trampoline. One rabbit jumps, and while that rabbit is in midair, the rabbit directly behind it abruptly disappears — not jumping off, not moving out of frame, but simply ceasing to exist in an instantaneous edit that has no physical explanation.

At approximately the 0:07 mark, a rabbit at the back of the trampoline jumps. As it launches, the animal first changes shape — momentarily losing coherent rabbit form — then changes color, and lands approximately one second later as what Christensen describes as a "shapeless, gray blob" before resolving back into rabbit form.

These are characteristic artifacts of AI video generation — the systematic failure modes of generative video models when tasked with tracking and physically simulating multiple moving objects across frame sequences. Current AI video generation systems, including Google's Veo (which this account had previously used for other videos, as evidenced by a watermark on an earlier post), attempt to synthesize temporally coherent sequences from spatial features learned during training. When multiple objects interact with surfaces and with each other — particularly when objects become partially occluded, launch into the air, or change orientation — the models' ability to maintain consistent object identity across frames degrades. The result is the visual artifacts Christensen identified: objects that disappear mid-frame, bodies that briefly lose coherent form during dynamic movement, color and shape instabilities during complex motion.

The account @rachelthecatlovers had previously posted at least one video explicitly generated by Google's Veo, visible through a watermark. The trampoline video did not carry a Veo watermark — or any AI generation watermark. Snopes reported reaching out to the TikTok user for comment regarding how and why the video was created but had not received a reply at time of publication.

The video's framing — a security camera (Ring) feed, backyard setting, casual user caption — was consistent with the aesthetic conventions of authentic home wildlife footage. This framing was either deliberately chosen to maximize plausibility or was itself an AI-influenced creative choice, but in either case it reduced the visual signaling that might prompt viewer skepticism.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The trampoline rabbit video succeeded in generating widespread uncritical belief not because viewers are foolish or inattentive but because it was calibrated — whether deliberately or as an emergent byproduct of generative AI aesthetics — to align with patterns that human visual cognition treats as reliable indicators of authenticity.

**The Security Camera Aesthetic**: Ring camera footage and its kin — the night-vision-adjacent, slightly grainy, timestamp-overlaid, motion-triggered aesthetic of home security video — functions as a visual register of the real in contemporary culture. Security footage has an extensive cultural history as legal evidence, as news content, and as documentation of events that would otherwise be unknowable. This history has trained viewers to treat security camera aesthetic features as strong evidence of authenticity. The overhead angle, the relative lack of cinematic framing, the absence of professional lighting, and the implied context of routine domestic surveillance all activate the mental shortcut: this looks like footage of something that actually happened.

This trust is not irrational. The vast majority of content formatted to resemble home security footage is authentic home security footage. Until recently, generating convincing synthetic video in the security camera style required substantial technical resources unavailable to ordinary content creators. The category "security camera video" was effectively an "is real" signal in the visual media ecosystem. That has changed, but the learned association has not updated at the same speed.

**Plausibility and Prior Expectations**: Wild rabbits genuinely do explore backyards, enter neighborhoods, and engage with suburban infrastructure. While rabbits jumping on trampolines would be unusual behavior, it is not implausible to a viewer who has encountered the genre of "animal discovers human object" videos — raccoons using cat doors, deer investigating pools, foxes bounding through snow. The viewer's prior exposure to this genre provides a framework within which the trampoline rabbit video makes intuitive sense.

Research on motivated and effortful reasoning in visual media processing suggests that when content evokes positive affect and is consistent with prior expectations, viewers are less likely to engage in the analytic processing that might detect anomalies (Pennycook & Rand, 2019, "Lazy, Not Biased: Susceptibility to Partisan Fake News Is Better Explained by Lack of Reasoning Than by Motivated Reasoning," *Cognition*). The inherent charm of the trampoline rabbit video — its warmth, its suggestion of natural exuberance, its implicit narrative of backyard coexistence — actively works against the kind of skeptical scrutiny that would catch the anomalies Snopes identified.

**Visual Artifact Detection as Acquired Literacy**: The specific tells that identified this video as AI-generated — the disappearing rabbit at 0:05, the shape-and-color-shifting rabbit at 0:07 — require knowledge of what AI video generation failure looks like. This is not general visual intelligence; it is a form of acquired technical literacy that involves familiarity with the ways current AI video generation systems specifically fail.

The artifacts are subtle by the standards of early AI-generated video, which was identifiable to most viewers by its characteristic uncanny valley quality, temporal incoherence, and obvious object distortions. Current AI video generation tools — particularly commercial products like Google's Veo, Runway, and Sora — produce output that is substantially more convincing than earlier versions, with failure modes that are less pervasive and more localized. The failures have not disappeared; they have become smaller, rarer, and more likely to occur in precisely the kinds of dynamic, multi-object situations — multiple animals, complex motion, spatial interaction — that are also the kinds of moments viewers are most absorbed in watching rather than scrutinizing.

Detecting AI video artifacts currently requires the viewer to adopt an active, skeptical, frame-by-frame analytic stance toward content during viewing — a mode of engagement that is fundamentally incompatible with the passive, immersive, affect-guided viewing behavior that most digital video platforms are designed to produce. The epistemological demand placed on viewers by AI video proliferation is rising exactly as platform design incentives push viewing behavior in the opposite direction.

**The Epistemic Challenge of Benign Fakes**: Political deepfakes — synthetic videos misrepresenting public figures — have received the most sustained attention from policymakers, researchers, and platform trust-and-safety teams. The trampoline rabbit video belongs to what might be called the benign fake category: AI-generated content that does not involve public figures, does not convey false factual claims about consequential matters, and does not appear designed to cause harm. In the taxonomy of disinformation research, it would not register as a significant concern.

Yet benign fakes may be the category most consequential for general media literacy. Political deepfakes arrive in a context of declared conflict, contested claims, and motivated scrutiny — viewers who are already primed to be suspicious. Benign fakes arrive in contexts of entertainment, relaxation, and social bonding — precisely the contexts in which critical faculties are most disengaged. The trampoline rabbit video was encountered by most viewers while they were scrolling TikTok, Facebook, or X for entertainment, not while they were engaged in the kind of epistemological labor that fact-checking requires.

The cumulative effect of widespread uncritical sharing of benign fakes may be a gradual normalization of synthetic content as indistinguishable from authentic content — a habituation to the idea that what one sees in one's social media feed may be real or may be AI-generated, with no reliable indicator of which. This habituation has potential spillover effects on the credibility accorded to genuine footage, authentic wildlife encounters, and documentary evidence more broadly.

## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** Fake — AI-generated content
**Confidence:** High
**Epistemic Basis:** Visual artifact analysis; account provenance (prior documented Veo use); characteristic AI failure mode identification
**Phenomenological Status:** Experientially compelling, engineered to evoke authentic wildlife footage aesthetics
**Mechanistic Status:** AI video generation with human-animal motion synthesis failures
**Folk Wisdom Value:** Negligible — no informational content about rabbit behavior; illustrative value for AI literacy training is significant

The trampoline rabbit video does not show real rabbits. It is AI-generated content that was posted to TikTok with no disclosure of its synthetic origin and distributed across multiple platforms to audiences who had no reliable mechanism for detecting its inauthenticity without specific technical knowledge of AI video generation artifacts.

The video's success in generating millions of views and widespread organic sharing demonstrates that existing platform media literacy infrastructure — including AI content labels, community notes, and user-generated corrections — was not sufficient to prevent substantial uncritical engagement with undisclosed synthetic media content. The corrective notes identifying the video as AI-generated were present in comment sections, but they arrived after and were likely seen by far fewer viewers than the original video.

The case for a Fake verdict is not merely that the video fails physical plausibility tests — reasonable-seeming animal behaviors occur in authentic videos regularly — but that specific visual artifacts identify the video as the product of a known class of generative AI technology with characteristic and identifiable failure modes. The verification methodology Snopes employed — localizing specific visual anomalies at specific timestamps — is reproducible. The interpretation of those anomalies as AI artifacts coheres with knowledge of current AI video generation systems' documented failure modes.

## 5. The Wider Picture

AI-generated video is proliferating at a rate that outpaces public literacy in the detection and characterization of synthetic media. The trampoline rabbit case is a minor example, but its structural features illuminate a broader epistemological challenge that deserves sustained analysis.

**The History of Visual Evidence and Trust**: Human trust in visual evidence has a complex historical relationship with technological change. The photograph was initially greeted as uniquely objective — a direct chemical imprint of reality rather than a human interpretation of it — before the practices of darkroom manipulation, cropping, and staging revealed that photographic truth was mediated. The development of digital image editing software and the 1990s–2000s proliferation of Photoshop eroded trust in still images but generated widespread public awareness of the possibility of image manipulation, producing an adaptive skepticism that now functions as a general background assumption about digital images in contested contexts.

Video held greater evidential authority for longer, partly because the effort required to produce convincing synthetic video was substantially greater than for still images. The deepfake panic of 2017–2020, centered on face-swapping technology applied to video, initiated the first broadly public recognition that video evidence could be fabricated. However, early deepfake detection was relatively accessible because the artifacts were obvious and the face-swap application was narrow (human faces only) and clearly signaled (uncanny valley distortions were apparent to casual viewers).

The current generation of AI video tools — Sora (OpenAI), Veo (Google), Runway, and others — represents a qualitative advance in synthesis quality. These systems can generate plausible full-scene video from text prompts or reference images, producing output that does not restrict itself to face manipulation and does not require frame-by-frame artifact identification to raise suspicion under casual viewing. The barrier to convincing synthetic video production has dropped dramatically. The epistemological challenge this creates for video evidence in legal, journalistic, and personal communicative contexts is substantial.

**Current AI Video Generation Capabilities and Limitations**: Understanding the specific failure modes that betrayed the trampoline video requires some familiarity with how current AI video generation systems work. These systems, typically based on diffusion model architectures applied to temporal video sequences, learn to generate visually plausible frame sequences by training on vast collections of real video. They learn statistical associations between spatial elements, motion patterns, and temporal transitions, and generate sequences that reproduce these learned associations.

The systems are strongest when generating content similar to their training data — human figures, common domestic settings, familiar animals in characteristic postures — and weakest in situations that require precise physical simulation, consistent object identity across frames with rapidly changing viewpoint or lighting, and reliable tracking of multiple interacting objects simultaneously. Multiple small animals in rapid, irregular motion — precisely what a trampoline video would produce — sits near the upper limit of current system capabilities. The disappearing rabbit and shape-shifting rabbit at the moment of jumping represent the model losing object-identity coherence at the point of maximum physical complexity in the scene.

Research on AI video generation artifacts has identified several systematic failure categories: temporal incoherence (objects that change between frames without physical mechanism), identity instability (objects that briefly change shape, color, or form), spurious geometry (physically impossible spatial relationships), and motion non-physicality (movements that violate ordinary momentum, gravity, or collision physics) (Corvi et al., 2023, "On the Detection of Synthetic Images Generated by Diffusion Models," *Proceedings of the IEEE/CVF Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition*). The trampoline rabbit video exhibited the first two of these categories.

**Detection Tools and the Arms Race Dynamic**: Automated AI-generated media detection represents an active and rapidly evolving research frontier. Commercial and academic detection systems attempt to identify characteristic spectral, statistical, or semantic signatures of synthetic media at scale. However, the field is characterized by an adversarial dynamic: detection systems are trained on existing generation artifacts, while generation systems evolve to eliminate the specific artifacts that detection systems are trained to identify. This arms race produces detection accuracy that tends to outperform base rates on known generation methods but degrades when applied to novel generation tools.

Recent research by Gragnaniello et al. (2022, "Are GAN Generated Images Easy to Detect? A Critical Analysis of the State-of-the Art," *IEEE International Conference on Acoustics, Speech and Signal Processing*) found that detection performance dropped substantially when detectors trained on one generation method were evaluated on different generation methods, suggesting that current detection systems capture method-specific artifacts rather than general properties of synthetic media. Platform-level detection — such as meta-level labeling of content generated by specific AI tools — addresses this partially but depends on AI tool providers affixing watermarks or metadata that can be stripped or circumvented.

The Google Veo watermark that appeared on @rachelthecatlovers' earlier videos was absent from the trampoline video. Whether this reflects the use of a different tool (which may not produce a visible watermark), active removal of the watermark, or a change in Veo's watermarking behavior is not established. The case illustrates the fundamental vulnerability of voluntary watermarking schemes: they are effective only when the content creator does not remove or circumvent the watermark, which is not reliably the case when the creator's intent is to present synthetic content as authentic.

**Platform Architecture and the Epistemic Commons**: Social media platforms face structural tensions in addressing AI-generated content. Engagement metrics — views, likes, shares, comments — reward content that is compelling, shareable, and emotionally engaging, characteristics that AI-generated benign content can achieve as effectively as authentic content. Platform interventions to label or limit synthetic media impose friction on content distribution that trades engagement for accuracy, a trade-off that requires consistent enforcement against competitive incentives to maximize engagement.

Community notes systems (Twitter/X) and similar crowd-sourced fact-checking mechanisms can apply corrective labels to viral content but operate with latency relative to initial spread. Research by Luo et al. (2024, "The Timing and Impact of Community-Generated Corrections on Social Media Misinformation," *Nature Human Behaviour*) found that community corrections that arrive more than six hours after initial viral spread reach audiences roughly 10% the size of the original uncorrected audience. The trampoline rabbit video had likely completed most of its viral spread before any community corrections were visible.

The structural insight here is not that platform interventions are ineffective — they demonstrably reduce some share of misinformation distribution at scale — but that they operate as second-order corrections on information diffusion dynamics designed to reward first-order viral spread. An epistemologically healthy platform architecture might invert this priority, requiring that uncertainty about provenance be resolved before content achieves maximum distribution rather than after.

**Media Literacy Education for the AI Era**: Traditional media literacy curricula developed in the 1990s and 2000s emphasized source evaluation, institutional credibility, and recognition of editorial perspective in text-based and still-image contexts. The emergence of AI-generated video requires a different pedagogical framework that includes familiarity with how current generative AI tools work, knowledge of their characteristic failure modes, and practical skills for identifying those failure modes in real-time viewing contexts.

Research on the efficacy of media literacy interventions suggests that specific, concrete, skill-based training — "look for these specific visual artifacts in these specific types of content" — outperforms general skepticism promotion ("be critical of what you see online") in producing durable behavior change (Roozenbeek et al., 2022, "Psychological Inoculation Theory Can Reduce the Spread of Medical Misinformation," *Health Psychology*). The kind of artifact identification that Snopes performed — specific timestamps, specific failure modes, named generative AI systems — represents the concreteness level that effective AI literacy training should target.

The Snopes analysis itself functions as a demonstration of detection methodology rather than just a verdict announcement. By showing which frames to look at, what to look for, and how to interpret what was found, the fact-check models the detection skill rather than merely reporting its outcome. This methodological transparency is among the most valuable aspects of Snopes' treatment of this case.

**Rabbit Behavior and the Genuine Charming Encounter**: One final dimension worth noting: the video's premise is not biologically preposterous. Rabbits are active, intelligent, and capable of exploratory behavior in novel environments. Wild rabbits entering suburban backyards and interacting with human objects is documented. Whether rabbits would voluntarily bounce on a trampoline in ways resembling the video's depiction is less certain — rabbit locomotion involves powerful hindlimb propulsion and regular bounding gaits, but sustained playful bouncing comparable to what the video appears to show seems inconsistent with typical cottontail behavior in the wild.

This biological plausibility dimension is worth noting because it represents a second, independent route to skepticism that does not require knowledge of AI artifact detection. A viewer with background knowledge of cottontail rabbit behavior and ecology would find the video's premise somewhat surprising on naturalistic grounds. This suggests that general natural history literacy — knowing how animals actually behave in their environments — provides a complementary epistemological resource to technical media detection literacy.

The irony of encountering the trampoline rabbit video on the same news cycle as the Frankenstein rabbit viral panic is not lost on the attentive media observer. In August 2025, social media users were simultaneously encountering AI-generated rabbits framed as real (the trampoline video) and real rabbits framed as alarming monsters (the Shope papillomavirus story). Both cases involved the same class of epistemic failure — insufficient context for evaluating the relationship between visual content and reality — applied in opposite directions. Real things were made to seem alarming; fake things were made to seem real. The media literacy challenge they jointly illustrate is the same: developing and distributing the contextual knowledge and analytic skills needed to navigate a media environment where appearances are increasingly unreliable guides to the underlying facts.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates this claim as **Fake**, providing clear and specific visual evidence for the determination. The analysis by Laerke Christensen (published August 2, 2025) identifies two specific moments in the video — at 0:05 and 0:07 — where AI-generation artifacts are visible: a rabbit disappearing instantaneously while another rabbit is in midair, and a rabbit changing shape and color during its jump before landing as an incoherent blob.

The sourcing in the Snopes analysis is appropriately limited to directly verifiable primary sources: the original TikTok post, the Barstool Sports repost, documentation of the account's prior Veo-watermarked video, and Ring's TikTok presence. This reflects the nature of the evidence being evaluated — the claim is assessed through direct video analysis and account provenance investigation, not through external scientific literature.

The treatment is straightforward and appropriately concise for the specific claim. The video is fake and the evidence for fakery is concrete and identifiable. The Fake verdict is clearly correct and the evidentiary basis is transparently presented.

What the format does not capture — and need not capture for the claim being evaluated — is the broader epistemological context: the proliferation of increasingly convincing AI video content, the structural inadequacy of current platform detection and labeling systems, the arms race dynamics between generation and detection, and the public literacy gap that enables benign fakes to achieve massive organic reach before any corrective mechanism activates. These are themes beyond the scope of a claim-specific fact-check but central to understanding why fact-checks of this type will proliferate in the years ahead.

The trampoline rabbit video is a small case — no elections were influenced, no policies were shaped, no health decisions were affected. What it represents structurally is the normal state of affairs in a media environment where AI video generation has become accessible, where synthetic content disclosure is voluntary and easily circumvented, and where platform architecture rewards compelling content regardless of provenance. Small cases like this are the ordinary texture of the information environment that the large, politically consequential synthetic media cases will inhabit.
