## The Claim

In July 2025, a post on X (formerly Twitter) by user @paularambles attracted 4.6 million views with the claim: "fyi if you like listening to 'rain sounds' they're almost all recordings of chicken being fried" (Winter, 2025, Snopes.com). The post framed as a disclosure what had previously circulated more quietly: that the soothing atmospheric rain sounds people listen to for sleep, relaxation, ASMR, and ambient background are, in reality, the sound of frying poultry. The implication was that audiences had been systematically deceived — or at least unknowingly exposed to a culinary process when they believed they were experiencing weather.

The claim was far from new in 2025. TikTok videos making the same assertion had circulated in 2024 and achieved significant reach. In October 2024, the claim had become culturally prominent enough to attract a brand partnership: KFC collaborated with Hatch, a sleep wellness company whose devices play white noise and ambient sounds, to release a "Kentucky Fried Chicken Rain" content channel for their sleep devices (Winter, 2025, Snopes.com). This corporate move treated the claim as established fact — good enough for a fast food brand to stake marketing content on it.

The specific claim under examination, that rain sounds in movies and audio recordings derive from frying food, sits at the intersection of two distinct contexts: professional film sound design (Foley and sound editing) and the consumer ASMR and sleep audio market. Each context has a somewhat different answer to the question. And the aggregate answer to "almost all" is: no, but the underlying phenomenon is real.

## What's Actually True

Frying food has been used as a source material for creating rain sound effects in film contexts. This is documented and not in question. A 2015 Guardian article by Jordan Kisner, reporting on a film sound designer's methods, described the sound of rain in movies as coming from frying bacon (Kisner, 2015, The Guardian). One Foley artist posted a TikTok video in 2024 demonstrating exactly this technique, creating a convincing rain sound by cooking bacon (Winter, 2025, Snopes.com). The acoustic similarity between sizzling fat and falling rain is genuine: both produce a broadband, relatively continuous, somewhat stochastic sound with a similar spectral profile in the mid-frequency range — the kind of sound that the human auditory system processes as ambient rushing or hissing texture.

The technique has a logical basis. Foley artists — professionals who recreate diegetic sound effects (footsteps, clothing rustles, ambient textures) in post-production — use whatever materials produce the required acoustic result. The standard is sonic verisimilitude, not raw material authenticity. If frying bacon produces a waveform that an audience accepts as rainfall when integrated with visual rainfall on screen, it serves its purpose. Gary Gottlieb, president of the Audio Engineering Society, confirmed to Snopes that rain sounds can indeed be created using "other white noise rich sounds like frying" as one approach among several (Gottlieb, quoted in Winter, 2025, Snopes.com).

Crucially, however, neither Gottlieb nor Foley artist Alyson Moore — who has over four decades of experience in the field — described frying food as the dominant or universal approach. Moore stated: "There are specific 'rain' microphones that are designed to record rain in a storm. Editors use actual recorded rain for rain sounds" (Moore, quoted in Winter, 2025, Snopes.com). The professional audio company Pro Sound Effects maintains a dedicated "Rain" sound library created by Oscar-winning sound designer Mark Mangini through extensive field recording of actual rainfall under various conditions, with documented methodology and photographic evidence of the recording process (Winter, 2025, Snopes.com). This library — explicitly for use in professional film, television, and game audio contexts — is made from real rain.

Beyond field recording and frying, Foley artists use numerous other techniques. Moore herself, when creating the specific sound of rain hitting an umbrella or window, uses a watering can; a video she shared shows her and colleagues recreating rain hitting a felt hat by sprinkling water from a watering can onto a boot (Winter, 2025, Snopes.com). Students at Pomfret School in Connecticut recreated a rainstorm using bubble wrap (Winter, 2025, Snopes.com). The animated film "The Boy and the World" used the sound of hands clapping with reverb as one of its rain sounds (Winter, 2025, Snopes.com). Modern Foley practice also frequently draws on commercially licensed sound effects libraries or synthesiser patches rather than live Foley material.

In the ASMR and sleep audio consumer market specifically, the claim holds even less well. One of the most-viewed ASMR rain content creators on YouTube — whose main rain video has been watched over 18 million times — posted a separate video series documenting the locations and equipment used to record the sounds in his videos, showing himself outdoors recording real rain falling over a lake (Winter, 2025, Snopes.com). Hatch, the sleep wellness company that partnered with KFC to create a "Kentucky Fried Chicken Rain" channel, confirmed to Snopes that none of its current sleep sounds are made from frying food of any kind (Winter, 2025, Snopes.com).

## Why People Believe This

The frying-food-as-rain-sound claim has several properties that make it particularly effective at spreading and persisting, even though it overstates the universality of a real but partial practice.

**The structure of the "you've been fooled" claim.** Information formatted as the revelation of a hidden truth has predictable viral properties. The claim does not say "some rain sounds in some movies are made by frying food" — it says "almost all recordings of chicken being fried." This universalising formulation turns a genuine but partial fact into an apparent gotcha: everything you thought was atmospheric rain was actually processed meat. The emotional arc of this reveal — from innocent enjoyment of ambient sound to the slightly disorienting awareness that the experience was mediated in an unexpected way — is pleasurable to share. It gives the sharer a sense of knowing something others don't, and it reframes a commonplace experience in a mildly unsettling way without causing genuine harm.

**Acoustic plausibility reinforces the claim.** The claim is not arbitrary. There is a genuine acoustic similarity between the sound of fat sizzling in a pan and the sound of rain falling on various surfaces. Both produce a continuous, mid-frequency, noisy audio signal with a stochastic texture. Anyone who has heard frying food and then been told it sounds like rain will likely agree: yes, the similarity is real. This acoustic plausibility makes the claim feel true independently of whether the technique is universal, because the plausibility suggests a mechanism that could in principle explain widespread usage. The plausibility of the specific claim ("frying sounds like rain, therefore frying is used for rain sounds") reinforces the implausible generalisation ("therefore almost all rain sounds are frying").

**Epistemic novelty: the acoustic texture convergence problem.** The deeper and less often noted issue in this story is the problem of acoustic texture convergence — the fact that many physically distinct processes generate acoustically similar outputs at the level of perceptual processing. Sound propagation is lossy and context-dependent: a microphone captures mechanical vibrations that have been altered by the medium, the distance, the recording equipment, and the post-processing chain before they reach a listener. The result is that acoustically distinct physical phenomena can become perceptually indistinguishable after recording, while the same physical phenomenon can sound very different under different recording conditions. This is why "what did they record to make this sound?" is often a less meaningful question than it appears. Moore's observation that "the sound of rain is not actually the sound of rain — it is the wind and the sound of droplets hitting something — a window pane, a stone, a puddle" (Moore, quoted in Gottlieb's paraphrase, Winter, 2025, Snopes.com) captures this: even "real" rain sounds are not homogeneous; they are the acoustic product of a specific rain hitting a specific surface under specific conditions. The frying-food technique is one approach to generating within the perceptual window of "rain-like sound"; field recording is another; synthesiser patches are a third. All can produce outputs that a listener cannot distinguish from "real rain."

**KFC as accidental endorser.** The KFC/Hatch collaboration in October 2024 functioned as inadvertent authority amplification for the claim. Fast food companies are not neutral parties, but when a major brand publicly bases a marketing stunt on a factual premise, many people treat this as implicit confirmation of the premise. The KFC campaign assumed the claim was widely believed and leveraged that belief — without verifying whether it was accurate — with predictable effect on the claim's subsequent spread.

## Verdict

The claim that rain sounds in movies are "almost all recordings of chicken being fried" is an overstatement of a genuine but partial phenomenon, and Snopes treated it as a news analysis piece rather than a binary fact-check. Frying food — particularly bacon rather than chicken, according to audio professionals — is one among several techniques used by Foley artists and sound designers to create rain sound effects. The technique is real, documented, and acoustically explicable. However, the universal formulation ("almost all") is false:

- Professional Foley artists also use real recordings made in the field.
- Commercial sound libraries for film and television use purpose-recorded rainfall.
- Contemporary sound design also uses synthesiser patches and sound effects libraries.
- Many ASMR and consumer sleep audio recordings are made from real rain in real outdoor locations.
- Hatch, the company most directly implicated by the KFC partnership, confirmed its sleep sounds are not made from food sources.

Gary Gottlieb of the Audio Engineering Society confirmed frying as one valid approach; Foley artist Alyson Moore described actual rain recording as standard practice; sound designer Mark Mangini documented a rain sound library made entirely from field recordings (all cited in Winter, 2025, Snopes.com). The truth is pluralistic: professional sound design employs whatever works, and many things work.

## The Wider Picture

The frying-food rain claim connects to several important and under-discussed topics: the epistemology of film sound, the history and practice of Foley work, the acoustic properties of broadband noise, and the way viral claims about media production mix genuine insight with overgeneralisation.

**The Foley craft tradition.** Foley sound design is one of the least visible and most consequential crafts in film and television production. The term "Foley" derives from Jack Foley, a sound effects artist at Universal Studios in the late 1920s and 1930s who pioneered synchronised, live sound effect performance as a practical response to the technical limitations of early optical sound recording (Wiener, 2022, The New Yorker). Early microphones were limited and stationary; capturing the sounds of actors moving, touching objects, and interacting with environments was often technically impossible in production. Foley artists solved this by performing sounds in post-production in sync with the image. The craft developed a toolkit of unconventional materials — coconut shells for horse hooves, recording studios filled with gravel pits, sandboxes, and wooden floors for different footstep textures.

The frying-food technique for rain fits within this tradition of pragmatic material substitution. Foley artists have no canonical list of "approved" materials for any given sound effect; what matters is the outcome, not the process. A brief survey of historical Foley techniques illustrates the range: car engines performing as lions' roars (Kisner, 2015, The Guardian), cellophane being crumpled to simulate fire, tearing a wet phone book for the sound of breaking bones in action films. The acoustic world of cinema is one of ingenious material repurposing.

**What makes a sound "right."** The criterion for a Foley sound's success is its perceptual plausibility in context — whether audiences accept it as the sound they expect to hear given what they are seeing on screen. This is a property of the human auditory system's contextual interpretation, not of the physical properties of the sound source. Research on audiovisual integration and perceptual binding by Shams and Beierholm (2010, Trends in Cognitive Sciences) established that what we hear is substantially influenced by what we see: when visual and auditory information are presented simultaneously, the brain integrates them into a unified percept rather than evaluating them independently. This means a sound that might not be identifiable as "rain" in isolation can be convincingly perceived as rain when paired with visual images of rainfall, because the visual context shapes auditory interpretation. Frying food sounds like rain in a film because the visual context supplies the "rain" label for a sound that falls within the acceptable perceptual range.

**The ASMR listening context.** The viral claim's focus on ASMR and sleep audio is interesting because this is a consumer context where the visual rain frame is usually absent. People listening to rain sounds on sleep devices or in ASMR audio have no visual rainfall to provide the contextualising frame. In this context, the relevant criterion is whether the audio produces the physiological and psychological responses associated with calm sleep-inducing listening, not whether it looks like rain on screen. The autonomic nervous system response to white-noise-like ambient sounds — reduced arousal, quieted orienting response — is triggered by the acoustic properties of the sound, not its origin. Whether frying food, real rain, or a synthesiser patch produces these properties is a question of spectral and temporal acoustic structure, and different sources can meet the criterion with equal effectiveness. The Hatch confirmation that their sleep sounds are not made from food (Winter, 2025, Snopes.com) matters not because frying-food sounds would be less effective for sleep but because the factual claim needed verification.

**The popularisation of behind-the-scenes knowledge.** The frying-food claim represents one instance of a broader genre of "hidden media production facts" that circulates on social media: things film crews do, tricks advertisers use, techniques photographers employ. Some of these claims are accurate; many are partially accurate; some are fabricated. The reliable identifying feature of this genre is its framing as revelation — the audience is positioned as having been deceived by media professionals and now being given inside access. This framing is emotionally effective because it offers a sense of demystification and media literacy, which are generally valued. The problem is that genuine media production knowledge is usually more nuanced than social media reveal formats allow. "Rain sounds are sometimes made with frying food by some sound designers in some contexts" is accurate but does not generate the same engagement as "almost all rain sounds are frying chicken." The accurate version undermines the reveal dynamic; the overclaimed version preserves it.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes article by Emery Winter (Winter, 2025, Snopes.com) — notably published in the "News" rather than "Fact Checks" section, indicating it is being treated as contextual journalism rather than a binary verdict — is a good example of responsible handling of a claim that resists simple True/False categorisation.

**Multi-source expert consultation.** Winter contacted two subject-matter experts directly: Gary Gottlieb, president of the Audio Engineering Society, providing industry-level perspective, and Alyson Moore, a Foley artist with over forty years of experience, providing practitioner-level perspective. Both sources confirmed frying food as a valid technique but neither endorsed the universal claim. The divergence between them — one more theory-oriented, one more practice-oriented — adds textural accuracy by showing that even within the professional community, the answer is pluralistic. Good fact-checking on claims about professional practice requires practitioner consultation rather than relying solely on secondary sources.

**Distinguishing contexts.** The article's treatment of ASMR rain sounds as a separate context from film Foley sounds is analytically important. The viral claim made no such distinction, but the mechanisms, professionals, and practices are substantially different in these two domains. Field recorders who create consumer sleep audio have different professional orientations and different relationships to their source material than Hollywood Foley artists. By examining both contexts, the article provides a more complete picture than the claim's framers intended.

**The Hatch confirmation is key.** Perhaps the single most important piece of evidence in the article is the Hatch spokesperson's confirmation that their sleep sounds are not made from food sources. Given that KFC's partnership with Hatch was the highest-profile institutional endorsement of the frying-food claim, having Hatch directly contradict the specific suggestion about their own products is significant. It demonstrates that the brand partnership was exploiting the claim's cultural currency without the claim being true of the company's own products.

**The limits of the news format.** Because the article is framed as a news explainer rather than a formal fact-check, it does not reach a formal verdict. This is appropriate given the complexity of the evidence — the claim is partly true, context-dependent, and not amenable to binary verdicting. The trade-off is that casual readers may leave without a clear orientation to the claim's accuracy. The explainer format serves analytical completeness at some cost to directive clarity.
