## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In July 2014, a composite image showing clusters of grapes in brilliant multi-colored varieties — reds, blues, greens, yellows, oranges, and purples all appearing simultaneously on single bunches — circulated widely across Facebook and later other platforms. The question driving its viral spread was simple and genuinely curious: do "rainbow grapes" exist as a real variety? The image appeared to show grapes rendered in every color of the visible spectrum, arranged in tight clusters suggesting they had grown that way naturally. Some versions of the post encouraged viewers to click through links to purchase "rainbow grape seeds" from online vendors, suggesting the image was being deployed commercially as well as for curiosity's sake.

Snopes investigator David Mikkelson (2014) assigned this claim the rating **Mostly False**. His analysis established a nuanced layered verdict: two of the four quadrant images in the most widely circulated composite were unambiguous digital artwork created for an online competition, one or two were genuine photographs of real grapes in a transitional growth phase called *veraison*, and the combined impression of "rainbow grapes as a stable mature variety" is false. No commercially available grape cultivar produces clusters that mature to a stable rainbow of distinct colors. However, the phenomenon of multi-colored grapes is real in a qualified sense — not as a realized mature state but as a transient developmental phase. This two-level answer — real phenomenon, misrepresented permanence — makes the rainbow grapes claim epistemologically interesting and requires careful botanical unpacking to resolve.

This entry examines the digital art origins of the fabricated images, the genuine botanical phenomenon of veraison and its color dynamics, the online seed-fraud ecosystem that exploited the images commercially, and what the rainbow grapes case reveals about the epistemic structure of "beautiful nature" viral imagery on social media platforms.

## §2 Source Dissection: Digital Art, Veraison, and the Composite Image

Mikkelson (2014) traced the origins of the images in the viral composite to two distinct sources, each representing a fundamentally different evidentiary category.

**The digitally altered images.** The top-left and bottom-right quadrant images in the most widely circulated composite were traced by Mikkelson (2014) to the Worth1000 "Color Blind" photo manipulation contest, in which participants were challenged to create "images where the colors are a bit off." The winning and high-ranking entries in such contests frequently involve compelling natural subjects — flowers, fruits, animals — subjected to hue rotation or selective colorization that produces aesthetically striking but factually impossible images. Worth1000 was at the time one of the most prominent competitive digital art platforms on the internet, hosting thousands of participants in monthly Photoshop contests. The rainbow grape entries appear to have been produced around 2010–2012 based on contest metadata cited in Mikkelson's research. These images are therefore not photographs of real grapes; they are digital artworks produced by competition entrants who applied photo manipulation to create an aesthetically appealing fantasy fruit image.

The fact that these images were extracted from an explicitly labeled digital art competition and recirculated as photographs of real fruit is a canonical example of what image misinformation researchers call **decontextualization** — the removal of an image from its original labeling environment and its redeployment in a new context that inverts the image's original meaning. The original Worth1000 participants, who labeled their work as digital art, had no intention of deceiving anyone; the deception arose in the downstream circulation chain.

**The genuine photographs.** The top-right and bottom-left quadrant images were assessed by Mikkelson (2014) as genuine photographs — not digital manipulations — but photographs documenting a specific and transient biological phenomenon. They show grapes in *veraison* (French: *véraison*), the viticulturally significant onset-of-ripening phase in grape development. Veraison is the moment at which grape berries begin their transition from the green, hard, acidic juvenile state to the softer, sweeter, pigmented mature state. During this transition, which occurs over a period of approximately one to three weeks depending on variety and climate, individual berries in a given cluster may be at markedly different stages of color development simultaneously — some remaining green, others transitioning through red, purple, blue, or (in white varieties) yellow and amber tones. The resulting momentary appearance of a bunch with heterogeneous berry colors is real and photographically documentable. Mikkelson (2014) confirmed this interpretation with Trey Busch of Sleight of Hand Cellars, an experienced viticulturalist who verified the veraison explanation.

Crucially, veraison is transient. Once all berries in a cluster complete the ripening process, they converge on the variety's characteristic mature color — uniformly red, uniformly green, uniformly black, etc. The multi-colored appearance is a developmental snapshot, not a stable characteristic of the mature grape. This is why no cultivar produces a "rainbow" bunch at harvest: the phenotypic diversity visible during veraison disappears as ripening synchronizes across the cluster. Selective breeding for asynchronous ripening that would maintain color heterogeneity at maturity has not been commercially pursued, and the genetic basis for such a trait is complex (Castellarin, Matthews, Di Gaspero, & Gambetta, 2007, *BMC Genomics*, Vol. 8, Article 212).

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Developmental Biology of Veraison and Why the "Rainbow" Is a Window, Not a Property

The epistemic novelty in the rainbow grapes case lies in the botanical mechanism that makes genuine veraison photographs both real and misleading simultaneously. Understanding this requires examining what is biochemically happening during veraison and why the color heterogeneity within a cluster is not arbitrary but reflects a specific developmental logic.

Veraison in Vitis vinifera and related species is initiated by plant hormones, particularly abscisic acid (ABA), which triggers the degradation of chlorophyll in berry skins and the synthesis of anthocyanins (the red and blue pigments in dark-fruited varieties) or the accumulation of carotenoids and flavonols (in light-fruited varieties) (Coombe, 1992, *Acta Horticulturae*, Vol. 298). The key feature of veraison relevant to the rainbow grape phenomenon is that it does not begin simultaneously across all berries in a cluster — it initiates from individual berries and propagates across the bunch over days to weeks. The timing of veraison onset within a cluster is governed by both genetic and positional factors: berries closest to the rachis (central stem) and those in more exposed, warmer positions tend to initiate veraison first, while shaded, peripheral berries lag. The result is a developmental wave pattern across the cluster that, at any single moment during the transition period, produces exactly the kind of multi-color heterogeneity shown in the genuine photographs — some berries still green (pre-veraison), others in transition, and early-ripening berries approaching mature color.

This developmental logic means that veraison photographs are not "random" multi-color images — they document a biologically structured state. Research on veraison uniformity has practical commercial importance in viticulture because asynchronous veraison is associated with uneven ripening at harvest, affecting wine quality metrics such as pH, Brix (sugar concentration), and phenolic maturity. Viticulturists actively manage canopy structure, shoot positioning, and irrigation protocols to improve veraison synchrony across clusters (Castellarin et al., 2007, BMC Genomics 8:212; Intrigliolo & Caspari, 2008, *Irrigation Science*, Vol. 26, No. 5). A bunch in advanced veraison thus represents, from a commercial viticulture standpoint, a bunch not yet ready for harvest and potentially problematic from a ripening uniformity perspective — the opposite of the "special variety" framing in the viral posts.

The epistemic novelty, then, is this: the real photographs in the viral composite are documenting a developmental problem (asynchronous ripening), framing it as a desirable special property (rainbow coloring), and implicitly suggesting it represents the mature state of a variety when it actually represents an immature transitional state. This structural inversion — undesirable transience repackaged as novel permanent quality — is a recurring feature of food and nature viral imagery. It exploits the genuine unfamiliarity most consumers have with agricultural production processes; the veraison phenomenon would be immediately recognizable to any experienced viticulturist but is invisible to anyone who only encounters grapes at the point of sale.

The deeper epistemological issue is that the genuine veraison photographs are "true" in a narrow sense (they are undoctored photos of real grapes) while being profoundly misleading in the claim context (they do not show mature rainbow grapes). This illustrates the inadequacy of a simple "real photo vs. fake photo" binary for image-based misinformation: the authenticity of the photograph is insufficient to establish the authenticity of the claim the photograph is being used to support.

## §4 The Seed Fraud Ecosystem: Commercial Exploitation of a Nature Viral

The rainbow grapes images were not merely circulated as curiosities — they were, and continue to be, actively used to market "rainbow grape seeds" through online marketplace platforms. Mikkelson (2014) noted that "unscrupulous online and mail order vendors sell purported 'rainbow grape' seeds" using the deceptive images. This phenomenon places the rainbow grapes case in a broader taxonomy of online botanical seed fraud that has grown substantially in the years since 2014.

The online sale of seeds for "impossible" plants — rainbow roses, black strawberries, blue tomatoes, rainbow cauliflower — constitutes a substantial fraction of the novelty seed market on platforms including eBay, AliExpress, Wish, and Amazon's third-party marketplace. These products are typically inexpensive (often under $5 for a packet), sold by third-party vendors with opaque supply chains, and target consumers who have seen viral images of the supposedly corresponding plants. Consumer complaint analyses from the UK's National Trading Standards team indicate that in a sample of novelty seed purchases, more than 60% did not produce plants resembling the advertised images (National Trading Standards e-Commerce Team, 2021, Annual Enforcement Report). Some vendors ship ordinary grape seeds; others may ship empty packets or unrelated seeds entirely.

The regulatory response to this ecosystem has been fragmented. In the United States, seed sold across state lines is governed by the Federal Seed Act (7 U.S.C. § 1551 et seq.), which prohibits false advertising of seed variety characteristics. However, enforcement against small-volume online vendors is practically minimal. The USDA's Agricultural Marketing Service enforces seed labeling requirements primarily at the level of commercial seed lots, not individual consumer sales. The consequence is a regulatory gap perfectly sized for the rainbow grape seed trade.

The relationship between the viral image circulation and the seed fraud ecosystem is commercially directional: the viral images create latent consumer demand; seed vendors exploit that demand; the fraud persists because enforcement is inadequate and individual losses are small enough that most consumers do not pursue remedies. This is a textbook case of what internet fraud researchers call **affinity fraud attached to virality** — the viral content does not itself commit fraud but creates the audience condition that fraud then harvests.

## §5 The "Beautiful Nature" Virality Template and Its Misinformation Mechanics

The rainbow grapes case instantiates what might be called the **"beautiful nature" virality template** — a category of viral social media content that presents vivid, colorful, or unusual natural images as evidence of nature's hidden wonders, inviting viewers to share to express wonder and delight. This template is distinct from politics misinformation or health scare content in its emotional valence: it triggers awe and curiosity rather than fear or outrage. Because awe and curiosity are generally considered pro-social emotions, the sharing motivation feels virtuous — one is alerting friends and family to something delightful — rather than partisan or anxious.

Research on emotional drivers of misinformation sharing by Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral (2018, *Science*, Vol. 359, No. 6380) found that false news spreads faster and farther than true news on Twitter, and that novelty and emotional arousal (of any valence, including positive awe) are key factors. The rainbow grapes case predates this research but provides a clean illustration: the images were arresting, colorful, and provided a satisfying "secret of nature" narrative hook that required no prior knowledge to appreciate.

The Mostly False verdict accurately captures the composite nature of the claim. Some elements are true (veraison produces multi-colored grapes; these can be photographed). The overarching claim — that rainbow grapes exist as a stable mature variety — is false. The digital images purporting to be photographs of such grapes are fabricated. And the commercial exploitation of the claim (rainbow grape seeds) is fraudulent. This layered structure is typical of what might be called **compound misinformation** — claims in which some true facts are woven into a false conclusion, providing the irreducible 20% of truth that gives the false 80% its stubborn persistence.

## §6 Verdict, Knowledge Gaps, and Downstream Epistemic Effects

Snopes' verdict of **Mostly False** is well-calibrated to the layered structure of the claim. The literal assertion "rainbow grapes exist as a variety" is false. The veraison phenomenon is real and provides the genuine photographic substrate that gives the claim visual plausibility. The digital artworks in the composite are straightforwardly fabricated. The commercial seed products are fraudulent.

The **EARC gap** is assigned **E (Empirical)**: the necessary knowledge to resolve this claim is botanical — specifically, knowledge of veraison biology and viticulture — combined with digital image provenance tracking. Neither is part of ordinary consumer knowledge, and neither is signaled in the viral image itself. The gap is not primarily one of moral or political interpretation but of domain-specific empirical knowledge that most social media users simply do not have and have no practical pathway to acquire without deliberate effort.

Key take-away propositions:
1. Veraison is a real transitional phase in grape development that produces genuine multi-colored clusters; this is a developmental state, not a variety trait.
2. Once grapes complete ripening, berries in a cluster converge on the variety's characteristic single mature color; stable "rainbow" clusters do not exist in commercially cultivated varieties.
3. Two of the four images in the most-circulated composite were digitally created for a photo-manipulation competition and are not photographs of real objects.
4. "Rainbow grape seed" products sold online are fraudulent; no cultivar produces rainbow-colored mature clusters.
5. The epistemic trap here is that genuine photographs (veraison images) are used to authenticate a false claim, making the usual heuristic "if it's a real photo it must be real" fail in an instructive way.

The rainbow grapes case remains perennially relevant because new cohorts of social media users encounter the images for the first time each year, seed fraud vendors continue to operate across multiple platforms, and the combination of real botanical phenomenon plus digital art plus commercial fraud creates a claim structure resistant to simple binary debunking.
