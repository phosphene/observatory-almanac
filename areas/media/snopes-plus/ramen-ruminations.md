## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

An email warning titled "The Correct Way of Cooking Noodles" circulated online from at least 2007, advising recipients that the standard method for preparing instant ramen — cooking the dried noodles and flavor packet together in boiling water for approximately three minutes — was dangerously incorrect. The claim had two distinct sub-components: first, that cooking monosodium glutamate (MSG) along with the noodles would "change the molecular structures of the MSG causing it to be toxic"; and second, that instant ramen noodles are coated in wax, "which would take around 4 to 5 days for the body to excrete" after consumption.

Snopes investigator Barbara Mikkelson (2010) rated this claim **False** on both sub-claims. MSG's molecular structure is not altered by boiling in a way that produces toxic compounds; the "heat changes MSG" claim reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of the chemistry of sodium salts. The wax coating claim is not supported by product examination or manufacturer disclosure; Nissin Foods explicitly denied coating their noodles in wax. This entry examines both sub-claims in detail, situates the email within the broader history of MSG panic, and explores what the pairing of these two false claims reveals about the rhetorical structure of "insider food safety" misinformation.

## §2 MSG Chemistry: What Heat Actually Does

Monosodium glutamate (MSG) is the sodium salt of glutamic acid, an amino acid that is naturally occurring in numerous foods — including tomatoes, Parmesan cheese, soy sauce, mushrooms, and fish sauce — and that serves as a primary ligand for the umami taste receptor (T1R1/T1R3 heterodimer), one of five basic taste receptor types identified in mammalian taste research (Chaudhari et al., 2000, *Nature Neuroscience*). As a food additive, MSG was first isolated and commercialized by Kikunae Ikeda at the Tokyo Imperial University in 1908 from kombu seaweed broth (Ikeda, 1909, *Journal of the Chemical Society of Tokyo*, translated in *Chemical Senses*, 2002).

The email's central chemistry claim — that boiling MSG "changes its molecular structure" to produce a toxic substance — is simply incorrect. MSG as a sodium salt dissociates readily in aqueous solution at standard cooking temperatures: the sodium cation (Na⁺) separates from the glutamate anion (C₅H₈NO₄⁻), and the glutamate exists in its ionized form in solution. This behavior is identical whether the MSG is added before the water boils, during boiling, or after heat is removed. There is no chemical transformation pathway by which the boiling of glutamate in water produces a distinct toxic compound.

The plausibility of the "heating changes molecules" idea rests on a correct general principle applied incorrectly: it is true that some organic compounds undergo harmful chemical transformations under heat. For example, acrylamide forms when reducing sugars and asparagine react in the Maillard reaction at high temperatures (Tareke et al., 2002, *Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry*). Heterocyclic amines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons form at very high temperatures in meat (Sugimura et al., 2004, *Mutation Research*). Nitrosamines form from nitrites under certain conditions. These are real and documented.

But glutamate — and specifically MSG in aqueous solution at 100°C — does not undergo any of these transformations. Glutamic acid is among the most chemically stable of the amino acids; its carboxylate groups are fully ionized in standard cooking conditions and do not participate in the high-temperature condensation or reduction reactions that generate the harmful compounds listed above. Mikkelson (2010) was therefore correct that "boiling or not boiling MSG doesn't change what it is."

The regulatory status of MSG under U.S. law reflects this chemistry. The Food and Drug Administration designates MSG as "Generally Recognized as Safe" (GRAS), with no restriction on heating. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) Panel on Food Additives completed a comprehensive reevaluation in 2017 (EFSA Journal, 2017) and established an Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) of 30 mg/kg body weight per day for the combined intake of free glutamates from all sources — a level substantially above what is delivered by a single packet of instant ramen. Critically, neither the FDA nor EFSA documentation suggests that heating affects MSG safety.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The "Chinese restaurant syndrome" Legacy and MSG Moral Panic

To understand why the "MSG becomes toxic when boiled" myth was plausible to a significant audience, it is necessary to understand the history of MSG stigmatisation in Western consumer culture — a history that reveals how a bogus health claim, once embedded in medical literature, can generate durable popular belief lasting decades.

In 1968, Robert Ho Man Kwok published a letter in the *New England Journal of Medicine* (Kwok, 1968) describing symptoms he experienced after eating at Chinese restaurants in the United States: numbness, general weakness, and heart palpitations. Kwok speculated that possible causes included soy sauce, MSG, and excess sodium. His letter introduced the term "Chinese restaurant syndrome" into medical discourse. This framing, published in one of the world's most prestigious medical journals, gave an appearance of scientific legitimacy to what was essentially an anecdote.

Subsequent controlled research consistently failed to confirm a specific causal link between MSG and the symptoms attributed to "Chinese restaurant syndrome." Randomized, placebo-controlled double-blind studies found that subjects reported symptoms at comparable rates when given MSG and when given placebos, and that neither dose nor timing was consistently associated with symptoms (Lessof, 1992, *Lancet* review; Freeman, 1994, *Journal of the American Academy of Nurse Practitioners*; Tarasoff & Kelly, 1993, *Food and Chemical Toxicology*). A comprehensive review by Geha et al. (2000, *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology*) found that even in individuals who believed they were MSG-sensitive, controlled exposure did not consistently reproduce symptoms.

The persistence of MSG concern despite negative controlled evidence is an extraordinary case study in **nocebo epidemiology** — the generation of symptoms by belief in the harm of a substance rather than by the substance itself. Walker (2007, *Appetite*) documented that self-reported MSG sensitivity correlates with anxiety about food in general rather than with objective MSG intake, and that the ethnic and racial framing of "Chinese restaurant syndrome" contributed to its durability as a cultural anxiety anchored to existing anti-immigration sentiment.

The email's claim that boiling MSG makes it "toxic" operates within this historical context: it recycles an existing cultural anxiety about MSG and adds a spurious mechanism that makes the claim feel more sophisticated. That mechanism — "molecular structure changes" — borrows the language of chemistry without its content, in exactly the same way that "quantum" or "microwave radiation" has been used in other food scares to add scientific-sounding scaffolding to groundless claims.

## §4 The Wax Claim: Examining the Evidence

The second sub-claim — that instant ramen noodles are coated in wax — has a distinct genealogical trajectory, though it similarly represents a misapplication of a real category to a false specific case.

The "ramen noodles contain wax" rumor has circulated in a related but distinct form since at least the 1990s, with a variant warning specifically about styrofoam Cup Noodles supposedly leaching wax from the container. Mikkelson (2010) noted that Snopes debunked this styrofoam-wax claim separately. The "wax on the noodle block" version specifically appears to have originated from visual inspection by non-experts: dried instant ramen noodles do have a somewhat shiny or glossy surface texture, which could be misidentified as a wax coating by someone unfamiliar with the production process.

The actual explanation for instant noodles' surface texture is the production process. Most instant ramen noodles are produced by one of two methods: deep-frying (the more common traditional process, in which noodles are briefly flash-fried in oil to achieve rapid dehydration and a porous structure that rehydrates quickly) or air-drying (the process used in some premium-line products). The glossy appearance that some consumers interpret as wax is the residual oil from the frying process — palm oil or other food oils — not a wax coating (Nissin Foods, consumer affairs response cited by Mikkelson, 2010; Brennan et al., 1991, *International Journal of Food Science and Technology*).

The *New York Daily News* independently examined all components of a Nissin Cup Noodles with Shrimp in 1997 (Beale, 1997) and found no wax in the product or among its ingredients. Nissin Foods' consumer affairs department responded to Mikkelson's direct inquiry: "The norms of having wax coating on our instant ramen is a false statement, at least towards Nissin Foods' products. Hereby, I can reassure you we do not have wax coating towards our noodle blocks." The response also noted, in a charming aside, that Nissin founder Momofuku Ando "lived till 96 by eating ramen on a daily basis" — a biographical counter-evidence that somewhat undermines the wax-toxicity concern.

The subsidiary claim — that wax would take "4 to 5 days for the body to excrete" after consumption — reflects a further misunderstanding of digestive physiology. While wax is not readily digested (wax is primarily composed of long-chain esters and alcohols resistant to saponification by gut lipases), food transit time through the human gastrointestinal tract averages approximately 24–72 hours depending on fiber intake, hydration, and individual variation (Sadik et al., 2006, *Gastroenterology*). Wax components in food (such as carnuba wax used as a glaze on some confectionery, or beeswax used in certain food applications) transit at the same rate as other insoluble materials; there is no documented mechanism by which wax would be selectively retained in the gut for 4–5 days.

To be precise about what wax is: common food-grade waxes include carnuba wax (from the Brazilian carnauba palm, commonly used on confectionery and as a tablet coating), beeswax, rice bran wax, and synthetic food-grade paraffin. These are all approved for specific food uses by the FDA. The wax compounds do traverse the digestive tract without absorption and are excreted with normal fecal transit. The "4–5 days" claim is therefore incorrect on physiological grounds, independent of whether the wax premise itself is false.

## §5 Ramen in Context: History and Global Significance

The email's target — the instant noodle packet — is a food product of extraordinary global cultural and economic significance that warrants brief contextualization.

Instant ramen was invented by Momofuku Ando in 1958 at Nissin Foods, following years of experimentation motivated by post-World War II food shortages in Japan. Ando's flash-frying process created a dehydrated noodle block that could be reconstituted in hot water in under three minutes — a product that was initially received with skepticism by the Japanese food industry (which viewed it as an inferior substitute for fresh ramen) but which became a mass-market phenomenon within years of its launch (Solt, 2014, *The Untold History of Ramen*, University of California Press).

The global reach of instant ramen as of the 2020s is difficult to overstate. The World Instant Noodles Association estimated global consumption at approximately 116.56 billion servings in 2022 — roughly 14 servings per person per year globally, with enormous variation by country. The product has been characterized as one of the most important food innovations of the twentieth century in terms of caloric delivery cost to low-income populations (Bak, 2006, *Society*).

In the United States, ramen's cultural position shifted dramatically between the 1980s (college staple, associated with poverty) and the 2010s (artisan ramen restaurant proliferation, premium instant ramen imports, ramen as an object of food media enthusiasm). This cultural repositioning is documented by Solt (2014) and others as a case study in how food products gain and lose status through social narratives rather than intrinsic properties.

The email's target audience — people who ate instant ramen frequently and might be anxious about their health — was real and large. The email's implicit message ("you've been cooking ramen wrong, and it's been harming you") follows a well-documented template for health-anxiety content that targets low-income or student demographics with messages about the hidden dangers of their affordable food choices. This class dimension of food scare messaging is worth marking: the "hidden danger in cheap food" genre disproportionately targets foods associated with poverty and students, rarely the foods of affluent consumption.

## §6 Verdict Analysis and Epistemic Gap Assessment

**EARC Classification: E (Empirically Refuted)**
The Snopes rating of **False** is well-supported on both sub-claims. The MSG-boiling claim fails at elementary chemistry. The wax claim fails on both product examination and manufacturer disclosure. Neither claim produced any evidentiary replication in the literature or media of the period. This is a clean refutation rather than an underdetermined case.

**Gap Classification: folk-chemistry-myth**
The epistemic gap is between "MSG has been the subject of health concern" (historically documented and partially legitimate given the nocebo literature) and "boiling MSG transforms it into a distinct toxic substance" (false, with no chemical mechanism). A secondary gap exists between "some foods do contain wax glazes" (true: carnuba wax on some confectionery) and "instant ramen noodles are coated in an especially persistent wax" (false on inspection). The email imports a true general claim (some foods contain wax) and falsely applies it to the specific target.

**Broader significance.** The ramen-MSG-wax email is a case study in **retrograde food chemistry** — the genre of health warnings that deploy chemistry vocabulary backwards, from desired conclusion (this food is dangerous) to post-hoc mechanism (something happens when you cook it). The warning is structured so that the reader who knows anything about food chemistry is likely to recognize a real mechanism category (molecular changes from heat; wax in foods) and accept the false specific application uncritically, because the leap from "cooking can change things" to "cooking changes MSG in particular" seems like a small and plausible one.

Understanding this rhetorical strategy is more valuable than debunking any individual email: it prepares readers to notice when a true general principle is being used as a vehicle for a false specific claim.
