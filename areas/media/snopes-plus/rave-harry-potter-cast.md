## §1 — Claim and Verdict Summary

In November 2023, a collection of AI-generated black-and-white photographs went viral claiming to depict the cast and characters of the *Harry Potter* film franchise at a Hogwarts rave in 1996. The images were captioned "The Great Hogwarts Rave of 1996" and featured: Daniel Radcliffe as Harry Potter dancing, Emma Watson as Hermione Granger with a cigarette dangling from her lips, Alan Rickman as Professor Snape screaming while holding a bottle, and both Ralph Fiennes as Voldemort and Michael Gambon as Dumbledore appearing to DJ. The images originated from a Facebook post by user Ed Haas, shared to the public group "Cursed AI" on November 16, 2023.

Snopes fact-checker Taija PerryCook investigated the claim and published a verdict of **Fake** on December 9, 2023. The verdict rests on two independent grounds:

1. The images were shared to a Facebook group explicitly dedicated to AI-generated content ("Cursed AI"), indicating the posting context was labeled.
2. The captioned premise is chronologically impossible: the images claim to depict events from 1996, yet Daniel Radcliffe—who played Harry Potter—was born in 1989 and would have been only seven years old in 1996. Furthermore, the *Harry Potter* films were not produced until 2001–2011; the cast members playing these characters were not yet associated with these roles in 1996.

The images achieved massive viral spread—20,000 reactions, 12,000 comments, and 42,000 shares on the original Facebook post; a crosspost to Instagram (accounts @moistbuddha and @shitheadsteve) reached 710,829+ likes before the fact-check was published.

---

## §2 — Origin and Circulation: The Cursed AI Ecosystem

The origin point of the viral spread was a specific Facebook community: "Cursed AI" (full name: Cursed AI WTF), a public group dedicated to sharing AI-generated images that are strange, uncanny, or deliberately absurd. The group functions as a creative space for generative AI imagery, and the posting context explicitly frames all content as artificial. Ed Haas, the account that shared the images, also crossposted to Instagram under the handle @moistbuddha, and the content was co-shared with @shitheadsteve, an Instagram account with 7.3 million followers.

This trajectory illustrates the crucial role of *platform topology* in viral AI-image spread. The Cursed AI group is labeled; its members understand the AI artificiality by participation norm. But Instagram resharings to general-audience accounts with millions of followers stripped away this context: the 7.3-million-follower @shitheadsteve crosspost reached audiences whose primary relationship to the content was entertainment, not AI-appreciation literacy. For those audiences—who may have received the images as a scroll-past item in a general humor feed—the AI production origin was not foregrounded.

The ScreenRant piece published on November 21 (Northrup, 2023) covered the images as entertainment news under the headline "Voldemort DJs The Great Hogwarts Rave In Harry Potter Art," using the term "art" rather than "photograph" or "authentic image." Trade entertainment coverage thus added an intermediary framing that acknowledged the artificial nature while also amplifying reach—a form of coverage that increases visibility without decisively resolving the authenticity question for all readers.

The December 9 timing of the Snopes debunking is significant: it arrived approximately three weeks after the initial viral explosion, well after the images had achieved their maximum organic reach. This lag is structural; fact-checking infrastructure operates at publication speeds that are systematically slower than viral content propagation.

## §3 — Epistemic Analysis: The Temporal Impossibility Heuristic and Its Limits

The Snopes debunking identifies two independent invalidating conditions for the claim, and they operate at different levels of epistemic accessibility. The *expert-knowledge condition* (the AI labeling of the originating group) requires knowledge of Facebook group contexts that many secondary viewers lack. The *public-knowledge condition* (Daniel Radcliffe's age in 1996) is more broadly checkable: Radcliffe's birth year (1989) is public knowledge, and simple arithmetic yields his age of seven in 1996. The fact that he appears to be filmed as an adult in the images creates a contradiction visible to anyone who performs the calculation.

This is an instance of what might be termed the *temporal verifiability advantage* in AI-image detection: when AI-generated content makes specific claims about historical timing, and those claims are contradicted by easily verifiable facts about depicted persons or events, the falsity is publicly detectable without specialized forensic tools. The "1996" caption is the Achilles' heel of these images: no amount of visual quality improvement could make a photograph from 1996 accurately depict adult Daniel Radcliffe in character as a screen version of Harry Potter that did not exist until 2001.

The epistemically novel aspect of this case is what it reveals about the *nostalgia-AI synthesis genre*. The images exploit two distinct psychological effects simultaneously: the nostalgia value of the *Harry Potter* universe (one of the most globally recognized fictional franchises, with extensive parasocial attachment among its audience) and the aesthetic conventions of 1990s rave photography—grainy, black-and-white, documentary in feel. The combination creates a specific "this *feels* real in the way archival photos feel real" response that may short-circuit analytical checking. Frenda, Knowles, Saletan, and Loftus (2013, *Psychological Science*, 24[11], 2181–2188) documented that exposure to fake event photographs reliably increased false memories of those events in a substantial proportion of subjects. The AI-generated rave photographs, presented in the aesthetic register of authentic archival imagery, may produce analogous false-memory susceptibility in *Harry Potter* fans with high parasocial investment in the franchise's history.

## §4 — The Chronological Impossibility in Detail

The claim's core impossibility is worth examining in structural depth, because it reveals how AI image generation disentangles visual plausibility from factual coherence. The generative model that produced these images was trained to produce images resembling 1990s rave photography. It was apparently prompted with descriptions of *Harry Potter* characters and film cast members. It produced visually convincing results within the aesthetic norms of the period. But it had no mechanism to enforce factual coherence between "1996" and "Daniel Radcliffe as adult Harry Potter"—because factual coherence is not a constraint that generative image models optimize for. They optimize for visual plausibility within the trained distribution.

The "Harry Potter" book series began publication in 1997 (Rowling, 1997, *Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone*, Bloomsbury), one year after the captioned rave date. The film series ran from 2001 (*Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone*, dir. Columbus, Warner Bros.) through 2011 (*Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows – Part 2*, dir. Yates, Warner Bros.). In 1996, Daniel Radcliffe was seven years old. Emma Watson was six. Rupert Grint was eight. Alan Rickman (Snape) and Ralph Fiennes (Voldemort) were alive and active as actors in 1996, but their associations with these roles did not exist. Michael Gambon (Dumbledore in films 3–8, following Richard Harris's death) had no Harry Potter association in 1996 whatsoever.

The caption's specificity—"1996" rather than a vaguer claim—may paradoxically increase its persuasive power with readers who do not perform the verification. Specific dates connote documentary authenticity: archival photographs rarely claim "sometime in the 1990s." The precision of "1996" signals that someone *knows* when this occurred, lending false evidential weight. This is a technique borrowed from historical fabrication traditions long predating AI (Grafton, 1990, *Forgers and Critics: Creativity and Duplicity in Western Scholarship*, Princeton University Press).

## §5 — Generative AI Aesthetics: The Revival of Archival Realism

The choice to generate these images in black-and-white with grain and documentary aesthetic conventions is a deliberate or at least consequential choice that significantly affects their reception. Black-and-white photography, combined with grain texture and candid framing conventions inherited from photojournalism and documentary traditions, carries strong authenticity connotations in contemporary visual culture. Barthes (1980, *La Chambre Claire*, Éditions de l'Étoile) theorized that photography's indexical nature—the sense that light from a real scene causally imprinted the film—was constitutive of its rhetorical power. Digital photography disrupted indexicality, but the aesthetic conventions of analog photography retained their authenticity associations even as the underlying technology changed.

AI-generated images that adopt these aesthetic conventions exploit a residual authenticity heuristic: viewers trained to associate documentary grain with "this really happened" apply that heuristic even when the underlying generative process has no indexical relationship to any real scene. The "rave photographs" are doubly fake—not merely altered authentic photographs but images generated entirely from noise, shaped by statistical patterns trained on real images. Their relationship to reality is statistical rather than indexical. But for viewers applying authenticity heuristics based on visual style, they *feel* real in the way that old photographs *feel* real.

The proliferation of AI-generated imagery in archival registers—photographs, newspapers, historical documents—represents a systematic attack on the cognitive heuristics that humans have relied on to assess visual authenticity for over 150 years. The "rave photographs" are an early and relatively benign example. The same generative capacity applied to politically consequential historical events—elections, conflicts, demonstrations—raises substantially higher stakes harms. The benign-but-viral cases like the Hogwarts rave may be valuable precisely as accessible examples for illustrating the problem to general audiences.

---

## §6 — Synthesis and Conclusion

The "Great Hogwarts Rave of 1996" images are **AI-generated fakes**, produced within an AI-labeled creative community and subsequently widely circulated without that context. The claim's factual impossibility is detectable through basic calendar arithmetic: Daniel Radcliffe was seven years old in 1996; the *Harry Potter* films were produced 2001–2011; no cast-character associations existed in 1996. The images achieved extraordinary viral spread—peaking at over 710,000 Instagram likes—before fact-checking published a verdict.

The epistemic significance of this case lies in its illustration of the nostalgia-AI synthesis genre: AI imagery in archival aesthetic registers exploits authenticity heuristics developed for real documentary photography, while targeting parasocially engaged fandoms with high emotional investment in the franchise's history. Temporal impossibility arguments are publicly accessible but must be actively performed by readers; the prevalence of passive consumption means that many are never executed.

The creative community framing (Cursed AI, @moistbuddha, @shitheadsteve humor account) positions these images as entertainment rather than disinformation. But the distinction between labeled AI entertainment and unlabeled misinformation collapses at the level of the individual scroll-past recipient who receives only visual content without framing. This collapse is structural to the social media ecosystem and will not be resolved by creator-level labeling alone.

**Snopes Verdict: Fake.** The images are AI-generated and not authentic photographs; the depicted scenario is chronologically impossible.
