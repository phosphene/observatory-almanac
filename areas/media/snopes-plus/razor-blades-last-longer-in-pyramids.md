# Storing razor blades inside a pyramid shape keeps them sharp


## 1. The Claim

The belief has a specific name in the literature of twentieth-century pseudoscience: pyramid power. In its most focused application to everyday household objects, the claim holds that a razor blade stored inside or beneath a pyramid-shaped structure — typically a cardboard or plastic replica scaled to the proportions of the Great Pyramid of Giza and aligned to the cardinal compass directions — will either be restored to sharpness after dulling or will maintain its sharpness substantially longer than a blade stored in any other configuration.

The claim is more specific than the general register of pyramid mysticism. It proposes a mechanism of a kind: something about the geometric shape, the specific proportions, and in most accounts the precise compass alignment of the pyramid causes an effect on fine-edged metal that prevents or reverses the microcrystalline degradation that dulls a blade through use. Some proponents have framed this in terms of unknown energy fields focused by the pyramid's geometry; others have appealed to cosmic rays or Earth's magnetic field; still others have speculated about desiccation effects on the crystalline structure of the steel edge. The claim was most prominently promoted during the 1970s paranormal literature boom, appearing in bestselling books including *Psychic Discoveries Behind the Iron Curtain* (Ostrander & Schroeder, 1970) and the more popular *Pyramid Power* (Flanagan, 1973, DeVorss & Company).

The specificity of the applica­tion — razor blades, specific proportions, compass alignment — and the apparent testability of the claim (you could verify it yourself with a cardboard pyramid and a disposable razor) gave it a quality of empirical seriousness that elevated it above purely astrological or energy-healing claims. Its inventor, the Czech radio engineer and inventor Karel Drbal, had pursued the claim through the formal mechanism of a patent application. The story of a bizarre patent granted by a rigorous European patent office to a device for sharpening razors with pyramid geometry became a centerpiece of the claim's appeal: surely, the argument ran, the Czechoslovak patent board would not have granted the patent if there were nothing to it.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The patent and its origins.** Karel Drbal (1907–1982), a Czech radio and electronics engineer, patented his "Cheops Pyramid Razor Blade Sharpener" — formally *Faraon's holítko* — in 1959. Soviet-era Czechoslovakia, 1959, Patent No. 91304. The patent application described a cardboard structure proportioned to match the Great Pyramid of Giza, oriented with one face parallel to Earth's magnetic meridian, apparently capable of restoring the sharpness of used razor blades. Drbal reported that he had successfully used the device himself and observed the effect.

The patent's existence, frequently cited as a form of institutional validation, is largely illusory as evidence. Patent examination bodies — including those operating under Soviet-era Czechoslovak institutional structures — assess novelty and industrial applicability, not empirical truth. A patent establishes that a claim is novel and potentially useful; it does not establish that the underlying scientific claim is correct. The Czechoslovak patent board's eventual approval of Drbal's application is not strong evidence that pyramid geometry affects crystalline blade structure. It is evidence that the patent examiner was eventually satisfied that the device was described precisely enough to be granted protection, which is a filing procedure, not a scientific experiment. As Coker (1990, *Science and the Paranormal*, Prometheus Books) notes, numerous patents in the twentieth century were granted for devices with no plausible mechanism; patent examination is not scientific peer review.

**Blade sharpness and the mechanisms of dulling.** A razor blade loses sharpness primarily through corrosion and mechanical deformation of the blade edge, not through a process that has any obvious relationship to ambient geometry or magnetic field orientation.

The cutting edge of a modern razor blade is a wedge of steel typically thinned to approximately ten microns at the apex — a dimension visible only by electron microscopy. When you shave, this apex deforms through contact with whisker shafts (which are surprisingly tough in cross-section: a human whisker has similar mechanical resistance per unit area to copper wire of the same diameter), and micro-notches accumulate. The secondary degradation mechanism is corrosion: the steel edge begins oxidising between uses, and the oxide layer is structurally different from the parent metal, altering the edge geometry further. Hruza & Böhm (2015, *Journal of Materials Science*) used scanning electron microscopy to directly image blade-edge microstructure before and after shaving, confirming that mechanical deformation of the blade apex accounts for the primary sharpness loss, with secondary corrosive contributions dependent on moisture and oxygen exposure.

**There is no mechanism for pyramid geometry to act on blade steel.** Proposed mechanisms in the pyramid-power literature include: alignment with Earth's magnetic field, focusing of cosmic rays, concentration of "orgone energy" or related constructs, and a crystallographic annealing effect driven by the pyramid geometry. None of these withstands scrutiny.

The claim that blade steel could be magnetically restored by compass-aligned pyramid orientation has been examined and dismissed. Steel blade sharpness is a function of microcrystalline grain structure and physical edge geometry; Earth's magnetic field is far too weak to produce thermodynamic reorganisation of crystalline steel at room temperature. The energy required to produce the described annealing or edge-restoration effect — essentially reversing mechanical deformation of metal at the micron scale — would greatly exceed the energy conversions achievable by any field or radiation in ordinary room conditions.

The cosmic ray hypothesis, which received some attention in the 1970s popular literature, proposes that the pyramid shape focuses cosmic rays (primarily muons at sea level) toward the blade edge, causing a restorative interaction. This is not on its face insane as a hypothesis — muons do penetrate matter, and focused flux might in principle affect material structure. However, the focusing power of a cardboard pyramid on a beam of isotropically-incident cosmic radiation is essentially zero; cosmic ray trajectories are not refracted by cardboard surfaces, and the internal geometry of a hollow pyramid places no special constraint on the distribution of muons inside it.

The desiccation hypothesis is marginally more interesting and has sometimes been cited as explaining a real but misinterpreted phenomenon (discussed in §3 below).

**Controlled experiments have found no effect.** The razor-blade claims generated enough interest in the 1970s to produce several informal and at least a few more rigorous attempts at experimental verification. Randi (1982, *Flim-Flam!*, Prometheus Books) discusses failed replications. The Committee for the Scientific Investigation of Claims of the Paranormal (CSICOP, later CSI) commissioned tests in the late 1970s that found no statistically significant difference in blade longevity under pyramid versus control storage conditions. These experiments were imperfect by modern standards, but the absence of any positive replication across multiple independent tests, combined with the complete absence of a plausible physical mechanism, provides the accumulative case for rejection.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The razor blade pyramid claim occupies an interesting position in the sociology of paranormal belief because it was supposed to be *especially testable* — you could try it yourself at home, with readily available materials, in a matter of weeks. This testability created the impression of a particularly secure form of paranormal evidence: not a healer's testimony or a medium's seance, but an ordinary object you could observe for yourself.

**Confirmation bias in informal self-testing.** The home experiment structure of the pyramid blade claim is almost perfectly configured to produce false positives through confirmation bias. When a person constructs a pyramid, places a used razor inside it, and checks after some days whether the blade feels sharper, they are conducting an informal experiment with several key confounds uncontrolled.

First, blade sharpness assessment is highly subjective when performed on one's own face; expectations substantially affect perception of sharpness, and a blade that one believes has been "treated" will reliably be judged as sharper than a blade one believes is untreated, particularly at the margins of detectable difference. Kahneman (2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Farrar, Straus and Giroux) discusses the general mechanism by which prior expectations shape sensory perception in conditions of ambiguity.

Second, random variation in blade-to-whisker interaction between uses generates natural variation in shaving experience that is easy to misattribute. A shave that was better than the last might be due to better skin hydration, slightly different shaving pressure, or a different shaving cream — none of which the informal tester is controlling.

Third, the desiccation confound. Razor blades stored inside any enclosed small-volume structure — pyramid-shaped or otherwise — are partially protected from ambient humidity. Since blade corrosion is moisture-accelerated, any storage configuration that reduces exposed moisture will extend blade life slightly compared to leaving a blade on a wet bathroom vanity surface. The pyramid believer who stores their blade carefully inside a cardboard pyramid and finds it lasts longer than a blade left on the bathroom sink may have observed a real effect — but the relevant variable is desiccation, not pyramidal geometry.

**The epistemic novelty in §3: the replication trap.** The specifically interesting epistemological feature of the pyramid blade claim is what can be called the *replication trap*: a claim is structured in a way that makes genuine self-testing feel available but makes the test genuinely insensitive to the hypothesis being tested. The pyramid blade test appears to be a clean experiment — blade in pyramid, blade not in pyramid, compare — but in its informal version it lacks all the controls that would actually distinguish the pyramidal geometry hypothesis from the confounds described above: blinding, standardised humidity, standardised shaving torque, standardised blade provenance, and mechanical edge measurement rather than subjective sensation. The appearance of testability without the reality of testability creates a particularly durable belief state, because the believer has confirmed the claim through personal experience.

This pattern — apparently self-testable claims that systematically produce false positives through uncontrolled confounds — appears throughout the paranormal literature. Ideomotor effects in dowsing, expectancy in alternative therapy, and regression to the mean in homeopathy all exploit a similar structure. The pyramid blade case is a clean example because the plausible physical mechanism that could explain a real effect (desiccation) is entirely distinct from the proposed mechanism (pyramidal energy field), and the two are indistinguishable in the informal test.

**The authority anchor.** Beyond home testing, the credibility of the patent provided an institutional authority anchor. For many people, the idea that a patent authority had scrutinised the claim and granted protection constituted a form of expert endorsement. This misunderstanding of what patents certify is widespread and represents a general category of authority misattribution: treating procedural or formal approvals as scientific validations. Drbal's patent, in this function, is similar to the way that FDA "generally recognised as safe" status has been misrepresented as scientific endorsement for various supplements, or the way that "peer reviewed" has been used to launder poor-quality studies by appeal to the journal as institution rather than the quality of the specific review.


## 4. Verdict

**False.** There is no credible evidence that pyramid geometry affects razor blade sharpness, and no plausible physical mechanism by which it could do so. Controlled experiments have failed to replicate the claimed effect. The historical origin of the claim lies in one inventor's anecdotal report, subsequently elevated by a patent that certifies novelty but not truth, and popularised by a cohort of 1970s paranormal bestsellers that shared no interest in empirical rigour. The only potentially real confound — desiccation of the blade edge by storage in any enclosed container — explains whatever small effect users may have genuinely observed, and has nothing to do with pyramidal proportions, compass alignment, or any form of "pyramid power."


## 5. The Wider Picture

Pyramid power was one of the more prominent strains in the broader paranormal and alternative-science explosion of the 1970s, a period that also produced widespread belief in psychokinesis, aura photography, Kirlian photography, orgone energy, and various forms of crystal healing. The cultural context in which these claims flourished — post-counterculture scepticism toward scientific establishment, Cold War anxiety about Soviet paranormal research, and a publishing industry hungry for the mass-market paperbacks that parapsychology sold very well — is important for understanding why a claim as specific and apparently testable as the blade-sharpening pyramid found such ready cultural purchase.

Drbal's role in the story is somewhat unusual for paranormal claims. He was an engineer by training, and his original investigations appear to have been conducted with genuine curiosity as informal experiments rather than as promotional vehicles for a commercial product. The commercial dimension arrived via figures like Patrick Flanagan, whose 1973 *Pyramid Power* became an international bestseller and spawned a significant market for pyramid-shaped cardboard kits, acrylic pyramid paperweights, and pyramid tents marketed for meditation. Flanagan's book attributed to pyramid geometry a truly remarkable range of effects: enhanced meditation, improved indoor plant growth, meat preservation without refrigeration, water enhancement, and drug-free psychedelic experiences. The blade sharpening claim was merely the most verifiable and thus most frequently cited subset of a much larger cluster of attributed effects.

The pyramid-power literature also illustrates how Cold War competition could be leveraged to provide apparent institutional credibility for paranormal claims. *Psychic Discoveries Behind the Iron Curtain* — the book that first brought Drbal's patent to wide English-language attention — was framed explicitly as a report on Soviet and Eastern Bloc scientific investigation of paranormal phenomena, and implied that behind the Iron Curtain, state resources were being poured into research that Western scientific establishments were suppressing. This framing was effective: it suggested that pyramid power had Soviet scientific backing, while conveniently placing the authoritative research behind a barrier that Western readers could not easily access or verify.

The claims did not survive the fall of the Iron Curtain and the resulting possibility of actually examining Soviet parapsychology research in detail. The extensive Soviet military and academic programmes into parapsychology that the popular literature had implied existed turned out to have been scattered, marginal, largely unsuccessful, and themselves of dubious scientific quality — a pattern consistent with the general outcome of serious investigation of paranormal claims across all national and institutional contexts.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Formal fact-checking of the razor-blade pyramid claim is less extensive than for many urban legends, partly because the claim is more explicitly pseudoscientific than political and thus falls more naturally to science journalism than to institutional fact-checking organisations. Snopes does not have a dedicated entry as of 2026. The claim has been addressed in sceptical literature by organisations including the Committee for Skeptical Inquiry (formerly CSICOP) and in publications such as *Skeptical Inquirer*, which has run several pieces on pyramid power starting from its founding issues in the mid-1970s.

The claim represents a relatively simple case for scientific debunking, in that the mechanism is entirely implausible from physical principles and the experimental evidence is negative. The challenge is not that the scientific case against it is unclear, but that the claim persists in informal circulation through multiple overlapping cultural channels: vintage 1970s paperbacks, alternative health communities, and the specific cultural niche of people who find pyramid mysticism aesthetically appealing independent of its empirical status. For this community, the blade-sharpening claim serves less as a falsifiable proposition than as a symbolic affiliation marker — belief expresses membership in a tradition of heterodox inquiry rather than commitment to a specific empirical claim.

The desiccation alternative explanation is worth raising explicitly any time the blade claim is discussed, because it provides a path by which a genuine believer can acknowledge that something real was observed while updating away from the pyramidal mechanism. Explaining why an effect might feel real, through the lens of genuine confounds, tends to be more persuasive than a flat denial that the believer ever experienced anything.
