## §1 Claim and Overview

This Snopes entry by David Mikkelson (2021) is an editorial or observational piece rather than a traditional fact-check with a binary verdict. It presents authentic photographic documentation of McDonald's menus from approximately 1973 and from the original 1955 franchise era, using these images to illuminate how dramatically the chain's menu has expanded over the intervening decades. The article is framed around a photograph of a McDonald's menu board from 1973—the year the Quarter Pounder was introduced nationally—and contrasts it with a contemporary McDonald's menu and with the still-more-spartan original 1955 franchise menu.

The 1973 McDonald's menu depicted includes: the original hamburger, cheeseburger, Big Mac (introduced nationally in 1968), Quarter Pounder (the newest addition in 1973), Filet-o-Fish, french fries, small/large shakes, small/large drinks, and hot apple pies. Coffee was available. The striking absence from this 1973 menu includes: no combo or "Extra Value" meals, no Happy Meals (introduced 1979), no "double" burgers, no chicken products of any kind (Chicken McNuggets were not introduced until 1983), no wraps, no salads, no breakfast items (the Egg McMuffin breakfast platform launched nationally in 1977), no bacon items, no habanero or jalapeño offerings, no Angus beef variants, no desserts other than hot apple pie and ice cream, no coffee drinks other than plain drip coffee, no smoothies, and no McRib.

The 1955 menu, captured in a separate photograph, was even more minimal: hamburger, french fries, and drinks (including milk). The contrast between three eras of the same global brand is used to frame observations about the phenomenological arc of fast food menu complexity.

## §2 Documentary Evidence: Menu Photographs and Historical Sources

Mikkelson (2021) presents three visual artifacts: a photograph of a contemporary McDonald's menu (illustrating current complexity), a photograph of what is identified as a 1973 McDonald's menu board, and a photograph of a vintage menu from the 1955 franchise era. The investigation's implicit claim is that these images are authentic historical artifacts and that the menus depicted are representative of what McDonald's actually offered at those times.

The primary documentary evidence supporting the historical accuracy of the 1973 menu photograph comes from two vectors: the internal consistency of the menu content with independently documented McDonald's menu history, and public corporate and journalistic records.

McDonald's corporate history pages (corporate.mcdonalds.com) confirm that the Quarter Pounder was indeed introduced in 1973, making it the "newest national menu offering" at that time as Mikkelson (2021) describes. The Big Mac's national introduction in 1968 is well-documented; it was originally developed and test-marketed by franchisee Jim Delligatti in 1967 in Uniontown, Pennsylvania. The Filet-o-Fish predates both, having been introduced nationally in 1965 after being developed by Cincinnati franchisee Lou Groen. These three items—Big Mac, Quarter Pounder, and Filet-o-Fish—alongside the basic hamburger and cheeseburger, representing the complete sandwich lineup visible on the 1973 menu, all have independently verified introduction dates consistent with the dating of the photograph.

The 1955 franchise era documentation is supported by Chicago Tribune reporting (cited by Mikkelson, 2021): the first McDonald's franchise opened in Des Plaines, Illinois, in 1955. The original McDonald's system, derived from Dick and Mac McDonald's San Bernardino, California, location, was built around what Ray Kroc described as "Speedee Service"—a radically simplified menu and production system that eliminated the need for waitstaff, carhops, and custom preparation. The 1955 menu's extreme simplicity (hamburger, fries, drink) reflects this Fordist production philosophy, which is well-documented in operational and business history sources.

The absence of chicken products in the 1973 menu aligns with documented history: McDonald's first tested a chicken offering with the McChicken sandwich in 1980, and Chicken McNuggets—the product that fundamentally transformed McDonald's relationship with poultry—were developed with Keystone Foods and introduced in 1983 (Pollan, 2006, *The Omnivore's Dilemma*, Penguin). Breakfast items are also correctly absent from the 1973 menu: the Egg McMuffin was invented by franchisee Herb Peterson in Santa Barbara, California, around 1972 and tested in a handful of locations before the breakfast platform launched nationally in 1977 (Love, 1986, *McDonald's: Behind the Arches*, Bantam). The 1973 national menu would not yet have included breakfast items.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Menu Complexity as a Proxy for the Political Economy of Fast Food

The epistemic novelty in Mikkelson's (2021) observational piece—beneath its surface of nostalgic image presentation—is its implicit argument that changes in fast food menu complexity are legible as a proxy measure for the shifting political economy of the fast food industry: its relationships with commodity agriculture, consumer preference research, regulatory environments, and competitive dynamics. The transformation from a 1955 menu with four items to a 2021 menu with dozens of sandwich variants alone is not merely an accumulation of product ideas; it reflects deterministic structural changes in the industry's economic architecture.

The hamburger's centrality to the original 1955 McDonald's menu reflected the specific economics of mid-century American beef production. The postwar expansion of beef ranching in the Great Plains, combined with the federal policy infrastructure supporting corn-fed feedlot production, created a supply structure that made cheap ground beef available in quantities sufficient to support uniform national fast food production. Schlosser (2001, *Fast Food Nation*, Houghton Mifflin) documented how McDonald's procurement demands drove the consolidation of the American meatpacking industry through the 1960s and 1970s: McDonald's requirements for consistent, large-volume, low-specification ground beef created the economic incentive for meatpacking companies to adopt industrialized slaughter and grinding practices at a scale previously unknown.

The 1973 menu's most significant new item—the Quarter Pounder—represents a specific response to competitive pressure from Wendy's, which had opened its first restaurant in Columbus, Ohio, in 1969 with the advertising promise of "made-to-order" fresh-never-frozen burgers. The Quarter Pounder was explicitly positioned by McDonald's as a premium beef offering emphasizing the weight of its patty (an 18-ounce patty as labeled, suggesting the burger's formulation at that date is that of 4-ounce precooked weight). The Quarter Pounder's introduction is thus a competitive stratagem as well as a product expansion—a response to Wendy's claims about beef quality and portion size (Gibson, 2011, *The Quarter Pounder Story*, QSR Magazine).

The absence of chicken on the 1973 menu is one of the most structurally consequential features of that menu, and the subsequent addition of chicken products represents the single largest transformation in McDonald's menu history. The development and introduction of Chicken McNuggets required, according to Pollan (2006), a parallel transformation in the American poultry industry: to produce McNuggets at the cost and volume required for mass-market pricing, McDonald's needed to work with Keystone Foods to develop industrial boneless chicken processing technology. The McNugget drove the commodification of whole-bird poultry processing and the explosive growth of broiler chicken production that characterized American agriculture from the 1980s onward.

Williams and Nestle (2019, *Big Food, Big Agra, and the Research University*, University of California Press) trace how the expansion of fast food menus from the 1970s to the 2000s was not driven primarily by consumer demand research in the conventional marketing sense, but by what they term "supply-side menu construction"—the addition of items that solved commodity surplus problems or utilized byproduct streams from existing ingredient processing. The development of the McRib in 1981, for example, was partly a response to pork supply economics: the restructured pork patty used in the McRib utilized pork trimmings that were otherwise low-value commodity material. Its cyclical availability is itself a sophisticated supply-chain pricing strategy, according to Glauber (2004, *McDonald's McRib as a Pork Market Barometer*, USDA Economic Research Service note).

## §4 What the Investigation Does Not Fully Address

Mikkelson's (2021) article is an editorial presentation rather than a traditional fact-check, which means its scope is intentionally bounded—it presents images and contextual commentary rather than pursuing verification of specific claims. Several threads invite further development.

**Menu photograph provenance**: The investigation does not document where the 1973 menu photograph was obtained or archived. The image's provenance matters for determining its authenticity: a photograph taken inside a real McDonald's in 1973 is qualitatively different from a reconstruction, a simulator menu, or a regional variation that may not be representative of the national menu at that date. Menu prices, for instance, would vary by region, and some items may have been tested regionally without appearing on the national menu board.

**Regional variation**: McDonald's has historically operated with considerable regional menu variation: specific items have been available in some markets and not others for extended periods. The 1973 photograph depicts a menu that appears to be from a specific location, and whether it accurately reflects a "standard" national menu as opposed to a regional variant is not explicitly addressed.

**Price context**: The 1973 menu includes price information that Mikkelson (2021) does not analyze in detail. The nominal prices visible on a 1973 McDonald's menu—a quarter to fifty cents range for most items at that date—could be contextualized using CPI deflation to contemporary equivalents, providing a concrete sense of how McDonald's pricing strategy and value positioning has shifted over fifty years. The Bureau of Labor Statistics CPI calculator allows this conversion; a 1973 Big Mac price of approximately $0.65 is equivalent to approximately $4.60 in 2023 dollars, suggesting that McDonald's pricing has tracked inflation relatively closely over this period when measured in constant dollars.

**The 1955 menu's full scope**: The investigation mentions that the original 1955 franchise menu included "a hamburger, french fries, and a drink (including milk). And that was it." Documentation associated with the Des Plaines franchise also mentions root beer and other soft drinks, soft serve ice cream (a Dairy Queen-derived item that influenced the early McDonald's soft serve program), and possibly other items depending on the specific opening date documentation.

## §5 Contextual Framing: The Quarter Pounder in Food History and the Larger Arc of Fast Food Menu Expansion

The Quarter Pounder—the central new item on the 1973 McDonald's menu featured in Mikkelson's (2021) piece—has a specific and well-documented history that illuminates the competitive and cultural moment of the early 1970s fast food industry. The burger was conceived by Al Bernadin, a franchisee in Fremont, California, in 1971. Bernadin's prototype was a larger, heavier beef patty intended to compete directly with Wendy's marketing around fresh, substantial burgers. McDonald's corporate initially resisted the innovation because it required new cooking equipment—specifically a new grill capable of handling the thicker patty—but eventually approved the Quarter Pounder for national launch in 1973 (Kroc with Anderson, 1977, *Grinding It Out: The Making of McDonald's*, Henry Regnery).

The Quarter Pounder's national launch is significant for another reason Mikkelson (2021) does not discuss: it was among the first McDonald's products to be marketed with an explicit nutritional weight claim ("Quarter Pounder" as a promise about patty weight). This marketing choice reflected a growing consumer interest in understanding what was actually in fast food products—an interest that would intensify dramatically in the 1980s and 1990s as nutritional labeling requirements expanded. The Quarter Pounder's explicit weight framing was a precursor to the more pervasive nutritional transparency demands of later decades, though it focused on quantity rather than quality composition.

The arc of McDonald's menu expansion from 1955 to the present spans approximately four major phases, each driven by distinct structural forces. The first phase (1955–1970) was characterized by radical simplification and standardization around hamburgers, fries, and shakes—the Fordist production model. The second phase (1970–1985) saw the deliberate addition of menu items targeting specific competitive pressures (Quarter Pounder vs. Wendy's, Egg McMuffin vs. Denny's breakfast market, McNuggets vs. fried chicken fast food) and the establishment of the Happy Meal as a child-targeting marketing vehicle. The third phase (1985–2000) involved the proliferation of limited-time offers, regional items, and the first serious attempts to add salads and premium items targeting health-conscious consumers. The fourth phase (2000–present) is characterized by the beverage platform revolution (McCafé specialty coffees), the expansion of chicken sandwich variants to meet shifting consumer preferences, and persistent attempts to position McDonald's as a source of "fresh" or "quality" ingredients against fast-casual competitive pressure.

Ritzer (1993, *The McDonaldization of Society*, Pine Forge Press) analyzed the original Fordist McDonald's model as a sociological template for the rationalization of service industries, arguing that its emphasis on efficiency, calculability, predictability, and control extended beyond food service and became a paradigm for organizational management more broadly. Ritzer's framework, while somewhat parochially focused on the original model, is directly illuminated by comparing the 1955 four-item menu (maximum Fordist predictability and efficiency) with the contemporary multi-hundred variation menu (maximum consumer customization at the cost of operational complexity). The managerial tension this trajectory creates—Fordist production efficiency vs. post-Fordist consumer customization demand—is visible in the gaps between the menus Mikkelson (2021) presents.

## §6 Verdict Assessment and Research Gaps

Mikkelson's (2021) piece contains no explicit fact-check verdict because it is an editorial/historical commentary item rather than a traditional Snopes investigation. The implicit claim—that the photographs depict genuine historical McDonald's menus—is strongly supported by the internal consistency of the menu content with independently documented McDonald's history. No item visible on the 1973 menu belongs to a category that did not exist in McDonald's portfolio until after 1973; all items present are confirmed in independent corporate and trade press documentation.

**Epistemic classification**: This is a case of *historical documentation presentation*—sharing authentic artifacts and contextualizing them historically. The verifiable factual content (introduction dates for specific menu items, franchise opening dates, menu item composition) is well-corroborated by independent corporate and journalistic sources.

**EARC gap A (Archival)**: The primary research gap is the lack of documented provenance for the specific 1973 photograph—where it was taken, who took it, and what institution or collection it is currently held in. Secondary gaps include the absence of price-adjusted comparative analysis and the limited engagement with the competitive strategic context that explains why the 1973 menu looked the way it did.

**Research vectors**: The McDonald's Corporation archives and the McDonald's Museum in Des Plaines, Illinois (though the museum was demolished in 2017 per the Chicago Tribune article Mikkelson cites, its collection was dispersed to various archival institutions), as well as the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History, which holds significant fast food industry artifacts. Newspaper morgue photographs from 1973 community coverage of McDonald's openings would also be productive sources of additional comparable menu board images.
