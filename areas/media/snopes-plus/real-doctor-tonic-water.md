# 'Real Doctor' Promoting Tonic Water for COVID-19 Spreads Dangerous Misinfo

## 1. The Claim

In April 2020, as the COVID-19 pandemic was sweeping the globe with New York City in the grip of an unprecedented public health crisis and the British prime minister recently released from intensive care, a chiropractor named Eric Nepute livestreamed a 20-minute video that was viewed over 1.3 million times. In the video, Nepute—described by the YouTube video title as a "real doctor telling it like it is"—made a series of claims that Snopes would later characterize as "potently dangerous in the time of a global pandemic."

Nepute's central claims were severalfold: that the mainstream media was lying about the severity of the pandemic; that people should drink Schweppes tonic water to prevent or treat COVID-19 because of its quinine content; that quinine, chloroquine, and hydroxychloroquine were "similar-ish" and therefore interchangeable treatments; that healthy living, supplements, and immune support were sufficient defenses against the virus; and that the pandemic would be over within weeks.

Snopes published its analysis on April 15, 2020, authored by Alex Kasprak. The article was not a traditional claim rating but rather an extended explanatory debunk that analyzed Nepute's arguments, his credentials, the chemistry of quinine, and the epidemiology of COVID-19. It rated none of the individual claims with a Snopes rating but collectively presented them as "dangerous misinformation."

## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes' analysis of the specific scientific and factual issues in Nepute's video is largely accurate and well-researched.

The article correctly identifies that Eric Nepute is not a medical doctor. He holds a Doctorate of Chiropractic from Logan University and is licensed in Missouri. Chiropractors are not MDs or DOs, the two medical degrees that qualify one to practice as a physician in the United States. Nepute's presentation of himself as a "real doctor" and his statement "I'm a physician" in the video are misleading. While chiropractors do hold doctorates, the public understanding of "doctor" in a medical context overwhelmingly means a medical doctor, and Nepute clearly leveraged this ambiguity to lend credibility to his claims.

The article's analysis of quinine content in tonic water is scientifically sound. The FDA limits quinine in tonic water to 83 parts per million, which translates to approximately 83 milligrams of quinine per liter. Medical treatment for malaria with quinine requires 648 mg every eight hours for seven days. As Snopes correctly calculates, one would need to drink over 12 liters of Schweppes tonic water every eight hours to maintain therapeutic levels. This analysis definitively refutes the notion that drinking tonic water could have any meaningful medical effect against COVID-19.

The article's treatment of quinine, chloroquine, and hydroxychloroquine is careful and scientifically accurate. It correctly explains that while all three belong to the quinoline family of compounds, they are not interchangeable. Quinine is a naturally occurring alkaloid from cinchona bark; chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine are synthetic 4-aminoquinolines. Their mechanisms of action against malaria are reasonably well understood, but their potential antiviral properties are far from settled science. The article cites the limited and conflicting evidence from cell-culture studies and the Cochrane Database's finding that zinc studies for the common cold have had "mixed results." This is responsible scientific communication.

The article's correction of Nepute's claim that "the cell wall" becomes more permeable is important. Nepute's proposed mechanism for how quinoline drugs work is not supported by the scientific literature. The leading hypothesis for these drugs' potential antiviral activity involves altering intracellular pH levels to inhibit viral replication, not any effect on cell wall permeability. Snopes correctly identifies this as a fundamental misrepresentation of the science.

The article's documentation of COVID-19 deaths among young, healthy people without known risk factors is accurate and essential. Nepute's claim that "getting healthy" is sufficient protection against the virus was, as Snopes notes, a dangerous oversimplification that downplayed the severity of a disease that was killing people across all age groups and health profiles.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

The Snopes article on Nepute's tonic water claims represents one of the stronger examples of explanatory debunking in the COVID-19 misinformation context. Alex Kasprak's analysis is methodical, well-sourced, and scientifically literate. The article successfully demonstrates why Nepute's claims are wrong, and it does so at a level of detail that is unusual for Snopes' shorter fact-checks.

**Where Snopes gets it right:**

The structural decision to write a long-form explanatory debunk rather than a brief claim rating was appropriate for the subject matter. COVID-19 misinformation was not a single false claim but a complex ecosystem of interlocking falsehoods, half-truths, and misrepresentations. Nepute's video exemplified this complexity: he mixed reasonable health advice (eat well, exercise, take supplements) with dangerous falsehoods (tonic water as treatment, media conspiracy, downplaying disease severity). Disentangling these required more space and analytical depth than a typical fact-check could provide, and the article delivers.

The article's handling of the quinine chemistry is exemplary. The calculation showing that one would need to drink 12 liters of tonic water every eight hours is the kind of concrete, intuitive refutation that readers can immediately understand and remember. The comparison between the FDA-approved dose for malaria and the trivial amount of quinine in tonic water is devastating to Nepute's argument.

The article's documentation of Nepute's credential ambiguity is important and well-handled. It does not attack chiropractic as a profession but rather draws a clear line between legitimate chiropractic practice and the unauthorized practice of medicine. The article respects the distinction while making clear that Nepute's claims about virology and epidemiology fall outside his area of professional competence.

The article's sourcing is strong, drawing on FDA regulations, peer-reviewed research published in PLOS One, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and reporting from established news outlets including NBC News, the New York Times, Vox, and the Atlantic. For a piece of explanatory journalism, this standard of sourcing is appropriate and credible.

**Where Snopes falls short:**

Despite its strengths, the article has several significant limitations that deserve critical examination.

First, the article frames Nepute as an individual bad actor—a lone chiropractor spreading dangerous falsehoods—without adequately addressing the systemic conditions that enabled his video to reach 1.3 million views. Nepute did not emerge from nowhere. He was part of a broader ecosystem of COVID-19 misinformation that was actively amplified by platform algorithms, political figures, and media dynamics. The article mentions that U.S. President Donald Trump had promoted hydroxychloroquine, a context that is briefly noted but not explored. Understanding the scale of the problem requires understanding that Nepute was not the source but a downstream beneficiary of a much larger misinformation pipeline that included the White House, cable news, and social media platforms.

Second, the article does not adequately address the role of platform amplification. Nepute's video was viewed 1.3 million times on YouTube. The article does not discuss YouTube's content moderation policies, the platform's decision to allow the video to remain online, or the algorithmic mechanisms that recommended it to users. In a piece about dangerous misinformation, the distribution system is at least as important as the content itself.

Third, the article's exclusive focus on Nepute omits the broader pattern of medical professionals and quasi-professionals exploiting credential ambiguity during the pandemic. Nepute was far from alone. Doctors of all types—MDs, DOs, chiropractors, naturopaths, functional medicine practitioners—spread COVID-19 misinformation during this period, from hydroxychloroquine advocates to ivermectin proponents to vaccine opponents. The article treats Nepute as a singular case rather than as a representative example of a systemic problem involving regulatory gaps, professional ethics, and platform responsibility.

Fourth, the article does not address the question of harm. The piece describes Nepute's claims as "dangerous misinformation" and "potently dangerous," but it does not provide evidence of actual harm caused. Did people drink tonic water in dangerous quantities? Did people avoid medical treatment because they believed Nepute's dismissal of the pandemic's severity? Did the video's reach translate into behavioral consequences? The article's claim of danger is asserted rather than demonstrated, and the lack of harm assessment is a gap in what is otherwise a thorough analytical piece.

Fifth, the article's framing of "a real doctor telling it like it is" as sarcastic dismissal is understandable but misses the opportunity to analyze why such messaging is persuasive. Nepute's rhetorical strategy—positioning himself as an honest truth-teller against a corrupt establishment—is a well-worn playbook that has proven highly effective across domains, from vaccine skepticism to climate denial to political conspiracy theories. Understanding why this framing works, and why millions of people found it convincing, is essential to combating it effectively.

## 4. Why This Matters

The Nepute tonic water article matters for several reasons that extend beyond the specifics of a single chiropractor's YouTube video.

First, it matters as a case study in the challenge of countering health misinformation during a public health emergency. The COVID-19 pandemic was accompanied by an "infodemic"—a parallel epidemic of misinformation that the World Health Organization identified as a major threat to public health. Nepute's video, which blended reasonable health advice (eat well, exercise) with dangerous falsehoods (tonic water as treatment, media conspiracy), exemplified the difficulty of responding to misinformation that contains grains of truth. A response that dismisses everything Nepute said would risk alienating people who agreed with his reasonable advice; a response that engaged selectively would risk seeming partial. Snopes' approach of methodically analyzing each claim was reasonable, but the time and effort required to produce such an analysis (days or weeks) cannot keep pace with the speed at which 1.3 million people can watch a video.

Second, it matters for understanding credential ambiguity in health communication. The title of this Snopes article—"Real Doctor" Promoting Tonic Water for COVID-19 Spreads Dangerous Misinfo—puts scare quotes around "Real Doctor" to signal that Nepute is not what most people would consider a doctor. But the scare-quote strategy is a weak response to a structural problem. The American healthcare system includes a confusing array of credentialing systems—MDs, DOs, chiropractors, naturopaths, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, functional medicine practitioners—whose boundaries are poorly understood by the general public. Misinformation exploiters deliberately leverage this confusion. Addressing it requires not just individual debunking but systemic reform of professional title regulation and public education about credentialing systems.

Third, it matters for understanding the economics of online health misinformation. Nepute's video was not a public service; it was content designed to build his brand, attract patients to his clinic, and generate revenue. The YouTube platform facilitated this by allowing the video to remain online and by recommending it to users. The economic incentives created by the platform—advertising revenue, audience building, algorithmic amplification—are the structural conditions that make health misinformation profitable. The Snopes article addresses the content of the misinformation but not its economics.

Fourth, it matters as a demonstration of the limits of expertise. Nepute's claim that he is a doctor highlights a fundamental challenge: not all expertise is equal, but the public lacks the tools to distinguish between different types of expertise. A chiropractor is an expert in musculoskeletal health; an immunologist is an expert in immune response; an epidemiologist is an expert in disease spread. Nepute's credibility on COVID-19 should have been zero, but his legitimate credential as a doctor of chiropractic created enough ambiguity to make him seem authoritative. The challenge of navigating different types of expertise is a feature of modern life that will only intensify as the number of credentialed professionals and the volume of health information continue to grow.

## 5. Research Notes

The Snopes article was published on April 15, 2020, by Alex Kasprak, a veteran Snopes journalist who has written extensively on science, health, and misinformation. Kasprak's byline appears on many of Snopes' more scientifically rigorous pieces, and his background in science writing is evident in the article's careful handling of the chemistry and pharmacology.

The article was published during the peak of the first wave of COVID-19 misinformation, which included false claims about miracle cures (hydroxychloroquine, ivermectin, bleach), downplaying the pandemic's severity, conspiracy theories about the virus's origin, and anti-vaccine propaganda. The article was part of a larger Snopes editorial effort to combat COVID-19 misinformation, which the organization explicitly framed as fighting an "infodemic."

The article's sources include the FDA Code of Federal Regulations (for the quinine limit in tonic water), peer-reviewed articles from PLOS One, PLOS Pathogens, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and the Journal of Medicinal Chemistry. News sources include NBC News, the New York Times, Vox, the Atlantic, the BBC, and STAT News. This is a robust selection of sources for a single article.

The article's structure is notable for its length. At well over 2,000 words, it is significantly longer than most Snopes fact-checks, which typically run 500–800 words. This reflects both the complexity of the subject matter and Snopes' editorial decision to invest more resources in countering the most dangerous COVID-19 misinformation.

The article does not explicitly address the question of whether Nepute faced any consequences for spreading dangerous health misinformation during a pandemic. In subsequent years, licensure boards in multiple states would take action against practitioners who spread COVID-19 misinformation, and some would face civil lawsuits. The article was written before these developments.

The article does not address the regulatory question of whether a chiropractor can lose their license for practicing medicine without authorization or for spreading harmful health advice. This is a consequential legal and policy question that remains relevant.

The article uses the framing device of "a real doctor telling it like it is" throughout, treating it as ironic. While effective as a rhetorical strategy, this framing does not explain the psychological appeal of the "truth-teller against the establishment" persona that made Nepute's messaging effective.

## 6. Verdict

**Rating: Scientifically accurate and methodologically sound, but structurally limited by its individual-actor framing and its failure to address the systemic conditions that make health misinformation endemic.**

Snopes' article on Eric Nepute's tonic water COVID-19 claims is one of the stronger examples of explanatory misinformation debunking from the pandemic era. It correctly identifies the factual errors, provides clear and accessible scientific explanations, and documents the credential ambiguity that made Nepute's claims seem credible to 1.3 million viewers.

However, the article's framing of Nepute as a singular bad actor obscures the systemic dimensions of the problem. Nepute was not an anomaly but a symptom of a healthcare system with confusing credential hierarchies, a media environment where platform algorithms reward sensational content, and a regulatory system that struggles to hold practitioners accountable for spreading dangerous health advice. The article addresses the symptom without diagnosing the disease.

For a reader who wants to know whether tonic water can treat COVID-19, this article provides an excellent answer: decisively no, and here is the science. For a reader who wants to understand why health misinformation spreads, why people believe it, and what can be done about it, the article points in the right direction but does not complete the journey. The truth about the tonic water misinformation is not just that it was wrong—it is that the conditions that produced it were structural, not personal, and addressing them requires more than individual debunking.
