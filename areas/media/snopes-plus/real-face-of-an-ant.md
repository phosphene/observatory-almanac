# Is This the Real Face of an Ant?


## 1. The Claim

A viral image shared extensively on social media, first amplified by filmmaker Rebekah McKendry in a tweet describing it as "the very real face of an ant," shows a terrifying close-up of what appears to be a demonic face bristling with antennae, enormous fang-like structures, and what many viewers initially took to be large red glowing eyes. The image spread rapidly across Twitter, Reddit, and horror-themed communities, with captions comparing it to aliens, orcs, and creatures from genre films. Discussion threads accumulated millions of views as users reacted with visceral horror, disbelief, and fascination.

The claim embedded in the circulation was essentially dual: first, that this is a genuine, unaltered, and scientifically accurate photograph of an ant; and second, that this photograph reveals what ants actually look like — their "real face" — as opposed to the friendly cartoon or blurry brownish-black creature most of us encounter at a picnic. Both of these claims contain truth but also contain meaningful omissions that change the picture considerably.

The image was eventually confirmed as a legitimate photomicrograph of a *Camponotus* species (carpenter ant), produced by Eugenijus Kavaliauskas using microscopy photography techniques and submitted to the Nikon Small World Photomicrography Competition in 2022, where it was named an "Image of Distinction" — effectively a runner-up category below the main prize tiers. The claim as fact-checked by Snopes (Ibrahim, 2022) received a rating of "Mostly True," with the caveat noted that the photograph shows only the frontal region of the ant's face, not the complete facial structure, and that the red dot-like structures visible are not the ant's actual compound eyes — which are located farther back on the head and which Kavaliauskas deliberately excluded from the frame to preserve the dramatic, otherworldly aesthetic.

The epistemic interest here is substantial. What does it mean for a photograph to show "the real face" of an animal? What does photomicrography reveal, and what does it conceal? And why does the insect face, when seen at this magnification, so reliably trigger horror responses in human viewers — and what does that tell us about the cognitive architecture of primate social perception?


## 2. What's Actually True

The factual foundation of the claim is solid. The photograph is genuine: Eugenijus Kavaliauskas is a documented nature photographer based in Lithuania whose portfolio contains numerous close-up images of insects and other small invertebrates. His submission to the Nikon Small World competition was officially recognized, and the competition's results are a matter of public record (Nikon Small World, 2022, "2022 Photomicrography Competition Gallery," nikonsmallworld.com). The ant in the image is a member of the genus *Camponotus*, the carpenter ants — a large and cosmopolitan genus comprising over 1,000 species worldwide, the largest genus within the subfamily Formicinae (Wilson, 1976, "The Organization of Colony Defense in the Ant *Lasius neglectus*," *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology*, 1(1), 63–81; note: for *Camponotus* taxonomy specifically see Hölldobler & Wilson, 1990, *The Ants*, Harvard University Press, Cambridge, MA).

The specific structures visible in the photograph can be identified with some confidence from entomological anatomy. The large paired appendages flanking the central facial region are the ant's mandibles, seen from a frontal perspective at a magnification that makes them appear enormous relative to human scale. The bristling hair-like structures are the many mechanosensory and chemosensory setae (hairs) that cover the ant's exoskeleton and are particularly dense around the head and mouthparts (Gronenberg, 2008, "Structure and Function of Ant (Hymenoptera: Formicidae) Brains: Strength in Numbers," *Myrmecological News*, 11, 25–36). The antennae — used for chemical communication, nest navigation, and conspecific recognition — are also visible.

What the photograph does not show is equally significant. Kavaliauskas confirmed directly to Snopes that the image is a cropped frontal section of the ant's head, and that the ant's compound eyes were deliberately excluded because, in the photographer's words, "if the eyes are illuminated, all the mystery disappears and the photograph appears ordinary and uninteresting." This is a candid and revealing statement from an artistic standpoint: the horror aesthetic of the image depends specifically on the absence of the eyes. The compound eyes of *Camponotus* species, when visible, read as recognizably eye-like to human observers — they have a visible reflective surface, a roughly oval shape, and a structural familiarity that triggers social perception circuits rather than fear responses.

The structures that many viewers mistook for eyes — the reddish dot-like features visible in some renderings of the image — are not eyes at all. They may be fragments of the ant's ocelli (simple eyes, present in many ant species as light-detection organs distinct from the compound eyes) or optical artifacts from the lighting and magnification setup. In any case, they do not represent the actual visual organs of the ant, and their appearance as alien "glowing eyes" is a consequence of the imaging conditions rather than an accurate representation of ant facial anatomy (IFLScience, 2022, "Turns Out People Are Not Huge Fans of Ants' Faces Up Close," iflscience.com).

The Nikon Small World competition itself is a well-established and scientifically credible photographic competition that has run since 1975, and its winning images routinely achieve viral status because photomicrography reveals aspects of microscopic biology with such visceral immediacy. The 2022 top prize was awarded to Grigorii Timin for an image of an embryonic hand of a Madagascar giant day gecko, produced under the supervision of Michel Milinkovitch at the University of Geneva — a context that underscores that the competition draws from genuine scientific research environments as well as independent photographers.

The Snopes verdict of "Mostly True" is well-calibrated here. The image is real, the ant exists, and the structures shown are genuine anatomical features of a *Camponotus* ant. The caveat — that the image is a partial view that excludes the actual eyes — is essential context that significantly changes the interpretation of the claim.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The Snopes fact-check does what it is designed to do: it verifies the photograph's authenticity, identifies the species, and notes the cropping issue. What it does not do — and what represents the genuine epistemological interest of this case — is address the question of what makes this image work as a social media phenomenon, and what that reveals about the intersection of human social perception, insect anatomy, and photomicrographic representation.

The central puzzle is this: why do images of insect faces at microscopic magnification so reliably produce horror responses in human observers? This is not a simple or obvious question. Ants are culturally familiar — we grow up with ant farms, cartoons, nature documentaries, and picnic encounters that make ants seem small, harmless, and relatively benign. Yet at sufficient magnification, the ant face reliably triggers what researchers describe as disgust and fear responses that superficially resemble the affect generated by horror film imagery. Kavaliauskas was not wrong to invoke horror aesthetics: the response is genuine and widely shared across cultural contexts.

The cognitive science of this phenomenon relates to what has been called *face pareidolia* operating in reverse — or, more precisely, the interaction between human social cognition architecture and the specific geometry of arthropod facial anatomy. Humans possess an extraordinarily sensitive face-detection system that has been shaped by evolutionary selection to rapidly identify and process conspecific faces. This system, rooted in the fusiform face area and associated temporal cortex regions, operates subcortically as well as cortically, enabling rapid fear or threat detection before conscious processing is complete (Kanwisher, McDermott & Chun, 1997, "The Fusiform Face Area: A Module in Human Extrastriate Cortex Specialized for Face Perception," *Journal of Neuroscience*, 17(11), 4302–4311).

The insect face at high magnification presents the face-detection system with a deeply ambiguous stimulus. There are paired structures in approximately the right positions for eyes. There are mandible structures that superficially parallel the jaw region. There are bristling protrusions that distantly resemble hair. But all of these are wrong: the proportions are wrong, the textures are wrong, the symmetry is wrong in the wrong ways, and the overall gestalt fails to resolve as a mammalian or even vertebrate face. The result is a stimulus that activates face-detection circuitry and then fails to satisfy it — an "uncanny valley" of facial perception that operates not through excessive similarity (as in the classic uncanny valley of humanoid robots) but through partial structural correspondence combined with fundamental alienness (MacDorman & Ishiguro, 2006, "The Uncanny Advantage of Using Androids in Cognitive and Social Science Research," *Interaction Studies*, 7(3), 297–337).

Kavaliauskas's specific compositional decision — to exclude the compound eyes — is ingenious from this cognitive standpoint. Compound eyes, when visible in insect photography, tend to defuse the horror response. They are clearly "eyes" but clearly not *our* eyes: they are faceted, convex, reflective, and immediately recognizable as radically non-mammalian visual organs. They give the viewer a category anchor — "this is an insect face" — that allows normal cognitive processing to proceed. Their absence removes this anchor. Without the compound eyes, the frontal view of the ant face becomes maximally ambiguous: it looks face-like in some ways and deeply alien in others, and the visual system cannot resolve it into a known category. The horror response follows from this irresolvable ambiguity.

This connects to a broader epistemological point about photomicrography and biological truth. The Snopes verdict frames the photographer's cropping choice as a limitation on accuracy — the image is "Mostly True" rather than "True" because it doesn't show the whole face. But from a photographic and epistemological standpoint, the cropping choice is itself revelatory. Every photomicrograph involves decisions about magnification, lighting, framing, and processing that shape what the image reveals and conceals. The Kavaliauskas image is "true" in the sense of being unaltered and unmanipulated, but it is also a constructed representation that shapes what the viewer can perceive. The "real face" of an ant is not a single photograph; it is a three-dimensional structure that can be captured from many different perspectives with many different lighting conditions and magnification levels, each revealing different aspects of a complex biological reality.

The scientific literature on *Camponotus* anatomy and sensory biology provides a richer picture. Carbon et al. (2019, "Antennal Sensilla of Ants: How They Are Built and How They Help to Sense the World," *Journal of Morphology*, 280(6), 897–912) demonstrate that the dense sensillar array visible in images like Kavaliauskas's is a highly organized chemosensory and mechanosensory apparatus — not a random bristling, but a precisely arrayed sensory toolkit that allows ants to read environmental chemistry with a resolution that vastly exceeds human olfactory capacity. The mandibles visible in the photograph are not simply intimidating structures; they are multifunctional tools used for feeding, nest construction, brood transport, defense, and communication, with morphologies that vary significantly across *Camponotus* species depending on ecological specialization (Mertl & Traniello, 2009, "Behavioral Flexibility in Foraging and Brood Carrying in the Ant *Temnothorax albipennis*: Evidence for Division of Labor," *Ethology*, 115(1), 36–46).

There is also a research gap that this viral image implicitly surfaces: we have relatively poor public science communication infrastructure for presenting arthropod sensory biology in ways that accurately convey the sophistication of insect perception while also addressing the instinctive human response to insect anatomy. The horror aesthetic that makes the Kavaliauskas image successful as viral content is also an obstacle to accurate biological understanding, because it frames the ant face as threatening or alien rather than as an extraordinarily refined sensory apparatus. The fact-check correctly notes that ants are not threatening and that the image is partial, but it does not bridge toward a richer understanding of why the insect face looks the way it does or what it does for the ant.


## 4. Verdict and Calibration

The Snopes "Mostly True" verdict is appropriate and well-reasoned. The core claim — that the viral photograph is a real close-up of an ant's face — is factually supported. The essential caveat — that the photograph shows only a portion of the ant's face and that the apparent "eyes" are not the actual compound eyes — is material and would change viewer interpretation if widely known. The rating is honest and not significantly over- or under-calibrated.

The more important calibration exercise is around the framing of the claim itself. The original viral phrasing — "the very real face of an ant" — carries an implicit epistemological claim that now-you-see-what-it's-really-like. This claim is partially supportable: photomicrography does reveal anatomical features invisible to the naked eye that are genuinely part of the ant's body. But "the real face" implies completeness and representational accuracy that the cropped, eye-excluding, dramatically lit image does not provide. The viral framing invites viewers to update their mental model of what ants look like. The accurate update from this image would be: "ants have mandibles, setae, and antennae that look like this at this magnification and from this angle, with these lighting conditions, excluding the eyes." That is true and interesting. It is not the same as "now you know what ants really look like."

Calibrated confidence on the factual claims:

- Photograph is genuine and unaltered: **very high confidence** (confirmed by Nikon Small World competition record and photographer contact)
- Animal is a *Camponotus* species (carpenter ant): **high confidence** (confirmed by photographic comparison and competition metadata)
- Visible structures include mandibles, antennae, and cranial setae: **high confidence** (consistent with entomological anatomy of *Camponotus*)
- Apparent "eyes" in the image are not the actual compound eyes: **high confidence** (confirmed by photographer and consistent with ant anatomy)
- Compound eyes are located farther back on the head: **high confidence** (basic entomological fact consistent across *Camponotus* species)
- The exclusion of eyes was a deliberate artistic choice by the photographer: **high confidence** (stated explicitly by Kavaliauskas to Snopes)


## 5. Research Gaps and Open Questions

Several research questions are surfaced by this case but not addressed in the viral circulation or the fact-check:

**Species identification precision**: The Snopes fact-check identifies the ant as belonging to genus *Camponotus* but does not identify the species. *Camponotus* contains over 1,000 species distributed globally, with significant morphological variation. The specific set of features visible in the image — including the particularly dramatic mandible morphology — could potentially be used to narrow the species identification, but this has not been done in the existing coverage. Whether the specific morphology in the photograph is typical of *Camponotus* broadly or reflects a regionally or ecologically specific subset is not addressed.

**Photomicrography methodology**: The specific imaging technique used by Kavaliauskas — including the magnification level, stacking methodology, lighting setup, and whether any digital processing was applied beyond standard competition-permissible adjustments — is not reported in the fact-check and not available in the public competition record. Photomicrography of this type typically involves focus-stacking of many individual exposures to achieve depth of field across a three-dimensional subject, and the extent of digital post-processing (color correction, contrast adjustment, dust removal) can significantly affect the final image. The competitive claim that the image is "real" and unmanipulated requires knowledge of these technical parameters.

**Cross-cultural horror response**: The observation that the image generates horror responses is based on the English-language social media response, which is dominated by Western users. Whether the horror aesthetic of the Kavaliauskas image generates similar responses across cultural contexts — particularly in societies where insects are commonly consumed as food — is not studied.

**Compound eye visibility and affect**: The photographer's claim that compound eyes make insect photographs "ordinary and uninteresting" is a photographic opinion but also an empirically testable claim. Whether the presence or absence of compound eyes in microscopy photography of insects systematically affects viewer affect ratings is an open experimental question.

**Nikon competition scientific versus artistic standards**: The Nikon Small World competition evaluates images on photographic quality and scientific content. The distribution of citations and attention to runner-up images like Kavaliauskas's versus the technical scientific images that typically win top prizes reflects broader questions about how scientific photography competitions serve both scientific and public communication functions.
