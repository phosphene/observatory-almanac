# Reba McEntire charged Fox News for using her music


## 1. The Claim

Versions of this story began circulating across Facebook, Twitter/X, and Instagram in recurring waves starting around 2022, with surges following notable moments of political tension around cultural figures in country music. The claim typically takes the following form: Reba McEntire, the legendary Oklahoma-born country singer and television personality, discovered that Fox News had been using one or more of her songs — frequently "Is There Life Out There," "Fancy," or a generic description of "her music" — as background audio on air without obtaining proper licensing or her consent. Upon discovering this, McEntire's legal team sent an invoice or a cease-and-desist letter to Fox News, demanding either payment or that the network stop using her music entirely. In some variants, McEntire is quoted directly with language expressing displeasure at the association between her music and the network's editorial identity. In others, Fox News allegedly refused to pay, and McEntire initiated legal proceedings.

The story is frequently accompanied by adulatory commentary from users who frame it as an act of political courage — a famous woman from a genre closely associated with conservative audiences standing up to the most-watched conservative cable news network in America. The emotional tonality of the claim is not primarily about copyright law but about cultural identity: it presents McEntire as a countercultural figure within country music, aligned with a political tradition that her fan base does not universally share.

This narrative framing places the claim within a broader category of viral stories about celebrities confronting Fox News over music use. Donald Trump's rallies generated an unusually large number of such stories — multiple artists publicly objecting to their music's use at political events — and the resulting media attention created a template that subsequent fabricated stories could mimic. The structural legibility of the pattern (artist objects to political use of their work) made stories following this template highly shareable and superficially plausible.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The underlying copyright framework.** Music licensing in television and broadcast media is governed by a complex system of licensing agreements, primarily administered through performing rights organisations (PROs) including ASCAP, BMI, and SESAC in the United States (Kohn & Kohn, 2010, *Music Licensing*, 4th ed., Aspen Publishers). Broadcast entities like Fox News Corporation hold blanket licensing agreements with these PROs that cover the use of a very large catalogue of music in their programming. A blanket licence grants the broadcaster the legal right to use any work in the PRO's catalogue without seeking individual artist approval, in exchange for a flat annual fee calculated based on the broadcaster's revenue and reach.

The practical consequence of this system is that artists — including major country artists whose catalogues are administered through BMI or ASCAP — typically cannot legally prevent a broadcaster who holds an appropriate blanket licence from using their music in programming. This is a source of genuine frustration for some artists when their work is used in contexts they find objectionable, and it has motivated some artists to pursue alternative legal strategies: challenging the specific application of the licence, arguing that certain uses (particularly synchronisation with political advertising) require a different licence type, or issuing public statements that may have no legal force but generate media attention (Passman, 2015, *All You Need to Know About the Music Business*, 9th ed., Simon & Schuster).

Reba McEntire's music catalogue is administered through BMI. Fox News Corporation holds an active blanket licence with BMI. The licensing architecture would, in the majority of programming contexts, already legally cover any incidental use of McEntire's music.

**What Reba McEntire has actually said about Fox News.** A thorough review of McEntire's public statements, press interviews, and social media activity through the relevant period reveals no public statement by McEntire charging, invoicing, or threatening legal action against Fox News over music use. McEntire is an active user of social media and regularly responds to public controversies affecting her brand and image; the absence of any statement on this alleged event is itself significant. Stars with her media management sophistication, whose legal team had taken a newsworthy action, would almost certainly acknowledge it.

McEntire gave a widely-noted interview to *People* magazine in 2021 in which she discussed her approach to political topics, characterised her own views as "independent," and expressed a preference for keeping her public presentation focused on her music and entertainment work rather than partisan alignment (People, March 2021). She has also discussed the complexity of her audience relationships, noting the challenge of navigating a fan base with diverse political views. None of these statements reference Fox News specifically or address music licensing.

**Fact-checker findings.** Snopes.com rated this claim FALSE in their detailed assessment, noting that there is no credible sourcing, no court filing, no reporting from entertainment industry outlets that cover copyright disputes, no confirmation from McEntire's management, and no Fox News acknowledgment of any such demand (Snopes, "Did Reba McEntire Charge Fox News for Using her Music?", 2022). Snopes identified the claim as originating from satirical or politically motivated fabrication sites that generate celebrity-vs.-Fox-News stories as a recurring content format.

**The satirical origin ecosystem.** The claim traces to a cluster of websites that produce politically inflected celebrity news fabrications in the format of credible journalism. These sites operate with names that mimic real news outlets, use stock photographs of the celebrities involved, and generate stories structured to satisfy readers who want confirmation that cultural figures they admire share their political views. Research on this content genre has identified it as a particularly persistent form of fabricated news because it generates strong emotional resonance, rarely involves directly verifiable real-world events, and tends to be shared primarily within politically homogeneous social networks where counter-evidence is unlikely to be introduced (Vosoughi, Roy & Aral, 2018, "The Spread of True and False News Online," *Science*, 359(6380), 1146–1151).


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The celebrity-resistance narrative template.** The most powerful structural explanation for this claim's persistence and spread is that it exactly matches a template that has generated thousands of shares of verified stories. The documented cases of artists objecting to Donald Trump's use of their music at campaign events — Neil Young, Rolling Stones, Rihanna, R.E.M., Adele, and dozens of others issued public objections between 2015 and 2024 — created deep cultural familiarity with the concept of an artist publicly challenging a politically identified entity over music use (McCormick, 2020, "Musicians Keep Telling Trump to Stop Using Their Songs. Why Can't He Be Stopped?", *Washington Post*, August 2020). This template is structurally indistinguishable from the McEntire/Fox claim in surface form. Readers encountering the fabricated claim have strong prior evidence that this type of event happens, and that prior correctly reduces their initial scepticism.

**Epistemic novelty in pattern-matching fabrication.** What is sociologically distinctive about this category of fabricated claim — and what distinguishes it from outright hoaxes or conspiracy theories — is that it operates through structural plausibility rather than evidential support. Traditional misinformation research focused on false factual claims that directly contradicted observable reality (vaccines cause autism; a specific event happened on a specific date). The celebrity-resistance fabrication genre works differently: it generates stories that *could* have happened, that fit a recognised real-world pattern, and that carry no obvious internal impossibility. This is a form of what epistemologists have called "epistemic injection" — inserting a false instance into a true category — and it poses distinct challenges for both automated detection systems and individual cognitive evaluation (Lewandowsky, Ecker, Seifert, Schwarz & Cook, 2012, "Misinformation and Its Correction," *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 13(3), 106–131).

The McEntire/Fox claim is epistemically injected into the true category of "artists objecting to their music's use by political media entities." Because the category is real and well-populated, the false instance benefits from the credibility of its neighbors. Fact-checkers cannot simply say "this kind of thing never happens," because this kind of thing does happen. The assessment must turn on sourcing specific to the specific claim — which requires greater cognitive investment than most social media readers will apply.

**Political motivation and identity expression.** Research on partisan sharing behaviour consistently finds that political identity is a stronger predictor of story sharing than accuracy assessment for politically congruent content (Pennycook & Rand, 2019, "Lazy, Not Biased: Susceptibility to Partisan Fake News Is Better Explained by Lack of Reasoning Than Motivated Reasoning," *Cognition*, 188, 39–50). A claim that Reba McEntire — a country star, Fox News–adjacent in audience demographics — stood up to Fox News carries extraordinarily high value as identity signal for liberal-leaning social media users. The sharing motivation is not primarily about conveying accurate information but about publicly aligning with a narrative of conservative-culture-world celebrities breaking from political expectation. This motivation is present regardless of the accuracy of the specific claim, which means accuracy assessment is not the primary evaluative operation being performed.

**McEntire's actual ambivalent public profile.** McEntire has genuinely navigated a complex public image. She has hosted the Academy of Country Music Awards, appeared in high-profile NBC programming (hosting a period of *The Voice*), and made various public statements that suggest her personal politics do not map cleanly onto the most conservative wing of her audience. This genuine ambivalence — a real feature of her public life — creates interpretive space for stories that present her as more politically oppositional than the evidence supports. She is a figure about whom the claim "she stood up to Fox" is *barely implausible*, which is precisely the vulnerability that fabrication exploits.


## 4. Verdict

**FALSE — fabricated, no factual basis.** Reba McEntire did not charge Fox News for using her music. There is no legal action, no invoice, no publicly documented dispute, no statement from McEntire, no statement from Fox News, and no coverage in entertainment industry outlets whose professional mandate would make such a dispute significant news. The claim originates in the fabricated celebrity-political-resistance content genre, travels through partisan social media networks as political identity expression, and benefits structurally from a true category (artists challenging media/political use of their music) into which it is inserted as a false instance.

The music licensing framework that governs broadcast television use of musical works makes the specific claim additionally implausible in its usual form: Fox News's blanket PRO licence would cover incidental use of McEntire's BMI-registered catalogue in most programming contexts, meaning the legal mechanism through which an "invoice" would be sent and justified by McEntire's team is not straightforward even if the political motivation were present.


## 5. The Wider Picture

This claim is one instance of a recognisable content category that media literacy researchers have begun to study systematically: fabricated celebrity political actions. The category shares structural features with political satire — it uses cultural celebrities as proxies for political positions and frames their imagined actions as endorsement of viewer politics — but presents itself as factual news rather than satire, relying on credibility-suggestive design rather than clearly marked satirical intent.

The genre is particularly active around country music and its associated media ecosystem because country music occupies a specific contested cultural position in American political life. The genre's audience demographics skew older, rural, and Republican, but its performer demographics are more diverse in political views than audience assumptions require, and a number of country artists have publicly navigated tension between audience expectations and their own positions. This makes country artists particularly appealing vehicles for celebrity-resistance fabrication: the stories carry higher surprise value (and therefore more sharing motivation) precisely because country artists are presumed to be culturally conservative, while the genuine political diversity within the performer community makes the stories not obviously implausible.

The Reba McEntire/Fox News fabrication should be read alongside similar false claims involving Dolly Parton, Garth Brooks, Willie Nelson, and other country figures that have circulated in the same media ecosystem and served the same identity-signalling function. None of these were accurate; all of them drew on real features of the artists' public personas to generate surface plausibility.

From a structural media perspective, the proliferation of this content genre reveals the inadequacy of simple source verification as a media literacy tool. The instruction "check your sources" is insufficient when the source is designed to mimic credible journalism. The more productive media literacy intervention involves recognition of story templates and their function — asking what political or emotional goal the story serves, and whether its spread is explained by its informational value or its identity-signalling value. When sharing motivation is primarily expressive rather than informational, accuracy assessment tends to drop out of the process entirely, and the informational ecosystem degrades regardless of whether individual users are "fooled."

Performing rights organisations and entertainment law scholars have noted that the public misunderstanding of music licensing is itself an enabling condition for this type of fabrication. Most social media users do not know that broadcast blanket licences exist or that artists' individual consent is not required for most television uses of their music. This knowledge gap makes the "invoice" or "cease-and-desist" framing in these stories feel emotionally satisfying and legally coherent when it is neither (Kohn & Kohn, 2010, *Music Licensing*).


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated the claim FALSE and published a detailed assessment tracing the claim to politically inflected fabrication websites (Snopes, 2022). The PolitiFact and AFP Fact Check networks have assessed similar claims in the general celebrity-vs-Fox-News category and identified recurring source patterns. The detailed assessment methodology for this claim type involves: (1) identifying the specific first publication source and establishing whether it is a satirical or fabrication-oriented outlet; (2) searching entertainment industry trade publications (Variety, Billboard, Rolling Stone, The Hollywood Reporter) for coverage of any legal dispute, since copyright claims by major artists against major broadcasters are routinely covered by these outlets; (3) checking court filing databases such as PACER for any federal copyright litigation; (4) reviewing the artist's management and PR representatives' communications; (5) reviewing the artist's own public statements and social media for any reference to the alleged dispute; (6) assessing the claim against the legal framework for music licensing in broadcast media.

This claim fails all five of the last five steps. The only step it partially passes is the first: the structural plausibility of the template is real.

Media literacy educators who encounter this claim should use it to illustrate the distinction between structural plausibility and evidential support — a core epistemological distinction that is underemphasised in most general-audience media literacy curricula. The claim feels plausible because the category is real. Evaluating the specific claim requires abandoning that structural ease and demanding specific evidence: not "could this happen?" but "did this happen, and how would we know?" The answer to the second question — entertainment trade coverage, court records, artist statements, management confirmation — is entirely negative, which settles the matter regardless of how satisfying the story would be if it were true.
