# "Red Cars Get More Speeding Tickets"


## 1. The Claim

The claim asserts that red-colored automobiles receive disproportionately more speeding tickets compared to vehicles of other colors, implying a systematic bias in traffic enforcement that correlates vehicle color with citation rates. This belief typically encompasses the idea that red cars either attract more police attention due to their visibility and perceived association with aggressive driving, or that red car owners exhibit more risk-taking driving behaviors that result in higher violation rates.

The claim appears in multiple variants across automotive culture, insurance discussions, and popular wisdom about vehicle purchasing decisions. Some versions focus on police officer psychology—suggesting that red cars "stand out" and thus draw enforcement attention. Other versions emphasize driver psychology—proposing that people who choose red cars are inherently more aggressive or speed-prone drivers.

The assertion often extends beyond pure speeding violations to include general traffic citations, accident rates, or insurance claims. It intersects with broader beliefs about color psychology, personality-vehicle choice correlations, and the relationship between aesthetic preferences and behavioral tendencies.

The claim carries practical implications for car buyers who may avoid red vehicles based on perceived enforcement risks, and for insurance companies who might theoretically incorporate color data into risk assessment models if the correlation proved valid.


## 2. What's Actually True

To evaluate this claim requires examining both traffic enforcement data and color psychology research. The fundamental question is whether any measurable correlation exists between vehicle color and citation rates, and if so, whether this correlation reflects enforcement bias, driver behavior differences, or statistical artifacts.

**Traffic Enforcement Data Analysis:**

Large-scale traffic enforcement studies have not identified significant correlations between vehicle color and citation rates when controlling for other variables. The most comprehensive analyses come from insurance industry data, which tracks both claims and violations across millions of vehicles with detailed demographic and vehicle characteristic information.

A 2017 study by the Australian insurer AAMI analyzed accident and claim data across vehicle colors and found that silver cars were most commonly involved in accidents, not red vehicles. While this study focused on accidents rather than citations, it provides insight into the relationship between color and driving incidents more broadly.

American insurance industry data, while not typically published in detail due to competitive considerations, generally does not identify vehicle color as a significant risk factor. Major insurers like State Farm, Allstate, and Progressive have indicated in various public statements that color is not among the factors they consider in premium calculations, suggesting absence of meaningful correlation with claims or violation patterns.

**Police Enforcement Psychology:**

Traffic enforcement operates under complex cognitive and practical constraints that affect citation patterns. Police officers must make rapid attention and pursuit decisions while processing multiple visual and behavioral cues simultaneously. While color perception certainly factors into vehicle identification and tracking, professional training emphasizes behavior-based enforcement over aesthetic characteristics.

Modern traffic enforcement increasingly relies on radar, lidar, and automated systems that detect speed violations independent of visual color assessment. These technological approaches would eliminate any potential color bias in initial violation detection, though officer discretion still affects citation decisions after violations are identified.

**Visibility and Attention Research:**

Color psychology research provides some theoretical basis for believing red vehicles might attract more attention. Red is associated with urgency, aggression, and heightened arousal across cultures. However, attention-grabbing properties don't necessarily translate to enforcement bias—they might equally result in more cautious driving around red vehicles or enhanced visibility that actually prevents violations.

Automotive visibility studies focus primarily on safety rather than enforcement implications. Research consistently shows that certain colors (bright yellow, white, silver) provide better visibility under various lighting and weather conditions, but these findings don't correlate with enforcement attention patterns.

**Vehicle Choice Psychology:**

Some research suggests correlations between personality traits and color preferences, with red sometimes associated with extroversion, risk-taking, or assertiveness. However, these psychological associations are typically weak, culturally variable, and complicated by practical vehicle choice factors like resale value, availability, and fashion trends.

The automotive market includes numerous variables that affect color distribution: manufacturer color options vary by model and year, certain colors command premium prices, cultural preferences shift over time, and practical considerations like heat absorption in warm climates influence choices independent of personality factors.


The epistemological foundation of this claim appears to rest primarily on intuitive associations rather than empirical evidence. The belief draws strength from several psychological and cultural factors that create apparent plausibility without requiring actual correlation.

**Cultural Color Associations:**

Red carries strong symbolic associations with speed, danger, aggression, and attention-seeking across many cultures. These associations make red car-speeding ticket correlations feel intuitively "obvious" even without supporting data. The cultural narrative around red as an aggressive, risk-taking color predisposes people to expect behavioral correlations that may not exist in practice.

**Automotive Marketing Influences:**

Car manufacturers and marketers have long exploited red-speed associations in advertising high-performance vehicles. This commercial messaging reinforces cultural connections between red color and driving excitement, creating marketing-driven expectations about red car owner behavior that may not reflect actual driving patterns.

**Selective Attention and Memory:**

The claim benefits from selective attention mechanisms that make confirming instances more memorable than disconfirming ones. People are more likely to notice, remember, and discuss instances where red cars receive citations, while overlooking the vastly larger number of citation-free red car encounters or non-red car violations.

**Social Transmission:**

The claim's persistence reflects effective social transmission characteristics—it's simple, memorable, connects to existing cultural knowledge (color symbolism), and provides actionable guidance (avoid red cars if concerned about tickets). These factors facilitate cultural reproduction independent of empirical accuracy.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The lack of empirical evidence supporting red car citation bias is given the massive scale of traffic enforcement data collection. Modern police departments maintain detailed digital records of citations including vehicle characteristics, violation types, locations, and officer information. If a meaningful correlation existed between red vehicles and citation rates, this pattern would be readily detectable in routine data analysis.

Insurance companies possess even more comprehensive datasets linking vehicle characteristics to claims, violations, and driver demographics across millions of policyholders. The competitive advantage of identifying genuine risk factors means insurers have strong incentives to discover and utilize any valid correlations, including color-based patterns if they existed.

The absence of color-based pricing in insurance markets provides strong indirect evidence against systematic color-violation correlations. Insurance companies routinely adjust pricing based on much smaller risk factors—vehicle make, model, year, engine size, safety features—making it unlikely they would ignore color-based correlations if they were statistically significant.

**Confirmation Bias Dynamics:**

The persistence of the red car belief despite lack of supporting data illustrates several cognitive biases that affect folk wisdom formation. Confirmation bias leads people to notice and remember instances that support existing beliefs while overlooking contradictory evidence. Seeing a red sports car receiving a speeding ticket reinforces the association, while observing red cars driving normally or non-red cars getting tickets doesn't challenge the belief structure.

Availability heuristic effects also apply—dramatic or unusual events (like seeing a flashy red car pulled over) create stronger memories than routine occurrences, leading to overestimation of the correlation frequency. The mental association between red color and speed/aggression primes attention toward instances that seem to confirm the connection.

**Statistical Confounding:**

Even if some correlation existed between red cars and citations, multiple confounding variables would complicate causal interpretation. Red vehicles might be disproportionately represented among sports cars, luxury vehicles, or other categories that independently correlate with different driving patterns. Driver demographics, income levels, age distributions, and geographic concentrations could all create spurious correlations between color and enforcement outcomes.

The sports car factor deserves particular attention—if red color is more common among high-performance vehicles that actually do receive more citations due to their capabilities and typical usage patterns, this could create apparent color-citation correlation that actually reflects vehicle type rather than color bias.


## 4. Verdict

**Classification: REFUTED**

**Gap Category: Phenomenologically Real**

The claim that red cars receive more speeding tickets lacks empirical support from traffic enforcement data, insurance industry analysis, or systematic research into citation patterns. Despite widespread cultural belief in this correlation, no credible evidence demonstrates that vehicle color significantly affects citation rates when controlling for other variables.

The "phenomenologically real" classification reflects the authentic experiential basis for the belief—people do observe red cars receiving tickets, red color does carry cultural associations with speed and aggression, and these observations create genuine subjective experiences that feel like evidence for the claim. The gap exists between folk pattern recognition and statistical reality.

The claim's persistence demonstrates how cognitive biases, cultural associations, and selective attention can create and sustain beliefs about correlations that don't exist in systematic data. The combination of confirmation bias, availability heuristic effects, and intuitive color-behavior associations creates a compelling narrative that operates independently of empirical verification.

Insurance industry practices provide the strongest evidence against meaningful color-citation correlations. The competitive insurance market's failure to incorporate color-based risk factors indicates that such correlations, if they exist, are too weak to provide useful predictive value for actual risk assessment.

While some individual police officers might subjectively notice red cars more than other colors, modern traffic enforcement data shows no systematic bias translating to measurable citation rate differences. Professional training, legal constraints, and technological enforcement tools minimize the potential for color-based discrimination in citation decisions.

The most accurate conclusion is that vehicle color has no meaningful correlation with citation rates, and car buyers need not consider enforcement implications when choosing vehicle colors. Other factors—vehicle type, driving behavior, maintenance quality, and driver demographics—provide much stronger correlations with both citation risk and insurance costs than aesthetic characteristics like color.

The educational value of examining this claim lies in understanding how cultural associations and psychological biases can create persistent beliefs about correlations that don't exist in systematic data, and how competitive markets can serve as empirical tests for folk wisdom about risk factors.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The red car citation claim exists within a broader ecosystem of automotive folklore that includes beliefs about vehicle characteristics and their behavioral or enforcement implications. Similar claims persist about sports cars, luxury vehicles, certain brands, and other visible vehicle attributes that supposedly attract police attention or correlate with driver behavior.

**Insurance Industry Perspective:**

The insurance industry's sophisticated risk modeling provides a useful benchmark for evaluating automotive correlation claims. Insurers have identified genuine risk factors including vehicle type, driver age, geographic location, credit scores, and driving history. The absence of color-based pricing in competitive insurance markets suggests that vehicle color doesn't provide meaningful predictive value for risk assessment.

This market test is particularly compelling because insurance companies have both the data and financial incentives to identify any genuine correlation patterns. The failure of color-based risk factors to emerge in competitive insurance markets provides strong evidence against their practical significance.

**Law Enforcement Training and Protocols:**

Modern police training emphasizes behavior-based enforcement focused on observable violations rather than vehicle characteristics that don't directly indicate rule violations. Professional law enforcement standards discourage profiling based on vehicle aesthetics, both for legal liability reasons and because such approaches reduce enforcement effectiveness.

Traffic enforcement efficiency depends on officers' ability to identify actual violations accurately and quickly. Focusing on vehicle color rather than speed, driving behavior, or other violation indicators would reduce citation accuracy and potentially expose departments to discrimination liability.

**Color Psychology Research:**

Academic color psychology research provides mixed and culturally variable results regarding personality-color associations. While some studies find weak correlations between color preferences and certain traits, these associations are typically much weaker than commonly believed and heavily influenced by cultural context, individual experience, and situational factors.

The gap between laboratory color psychology findings and real-world behavioral predictions is substantial. Even when studies find statistical associations between color preferences and personality measures, these correlations rarely translate to meaningful behavioral predictions in complex real-world contexts like driving.

**Automotive Industry Data:**

Vehicle manufacturers maintain extensive data on color preferences, sales patterns, and customer demographics that could potentially reveal color-behavior correlations if they existed. The automotive industry's failure to emphasize color-behavior connections in their marketing and research suggests absence of strong empirical patterns.

Manufacturer safety research focuses intensively on color visibility and accident prevention, areas where color genuinely affects outcomes. The contrast between evidence-based color safety research and absent color-behavior research highlights the difference between genuine correlations and folk beliefs.

**International Comparison:**

Cross-cultural analysis provides additional perspective on color-citation claims. Different countries have varying color preferences, traffic enforcement practices, and cultural color associations. If red car citation bias were a genuine psychological phenomenon, it should appear consistently across different enforcement environments. The lack of international documentation of this pattern further undermines the claim's validity.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly addressed the specific claim about red cars and speeding tickets, though they have examined various automotive myths and traffic enforcement claims. The absence of coverage may reflect the claim's position as a widely-believed folk wisdom that hasn't reached the threshold for systematic fact-checking attention, despite its persistence in popular culture.

The lack of mainstream debunking allows the claim to circulate freely in automotive communities, insurance discussions, and car purchasing decisions without confronting rigorous empirical analysis. This creates an environment where anecdotal evidence and confirmation bias can sustain the belief without systematic challenge.
