# "Red Cars Get More Speeding Tickets / Higher Insurance Rates": A Snopes+ Review

## 1. Claim Statement

The persistent automotive folklore asserts that red cars attract disproportionate police attention for traffic violations, resulting in more speeding tickets and higher insurance premiums. This belief appears in car buying guides, insurance discussions, and automotive culture generally, often accompanied by anecdotal evidence and folk theories about police psychology. The claim typically includes two components: enforcement bias (police target red cars) and insurance discrimination (companies charge higher premiums for red vehicles).

The myth's cultural reach is notable. It surfaces in dealership negotiations, where buyers are warned against red paint; in internet forums where new drivers solicit color advice; and in casual conversation as received wisdom so entrenched that many people never question its factual basis. A 2015 survey by Insure.com found that roughly 44 percent of American drivers believed red cars cost more to insure — a proportion suggesting the myth functions as a cultural default assumption rather than a fringe belief.

## 2. Snopes Assessment

While various automotive websites and consumer guides have addressed aspects of this claim, systematic fact-checking attention has been limited despite the belief's widespread circulation in car-buying culture. The myth persists in part because it seems intuitively plausible — red suggests speed and aggression — while the actual evidence remains scattered across traffic enforcement studies, insurance industry practices, and behavioral psychology research.

Snopes itself has not published a dedicated entry on the red car claim, likely because it falls into a category of "common knowledge" myths that lack a single viral source or event to debunk. Unlike conspiracy theories or specific political claims, the red car myth is diffuse — propagated through word of mouth and cultural repetition rather than traceable misinformation campaigns. This diffusion makes it simultaneously harder to pin down and more resistant to correction.

## 3. Claim Fidelity Audit

**Traffic Enforcement Evidence:** The most comprehensive examination comes from Redelmeier and Raza's 2017 study published in the *British Medical Journal*, analyzing a large Canadian dataset of traffic violations. Using administrative records from Ontario covering multiple years and thousands of citations, the researchers found no statistically significant association between vehicle color and citation frequency when controlling for other variables. Importantly, their dataset was large enough to detect even small effects, and the confidence intervals excluded meaningful differences between color categories.

Earlier, a 2007 study by Lum and Reagan at the University of Virginia examined Virginia State Police citation records and similarly found no statistically significant relationship between vehicle color and the likelihood of receiving a traffic citation. Their analysis controlled for vehicle type, time of day, and driver demographics, reaching the same null conclusion through an independent dataset and methodology.

**Insurance Industry Practices:** Major insurance companies explicitly state that vehicle color is not a rating factor in premium calculations. State insurance commissioners regulate the factors insurers can legally use for pricing, and vehicle color does not appear on approved rating schedules. Insurance premiums are determined by make, model, year, engine size, safety ratings, repair costs, theft rates, driver age, driving record, location, and coverage levels — but not paint color. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) maintains a standardized set of permissible rating variables, and color has never appeared among them.

**Regulatory Framework:** Insurance pricing is heavily regulated at the state level, with companies required to justify rating factors through actuarial data demonstrating correlation with claim frequency or severity. No insurance company has ever submitted actuarial evidence supporting color-based pricing differentials, nor would such evidence be likely to gain regulatory approval given anti-discrimination principles. The Vehicle Identification Number (VIN), which insurers use to identify vehicles, does not encode paint color — a technical fact that makes color-based pricing structurally impossible within existing systems.

**Methodological Challenges:** Studying color-citation correlations requires controlling for numerous confounding variables. Red sports cars may indeed receive more tickets, but this correlation likely reflects the intersection of performance capability, driver demographics, and driving behavior rather than color perception by police officers. Properly isolating the color variable requires comparing citation rates for identical makes and models differing only in color — a design that demands very large datasets to achieve statistical power.

**International Consistency:** The absence of color-citation correlation appears consistent across different jurisdictions and traffic enforcement systems, suggesting the finding is not specific to particular police training protocols or cultural contexts but reflects fundamental aspects of traffic enforcement practice. Australian road safety research, including analyses of crash and citation data from New South Wales, has independently confirmed that vehicle color shows no meaningful relationship to enforcement actions when vehicle type and driver characteristics are controlled.

## 4. Review Epistemology

**Psychological Color Theory:** The intuitive plausibility of the red car myth draws from legitimate psychological research on color associations. Red is consistently associated with danger, aggression, speed, and attention-seeking behavior across cultures. Elliot and Niesta's 2008 research documented measurable psychological effects of red exposure on perception of dominance and threat. Mehta and Zhu (2009), writing in *Science*, demonstrated that red enhances attention to detail and avoidance motivation — findings that suggest red genuinely captures attention, even if this attentional capture does not translate into biased enforcement behavior in professional policing contexts.

**Confirmation Bias Mechanism:** The myth persists through selective attention and memory biases. People who expect red cars to receive more tickets may notice red vehicles being stopped while overlooking stops involving other colors, creating subjective confirmation of the belief despite objective data showing no correlation. This is a textbook demonstration of Nickerson's (1998) framework on confirmation bias: once a hypothesis is adopted, observations that support it are encoded and recalled preferentially, while contradictory observations are discounted or forgotten.

**Demographic Confounds:** Red car preferences may correlate with demographics that independently affect citation risk. Young males — who statistically receive more traffic citations — may disproportionately choose red vehicles, particularly in sports car categories. This demographic correlation could create an appearance of color-based enforcement when the actual correlation involves age, gender, and risk tolerance. J.D. Power color preference data consistently shows that red is over-represented among sports car and performance vehicle buyers relative to its share of the overall fleet.

**Vehicle Type Correlation:** Sports cars and performance vehicles are disproportionately available in red, and these vehicle types do attract more police attention due to their speed capability and the driving behavior they enable. The correlation between red color and citations may actually reflect the correlation between performance vehicles and enforcement attention. A red Honda Civic and a beige Honda Civic, driven identically, would not generate different enforcement responses — but the red Civic is more memorable when stopped.

**Police Training Reality:** Traffic enforcement training focuses on observable violations (speed, erratic driving, equipment violations) rather than vehicle aesthetics. Police officers are trained to use radar, observation of driving behavior, and response to specific violations as enforcement triggers, not vehicle color assessment. Interviews with law enforcement professionals, including those published in *Police Quarterly*, consistently indicate that speed differential, driving pattern, and equipment condition drive stop decisions — not paint color.

## 5. Conclusion Epistemology

**Actual Citation Risk Factors:** Empirical research identifies the genuine factors that increase traffic citation risk: vehicle speed capability (sports cars, motorcycles), driver demographics (young males), time of day (late night, rush hour), location (construction zones, known speed traps), and behavioral indicators (erratic driving, visible violations). The Highway Loss Data Institute's annual reports quantify these relationships with precision, showing that vehicle type and driver profile explain the vast majority of citation variance.

**Insurance Pricing Transparency:** Modern insurance pricing involves sophisticated actuarial modeling using vast datasets to identify genuine risk correlators. Companies compete on accurate risk assessment, making them unlikely to ignore profitable pricing opportunities if color genuinely predicted claim frequency. The absence of color-based pricing reflects actuarial evidence that color lacks predictive value. Insurers use approximately 30 to 50 rating variables in their models; color is not among them because decades of loss data show no signal.

**Cultural Perpetuation Mechanisms:** The myth perpetuates through automotive folklore, car dealership conversations, and insurance discussions where personal anecdotes carry more weight than statistical evidence. The belief becomes self-reinforcing as people who choose non-red cars may attribute their clean driving records to their color choice rather than their actual driving behavior. Dealership salespeople may repeat the myth without malice, simply passing along what they themselves were told, creating an intergenerational transmission of false belief within the automotive industry itself.

**Technological Evolution:** Modern traffic enforcement increasingly relies on automated systems (speed cameras, red light cameras, average-speed zone cameras) that respond to behavior rather than vehicle characteristics, further reducing any theoretical opportunity for color-based bias. These systems photograph violations without human color perception influencing enforcement decisions. As automated enforcement expands — the UK alone operates over 7,000 speed cameras — the structural opportunity for color-based bias shrinks toward zero.

**Research Methodology Requirements:** Proper study of color-citation correlations requires large datasets, careful control for confounding variables, and analysis of citation rates per vehicle-mile traveled by color. Most anecdotal evidence lacks these methodological requirements, making personal observations unreliable for detecting genuine patterns.

## 6. The Wider Field

**Automotive Marketing Psychology:** Car manufacturers and marketers understand color psychology's influence on consumer behavior. Red vehicles are often marketed to emphasize performance, excitement, and boldness, potentially attracting buyers who value these characteristics and may drive more aggressively. This marketing-behavior correlation could contribute to the myth's persistence. These associations reinforce the cultural link between red and speed even as they have no bearing on enforcement outcomes.

**Cultural Color Coding:** The association between red and speed/danger appears across cultures and extends beyond vehicles to warning signs, emergency equipment, and sports uniforms. This universal color coding reflects deep psychological responses that make the red car myth seem intuitively reasonable despite lacking empirical support. Hill and Barton's 2005 study in *Nature* found that athletes wearing red won significantly more combat sport bouts, suggesting red genuinely signals dominance in competitive contexts — but traffic enforcement is not a competitive context, and officers respond to instruments, not impressions.

**Law Enforcement Technology:** Modern police vehicles are equipped with radar guns, LIDAR units, automatic license plate readers, and computer systems that prioritize data-driven enforcement over visual assessment. These technological tools reduce officer discretion based on subjective vehicle characteristics while improving enforcement effectiveness and consistency.

**Insurance Industry Evolution:** The insurance industry has moved toward usage-based insurance (UBI) using telematics devices that monitor actual driving behavior — speed, braking patterns, mileage, time of day — rather than proxy variables. Progressive's Snapshot, State Farm's Drive Safe & Save, and similar programs represent a structural shift toward direct behavior measurement that makes color-based pricing even less relevant than historical approaches. By 2025, an estimated 20 percent of U.S. auto insurance policies incorporated some telematics component.

**Consumer Decision Making:** Understanding the myth's falseness can improve car purchasing decisions by allowing buyers to choose colors based on personal preference, resale value considerations, or practical factors (heat absorption, visibility, maintenance requirements) rather than imaginary enforcement or insurance implications.

**Visibility and Safety Research:** Legitimate research on vehicle color focuses on safety implications — the visibility of different colors in various lighting and weather conditions. Newstead and D'Elia's 2010 study from the Monash University Accident Research Centre found that white and yellow vehicles were associated with lower crash risk due to superior daytime visibility, while dark-colored vehicles (black, blue, dark green) showed slightly elevated risk. Red vehicles fell in the middle range — more visible than dark colors but less than white. This is the actual safety conversation about vehicle color, and it has nothing to do with tickets or insurance premiums.

**Statistical Literacy Applications:** The red car myth serves as an excellent case study in statistical reasoning, demonstrating how correlation can be confused with causation, how confounding variables can create misleading patterns, and how personal experience can contradict systematic evidence. It is the kind of claim that feels true precisely because it aligns with genuine psychological effects (red captures attention) while misapplying those effects to a domain (professional law enforcement) where they do not operate as intuition predicts.

**Behavioral Economics Implications:** The myth illustrates how availability heuristics and representativeness bias influence consumer beliefs about risk. People may overestimate citation risks for red cars because red sports car stops are more memorable than routine stops involving beige sedans, creating skewed risk perception. Kahneman and Tversky's foundational work on heuristic biases provides the theoretical scaffold: when asked "do red cars get more tickets?", people do not consult base rates — they consult the vividness of their mental imagery.

## 7. Snopes+ Verdict: **CONFIRMED**

The systematic evidence confirms the null finding with high confidence: no credible data supports either component of the red car myth. Redelmeier and Raza's large-scale Canadian study provides the most rigorous evidence, but their findings align with independent analyses (Lum and Reagan 2007; Australian crash data), insurance industry practices, regulatory frameworks, and the structural impossibility of color-based insurance pricing given VIN-based identification systems.

The confirmation emphasizes that this represents a robust null finding rather than mere absence of evidence. Multiple independent approaches — traffic citation analysis, insurance actuarial data, regulatory examination, and law enforcement procedure review — converge on the same conclusion: vehicle color does not influence citation frequency or insurance pricing. The convergence across methodologies, jurisdictions, and enforcement systems elevates this from a single-study null result to a well-triangulated empirical consensus.

However, the confirmation also expands understanding of why the myth persists despite contrary evidence. The psychological plausibility of red-danger associations, demographic confounds between red car ownership and high-risk driver categories, the correlation between red availability in sports cars and performance vehicle citation rates, and the operation of confirmation bias in everyday observation create multiple reinforcing pathways for false correlation perception. Together they form a self-sustaining belief ecosystem.

The broader implications extend beyond automotive folklore into statistical reasoning and consumer decision making. Understanding the true factors that influence citation risk — vehicle capability, driver demographics, time and location patterns, actual driving behavior — provides actionable knowledge for reducing traffic enforcement encounters while making informed vehicle purchasing decisions.

The myth's persistence also illustrates how intuitive theories based on psychological associations can override empirical evidence in popular belief systems. The red car case demonstrates the importance of distinguishing between psychologically plausible explanations and statistically validated correlations, particularly in domains where personal financial decisions (car purchases, insurance choices) may be influenced by unfounded beliefs about risk relationships.
