# Attempted Tomato Assassination of George Washington

## §1 — Claim & Verdict

The claim in question holds that during the Revolutionary War period, a cook loyal to the British Crown attempted to assassinate General George Washington by feeding him a stew or dish containing tomatoes, which the assassin believed to be poisonous members of the deadly nightshade family. In most versions of the story, the cook subsequently committed suicide upon discovering that Washington had not died — some versions attribute the failed assassination to divine protection, others to the cook's shock at Washington's survival. The story travels in first-person form, purportedly transcribed from a suicide note or confession letter written by the cook.

Snopes rates this claim **FALSE**. The story is not history. It is fiction — specifically, it is a misremembered short story published by Richard M. Gordon entitled "The Murder of George Washington" in the April 1959 issue of *Ellery Queen's Mystery Magazine* (Gordon, 1959). Those who encountered the story in school or through other channels retained accurate memory of most of its plot elements — the cook, the stew with tomatoes, the suicide — but lost the crucial contextual detail that they were reading genre fiction in a mystery magazine, not a historical document. The story appears historically plausible to modern readers because it invokes a real widespread belief: that colonial Americans feared tomatoes as poisonous. But this belief is itself doubtfully founded, and the specific assassination narrative has no documentary basis in Washington's historical record.

## §2 — Origins & Circulation

The circulation pattern for this claim is among the most pedagogically interesting in the Snopes archive, because it involves **school-classroom transmission**. Multiple independent accounts of the story's circulation invoke a junior-high or high-school history teacher reading the piece aloud as an example. One Snopes correspondent wrote: "I remember one of my junior high history teachers reading us a suicide note by George Washington's cook." Another recalled: "I ever tell you guys the story of the French chef who tried to poison George Washington with a tomato stew?"

This pattern suggests that Gordon's 1959 mystery story was reprinted or excerpted in educational anthologies or classroom readers — a practice common in mid-20th-century American language-arts instruction, where short fiction appearing in magazines was routinely collected into textbook collections without detailed attribution or genre labeling. When students encounter a piece through a teacher's classroom reading, the implicit framing is that the reading is pedagogically significant — history, literature, or cultural instruction. The generic framing of a mystery magazine is lost entirely.

What persists is the narrative content: a first-person letter from a cook, confessing to a failed assassination attempt using a tomato stew prepared for Washington. Gordon's story presents "the scarlet flesh of a fruit of a variety of the deadly nightshade" as the would-be weapon, and the cook's confidence in its lethality is portrayed as historically plausible. The postscript to the fictional letter states that the cook will take his own life, adding — in a darkly comic touch suited to the mystery genre — "As a cook, I have a prejudice against dying by poison; I am too corpulent to hang; but by reason of my calling, I am expert with a carving knife."

This is an exceptionally well-crafted piece of historical pastiche, which likely explains both its classroom adoption and its epistemic stickiness. Gordon's prose is convincing in period register, the setup exploits genuine historical anxieties about tomatoes, and the narrative has the structural features of authenticity — a first-person confessional document, a specific historical figure, a plausible motivation (Loyalist anger at Washington's treason against the Crown).

The claim was sufficiently widespread by 2013 that Snopes investigated it in earnest, sourcing the original April 1959 issue of *Ellery Queen's Mystery Magazine* and identifying Gordon's story as the origin point.

## §3 — Epistemic Analysis: The Classroom as Decontextualization Engine

This case illustrates a specific and undertheorized mode of knowledge distortion that might be termed **institutional decontextualization** — the process by which material transmitted through authoritative-seeming institutional channels (schools, teachers, curated anthologies) is stripped of its original generic and source context, causing readers to misassign it to a different epistemic category.

The classic philosophical vocabulary for this phenomenon comes from the epistemology of testimony. Coady (1992, *Testimony: A Philosophical Study*, Clarendon Press) argued that testimony is a basic and largely reliable source of knowledge, but emphasized that the reliability of transmitted testimony depends on the preservation of source-tracing links. When those links are broken — when a student hears a story read by a teacher in a history class without genre attribution — the testimony is epistemically malformed. The listener has no mechanism to evaluate the claim against its originating source.

What is epistemically novel in this case is the role of **plausibility scaffolding**. Gordon's story is not a claim that looks obviously wrong. It works because it is plausible. And the feature that makes it plausible — the colonial-era fear of tomatoes as poisonous — is itself only dubiously founded. The story thus operates as a double-layered plausibility trap: the assassination narrative gains credibility from the purported historical fact that colonials feared tomatoes, while the belief that colonials feared tomatoes gains strength from repeated narrative instances like Gordon's story itself.

Snopes addresses both layers directly. The tomato fear is real in popular cultural memory but considerably overstated in the historical record. Tomatoes are native to South America and were adopted into European cookery during the 16th century; Thomas Jefferson grew them in his Monticello garden; North American consumption in the Revolutionary period was regional rather than absent. The claim that colonials universally viewed tomatoes as deadly poison is itself a persistent semi-myth, as documented by food historian Andrew F. Smith in *The Tomato in America: Early History, Culture, and Cookery* (Smith, 1994, University of South Carolina Press). The tomato's membership in the Solanaceae family — which includes deadly nightshade (*Atropa belladonna*), henbane (*Hyoscyamus niger*), and mandrake (*Mandragora officinarum*) — did generate some European suspicion, but the claim of near-universal colonial American terror of tomatoes is an oversimplification.

The Colonel Robert Gibbon Johnson story provides an instructive parallel sub-case. Folk history records that Johnson stood before a courthouse in Salem, New Jersey, in 1820 and publicly ate a quantity of tomatoes to demonstrate their safety, while a band played a funeral dirge. While a real Colonel Johnson existed and served in the New Jersey State Assembly and as president of the Salem Horticultural Society, there is no mention of the tomato-eating exhibition in any of his contemporaneous writings. The episode first appeared in Joseph Sickler's *The History of Salem County* (1937), apparently as a piece of local color that the author introduced without sourcing. The Johnson story, the Washington assassination story, and the general colonial-tomato-fear narrative are therefore mutually reinforcing components of a folk history that amplifies each other's apparent credibility.

## §4 — Scientific & Technical Evidence

The **toxicology of the nightshade family** (Solanaceae) provides the rational core for why the tomato was historically suspected. The family encompasses an extraordinary range of biochemical properties. *Atropa belladonna* (deadly nightshade) contains tropane alkaloids including atropine, scopolamine, and hyoscyamine, which are genuinely lethal in moderate doses through anticholinergic poisoning, characterized by tachycardia, hyperthermia, mydriasis, and eventually cardiovascular collapse (Krenzelok & Mrvos, 2011, *Clinical Toxicology*, 49(5), pp. 455–460). The tomato plant (*Solanum lycopersicum*) does contain glycoalkaloids — specifically alpha-tomatine and dehydrotomatine — primarily in its leaves, stems, and unripe fruit. These compounds have measurable biological activity.

However, the concentration of glycoalkaloids in ripe tomato fruit has been extensively characterized and found at levels far below toxicological significance in normal consumption. Maga (1980, *Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry*, 28(3), pp. 675–683) surveyed tomatine concentrations across tomato cultivars and plant tissues, finding that ripe fruit contains approximately 2–10 mg/kg, compared to 500–3000 mg/kg in leaves and stems. The estimated toxic dose of tomatine for humans has not been precisely established, but the compound is poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract compared to structurally related alkaloids and is rapidly conjugated by gut flora (Friedman, 2002, *Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry*, 50(22), pp. 6541–6553). Fried green tomatoes, green salsa, and other preparations using unripe tomatoes are therefore safe at normal culinary quantities, though green tomatoes carry meaningfully higher alkaloid loads than ripe ones.

The classification of the tomato as a **botanical member of the deadly nightshade family** is accurate in the sense that Solanum lycopersicum and Atropa belladonna are both Solanaceae, but this taxonomic relationship does not imply comparable toxicity any more than membership in the Rosaceae family makes apples, roses, and almonds toxicologically equivalent. Mid-18th-century natural philosophers were beginning to understand systematic botany through the Linnaean framework (first published in *Species Plantarum* in 1753), but the mapping of botanical classification onto toxicological risk was imprecise. The tomato's physical resemblance to *Atropa belladonna* berries — round, red or black, shiny — would have been visually suggestive to non-specialists, creating a reasonable heuristic for caution.

**George Washington's actual diet** and documented health during the Revolutionary War period is a topic with a substantial historical literature. Washington suffered periodic illnesses — smallpox, dysentery, recurrent respiratory infections — but no documented poisoning attempts appear in the surviving correspondence, orderly books, or post-war memoirs of the period. The standard biographical and documentary sources, including *The Papers of George Washington* (University of Virginia Press, ongoing), contain no reference to any cook attempting to use food as a weapon against Washington. This silence is not in itself conclusive — failed covert acts leave few records — but the claim would, if true, have generated survivor accounts, legal proceedings, or military correspondence that does not exist.

## §5 — The Gap: Source Provenance and the Fiction-History Boundary in Educational Transmission

This case's primary analytical gap is in the sociology and epistemology of **source provenance** in educational contexts. The central mechanism of the claim's persistence — classroom transmission by teachers using unattributed fiction — is almost certainly not unique to this story. The question of how often historical-seeming literary material is presented to students without adequate genre attribution, and how often that presentation generates durable false beliefs, is not well empirically characterized.

The cognitive science of **source monitoring** — the ability to correctly attribute memories to their originating source — is well developed. Johnson, Hashtroudi & Lindsay (1993, *Psychological Bulletin*, 114(1), pp. 3–28) established that source monitoring failures are systematically more frequent when: (1) the original acquisition context is not distinctive or salient, (2) the content fits pre-existing schemas, and (3) significant time has passed since encoding. All three conditions are met in the Washington tomato assassination case: a classroom reading is not a distinctive source-marking context; the story fits a colonial-era schema; and most people recalling it are doing so years or decades after their school exposure.

What is underdeveloped in the literature is a systematic account of how institutional authority (the teacher, the school, the textbook anthology) distorts source monitoring specifically. When information is transmitted by an authority figure in an educational setting, the authority provides an implicit endorsement that may be mis-attributed to the content rather than to the transmitter. Research on **epistemic authority attribution** in educational psychology (Hardman, 2019, *Educational Psychology Review*, 31(2), pp. 397–416) suggests that students regularly over-generalize the reliability of teacher-transmitted information across domains, including historical claim domains where the teacher is themselves relying on an unverified source.

No systematic audit of historical mis-remembering generated by classroom fiction transmission exists. Building such an audit would require: identifying literary magazine pieces that were anthologized into mid-20th-century educational collections, surveying adults of appropriate cohorts for their beliefs about specific historical claims appearing in such pieces, and testing whether those beliefs correlate with exposure to the original classroom context versus other sources. This is a tractable empirical program but one that has not been executed.

## §6 — Verdict Synthesis

The tomato assassination of George Washington is **unambiguously false as a historical claim**. It is a work of genre fiction by Richard M. Gordon, published in *Ellery Queen's Mystery Magazine* in April 1959, that was transmitted through educational channels with its fictional identity stripped away, creating a persistent belief in an event that never occurred.

The case is notable for illustrating a multi-layered epistemic problem: not merely "people are wrong about a historical fact" but "people are wrong because a reliable institutional context (school, teacher) was used to transmit a fictional claim without adequate attribution, and the claim was constructed to be maximally plausible by exploiting known cultural anxieties about the Solanaceae family." The fictional plausibility is itself grounded in historical semi-myths (universal colonial terror of tomatoes) that are themselves overstated.

EARC classification: **E (Established)** — the origin in genre fiction is identified and documented. Gap: **source-provenance** — the primary open research question concerns the mechanisms and frequency of classroom-mediated source-context loss in the transmission of historical claims.
