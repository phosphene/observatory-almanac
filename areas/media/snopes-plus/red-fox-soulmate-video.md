## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

On or around December 16, 2015, a Vine user operating under the handle "Miles Foxglove" uploaded a three-second video depicting two red foxes (*Vulpes vulpes*) vocalizing at each other in what appeared to be a captive or semi-captive setting. The clip was brief, noisy, and visually unremarkable—two foxes facing each other and producing a series of high-pitched growling and screaming sounds. Within days, the clip migrated to Tumblr, where an account using the name "Icelandic Stripper Boots" appended a caption that would transform the innocuous footage into a viral phenomenon: "fun fact! red foxes make this sound when they have meet their perfect mate or soul mate would you have it! so basically they're just screaming for all the other red foxes that they have found their love and for all the others to f*ck off."

The claim was entirely fabricated. The vocalizations shown in the video are consistent with agonistic or conflict-resolution behavior—specifically, ritualized aggression in which two foxes settle a territorial, dominance, or resource dispute through vocalizations and threat postures rather than physical combat. This behavioral interpretation was provided by the Tumblr blog "Why Animals Do The Thing," an account maintained by a wildlife educator and devoted specifically to debunking fabricated animal facts circulating on social media. The blog's analysis identified the interaction as conflict resolution: "This looks like conflict resolution in a tense situation to me. Actually fighting is really detrimental to most animals so anything except dire conflict is resolved with vocalizations and threat postures instead of actual aggression."

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2016) rated the claim **False**. The original Tumblr poster subsequently admitted that the "fun fact" had no factual basis, writing: "I apologies. I guess I got it mixed up... yeah I was just basing it off on the sounds, but I guess your point proves more fruitful." This admission—that the poster had invented the "soulmate" narrative based on the emotional quality of the sounds rather than any zoological knowledge—is itself analytically significant. The fabrication was not malicious disinformation but a spontaneous narrative generation triggered by the emotional resonance of the sounds, a process that reveals important dynamics about how folk-biological claims are born and propagated in social-media ecosystems.

The red fox soulmate claim belongs to a recognizable category of viral misinformation: fabricated animal "fun facts" that anthropomorphize animal behavior by mapping romantic or emotionally satisfying human narratives onto non-human behavioral repertoires. This entry examines the specific ethological evidence for red fox vocalizations, the mechanics of the Tumblr-to-viral pipeline that amplified the claim, and the epistemic structures that make fabricated folk-biology both easy to produce and difficult to correct.

## §2 The Tumblr-to-Viral Pipeline: How a Three-Second Vine Became a "Fun Fact"

The propagation path of the red fox soulmate claim illustrates a specific viral mechanic that was particularly potent in the 2014–2016 Tumblr ecosystem: the reblog-with-caption model. On Tumblr, any user could append commentary to a reblogged post, and subsequent rebloggers would see the accumulated caption chain as if it were a single unified text. This meant that a fabricated "fun fact" appended by one user would appear to later rebloggers as an integral part of the original post, with no visual distinction between the original content (the video) and the appended fabrication.

This architecture created a structural credibility inflation problem. A claim that originated from a single anonymous user with no credentials appeared, after multiple reblogs, as a piece of received wisdom endorsed by the apparent collective judgment of every reblogger in the chain. Marwick (2018), writing in *Journal of Communication* (Oxford University Press), documented this phenomenon as characteristic of platform-native misinformation: "the iterative nature of reblogging obscures the origin of claims, creating a form of distributed authorship that resists attribution and therefore resists correction." The red fox soulmate claim is a textbook case. By the time fact-checkers and ethology-literate users encountered it, the claim had been reblogged thousands of times, each reblog implicitly endorsing it.

The original platform—Vine—contributed a second structural factor. Vine's six-second maximum video length meant that the source footage contained almost no behavioral context. A three-second clip of two foxes vocalizing provides insufficient information for even an experienced ethologist to make a definitive behavioral interpretation without additional context (duration of the interaction, preceding and following behavior, setting, relationship between the individuals). This brevity created an epistemic vacuum that the fabricated caption filled. As Phillips and Milner (2017) argued in *The Ambivalent Internet* (Polity Press), short-form media content is particularly susceptible to narrative colonization because the content itself provides too little information to constrain interpretation.

The cross-platform migration from Vine to Tumblr also eliminated the original poster's context. On Vine, the video existed within the uploader's profile, which might have contained other fox-related content providing behavioral context. On Tumblr, it became a decontextualized clip—raw material awaiting narrative framing. Shifman (2014), in *Memes in Digital Culture* (MIT Press), documented that the most viral content tends to be content that is maximally ambiguous and therefore maximally receptive to user-generated reinterpretation: "The more open-ended the text, the greater the range of memetic derivatives it can spawn."

The intervention by "Why Animals Do The Thing" demonstrated both the promise and the limitations of community-based fact-checking in this environment. The correction was technically accurate, clearly worded, and arrived relatively quickly. But the correction faced an asymmetry documented by Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral (2018) in *Science* (American Association for the Advancement of Science): on social platforms, false claims spread faster, farther, and to more people than corrections, and this asymmetry is particularly pronounced for claims that evoke strong emotional responses. The "soulmate" claim was emotionally satisfying—romantic, cute, and surprising—while the correction ("ritualized aggression during conflict resolution") was scientically accurate but emotionally neutral. The correction could not compete on the dimension that mattered most for virality: affect.

The Tumblr "Icelandic Stripper Boots" account's retraction, when it came, was notable for its honesty about the fabrication mechanism. The poster admitted to "basing it off on the sounds"—that is, interpreting the vocalizations through an emotional lens shaped by human romantic categories and then presenting that interpretation as established zoological knowledge. This is not a unique cognitive failure but a predictable outcome of what psychologists call *intuitive anthropomorphism*: the spontaneous attribution of human mental states and motivations to non-human animals based on superficial behavioral cues (Epley, Waytz, & Cacioppo, 2007, *Psychological Review*, American Psychological Association).

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Anthropomorphism, Romantic Projection, and the "Cuteness Override"

The red fox soulmate claim is epistemically distinctive because the fabrication mechanism was not deliberate deception but spontaneous folk-biological inference driven by anthropomorphism. The poster heard two foxes screaming, interpreted the sounds as emotionally intense, mapped that intensity onto a romantic narrative framework, and then presented the result as factual. This production process—perception → emotional interpretation → anthropomorphic mapping → assertion as fact—represents a specific failure mode in lay biological reasoning that has been extensively documented in the cognitive science literature.

Anthropomorphism—the attribution of human characteristics to non-human entities—is one of the most robust and well-studied cognitive biases in human psychology. Epley, Waytz, and Cacioppo (2007) proposed a three-factor model in *Psychological Review* (American Psychological Association) arguing that anthropomorphism is triggered by (1) the availability of human-like perceptual cues (such as eyes, vocalizations, or facial expressions), (2) the desire for social connection (lonely or socially motivated individuals anthropomorphize more), and (3) the need to explain or predict behavior (anthropomorphism provides a ready-made explanatory framework). Fox vocalizations activate all three triggers: they are audibly similar to human screams, they occur in social contexts that invite narrative interpretation, and their causal structure is opaque to untrained listeners.

The specific variant of anthropomorphism operating in the soulmate claim is what Serpell (2003), writing in *The Animal Ethics Reader* (Routledge), termed "romantic projection"—the attribution of human romantic emotions and pair-bonding narratives to animal behavior. Romantic projection is not limited to social media; it is deeply embedded in Western cultural traditions of animal representation, from Aesop's fables to Disney films. But social media dramatically amplifies both the production and propagation of romantic projection because the reward structure of platforms—likes, reblogs, shares—selects for emotionally resonant claims over accurate ones.

A related cognitive phenomenon relevant to this case is what Herzog (2010), in *Some We Love, Some We Hate, Some We Eat* (Harper Perennial), described as the "cuteness override"—the tendency for perceived cuteness or emotional appeal of an animal to override critical evaluation of claims made about it. Foxes occupy a privileged position in the Western cultural imaginary as simultaneously wild and aesthetically appealing, and videos of foxes vocalizing are perceived as cute or emotionally moving by many viewers. This emotional response creates a motivated reasoning pathway: viewers *want* the soulmate claim to be true because it enhances the emotional payoff of the video.

The epistemic consequence is that corrections face not merely an informational deficit but an affective one. Correcting the claim requires not only providing accurate ethological information but also replacing an emotionally satisfying narrative with an emotionally neutral one—a substitution that the audience has no psychological motivation to accept. Lewandowsky, Ecker, Seifert, Schwarz, and Cook (2012), in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* (Association for Psychological Science), documented that corrections to emotionally valued misinformation are less effective than corrections to emotionally neutral misinformation, and that "the continued influence of misinformation" is particularly strong when the original claim served an identity or emotional function.

This dynamic is not unique to the soulmate claim. Fabricated animal "fun facts" constitute a distinct genre of social-media misinformation characterized by high emotional appeal, low verifiability by lay audiences, and strong resistance to correction. The genre includes claims about animal "funerals" (elephants, crows), animal "friendships" across species boundaries, and—most directly relevant here—animal "love" behaviors that mirror human romantic norms. Each of these claims follows the same epistemic pathway: genuine animal behavior is observed, anthropomorphically reinterpreted, and then presented as established fact with the emotional interpretation replacing the ethological one.

## §4 What Fox Vocalizations Actually Signal: Ethological Evidence

Red foxes (*Vulpes vulpes*) possess one of the most complex vocal repertoires of any canid species, producing at least 20 distinct call types documented in the scientific literature. Newton-Fisher, Harris, White, and Jones (1993), in a systematic study published in *Bioacoustics* (Taylor & Francis), catalogued the red fox vocal repertoire and identified calls ranging from low-frequency "wow-wow-wow" barks used in long-distance communication to high-pitched screams, gekkering (a rapid stuttering chatter), and a suite of whine and growl variants used in close-range social interactions.

The vocalizations visible in the Vine video—two foxes facing each other and producing high-pitched growling and screaming sounds—are most consistent with what the ethological literature identifies as "gekkering" or close-range agonistic vocalizations. Gekkering is a distinctive vocalization produced during close-proximity social encounters, particularly those involving conflict or dominance negotiation. Harris and White (1992), in *Mammal Review* (Wiley-Blackwell), described gekkering as "a rapid series of high-pitched, stuttering notes produced during aggressive or submissive encounters between foxes, typically at close range and often accompanied by open-mouth gaping and lateral head movements." This description closely matches the behavior visible in the viral clip.

The functional significance of gekkering and related close-range vocalizations in red fox behavior has been studied in multiple contexts. Iossa, Soulsbury, Baker, Edwards, and Harris (2008), publishing in *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology* (Springer), documented that red fox social groups in urban environments exhibit complex dominance hierarchies maintained primarily through vocal and postural signaling rather than physical combat. Their study found that vocalizations were the primary mechanism by which dominance relationships were negotiated, renegotiated, and maintained—exactly the "conflict resolution" function identified by the "Why Animals Do The Thing" Tumblr blog.

Critically, red fox vocalizations associated with actual mating behavior are quite different from the aggressive vocalizations in the video. The red fox mating season occurs in late December through February in the Northern Hemisphere. During this period, female foxes (vixens) produce a distinctive "vixen's scream"—a single, loud, high-pitched shriek repeated at intervals—that functions as a contact call to attract males (Macdonald, 1987, *Running with the Fox*, Unwin Hyman). This call is produced by solitary females, not by pairs, and its function is to establish contact with potential mates over long distances, not to "announce" an existing bond. Male foxes respond to vixen screams with a series of barks. The two-fox, face-to-face, mutual vocalization pattern in the viral video is inconsistent with this mating-season contact-calling behavior.

Agonistic vocalizations in canids more broadly—the taxonomic family to which foxes belong—serve what ethologists term a "ritualized aggression" function. Lorenz (1966), in *On Aggression* (Harcourt, Brace & World), provided the foundational theoretical framework: in social species, the fitness cost of physical combat is sufficiently high that natural selection favors the evolution of ritualized conflict-resolution mechanisms—vocalizations, threat postures, and submission displays—that allow disputes to be resolved without physical injury. Fox-to-fox vocalizations of the type shown in the video represent precisely this category of behavior: an escalated but still ritualized interaction in which both animals are communicating intensity of motivation and willingness to escalate further, with the goal of resolving the dispute short of actual combat.

Bradbury and Vehrencamp (2011), in the comprehensive reference *Principles of Animal Communication* (Sinauer Associates), formalized this understanding within signal-theory frameworks: agonistic vocalizations function as "assessment signals" that allow both parties to evaluate the other's fighting ability and motivation without incurring the costs of physical conflict. The growling and screaming in the video are consistent with high-intensity assessment signals in which both foxes are communicating that they are motivated to defend their position but are still engaged in the ritualized negotiation phase rather than physical conflict.

The ethological evidence therefore converges on a clear interpretation: the vocalizations in the viral video represent agonistic or conflict-resolution behavior, not pair-bonding, mate recognition, or any form of "soulmate" announcement. No published ethological study of red fox vocal behavior has identified a vocalization type that functions to announce pair-bond formation to conspecifics, nor would such a vocalization be expected to evolve in a species whose pair-bonding dynamics do not involve public announcement as a reproductive strategy.

## §5 The Anatomy of a Made-Up Animal Fact

The red fox soulmate claim provides a unusually clean case study in the anatomy of fabricated folk-biological claims because the fabrication was acknowledged by its creator and the correction was documented in real time. This allows reconstruction of the full lifecycle: creation, propagation, correction, retraction, and—crucially—persistent circulation despite retraction.

**Creation.** The original poster admitted to generating the claim by interpreting the emotional quality of the sounds and then presenting that interpretation as established fact. This production mechanism—affect-driven confabulation presented as knowledge—is consistent with what Fernbach, Rogers, Fox, and Sloman (2013), in *Psychological Science* (Association for Psychological Science), termed the "illusion of explanatory depth": people systematically overestimate their understanding of how things work, and when asked to produce explanations, they generate plausible-sounding but fabricated accounts that they themselves may partially believe. The soulmate claim may not have been experienced by its creator as a deliberate lie but rather as a confident inference from insufficient evidence—a subjective experience of "knowing" that was in fact confabulation.

**Propagation.** The claim propagated because it satisfied multiple virality criteria simultaneously. It was emotionally resonant (romantic, surprising), visually anchored (the video provided apparent evidence), epistemically inaccessible (few Tumblr users could evaluate fox ethology claims), and aesthetically rewarding (foxes are considered charismatic animals). Berger and Milkman (2012), in *Journal of Marketing Research* (American Marketing Association), demonstrated that online content that evokes high-arousal positive emotions—awe, amusement, surprise—is significantly more likely to be shared than content evoking low-arousal or negative emotions. The soulmate claim evoked exactly this combination: the surprise of a novel "fun fact" combined with the warmth of a romantic narrative.

**Correction.** The correction by "Why Animals Do The Thing" was timely and accurate but faced the structural disadvantages documented above: it was less emotionally engaging, it required the audience to abandon a satisfying narrative, and it was appended to a reblog chain in which the original claim had already accumulated credibility through repetition. The correction also faced what Nyhan and Reifler (2010), in *Political Behavior* (Springer), identified as the "backfire effect" in analogous political-misinformation contexts: corrections can paradoxically strengthen belief in the original claim among audiences strongly motivated to maintain it.

**Retraction.** The original poster's retraction was unusually candid. Unlike most misinformation scenarios, in which the original claimant either doubles down or simply disappears, "Icelandic Stripper Boots" explicitly acknowledged the error and attributed it to incorrect inference rather than deliberate fabrication. This retraction, however, had minimal impact on the claim's continued circulation because the Tumblr reblog architecture meant that most copies of the claim in circulation were downstream reblogs that did not include the retraction. Cook, Lewandowsky, and Ecker (2017), writing in *PLOS ONE* (Public Library of Science), documented that retractions are significantly less effective when the original claim has been reproduced across multiple independent copies that are not updated when the source is corrected—precisely the situation created by Tumblr's reblog model.

**Persistent circulation.** The claim continued to appear across social media platforms long after the Snopes fact-check and the original poster's retraction. This persistence illustrates a fundamental limitation of fact-checking as a strategy against folk-biological misinformation: the claim is self-contained, memorable, and emotionally satisfying, while the correction requires engagement with unfamiliar ethological concepts (ritualized aggression, agonistic vocalizations, gekkering) that lack the narrative simplicity of "they scream when they find their soulmate." The result is an informational asymmetry in which the false claim has higher memetic fitness than the true correction—a dynamic that Wardle and Derakhshan (2017), in their Council of Europe report *Information Disorder*, identified as one of the fundamental challenges of the contemporary misinformation landscape.

## §6 Verdict, Knowledge Gap, and Epistemic Assessment

**Verdict: FALSE**

The claim that red foxes scream when they find their "soulmate" or "perfect mate" is entirely fabricated. The vocalizations shown in the viral three-second Vine video are consistent with agonistic behavior—specifically, ritualized conflict-resolution vocalizations (gekkering or close-range threat vocalizations) documented extensively in the ethological literature on red fox communication (Newton-Fisher et al., 1993; Harris & White, 1992; Iossa et al., 2008). No published ethological study has identified a red fox vocalization that functions to announce pair-bond formation. The claim was spontaneously fabricated by a Tumblr user who later admitted to inventing the "fun fact" based on the emotional quality of the sounds rather than any zoological knowledge. Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2016) correctly rated the claim False.

**Epistemic Class: Fabricated Folk-Biology**

This claim belongs to the epistemic class of fabricated folk-biology—claims that present invented behavioral or biological "facts" about animals in formats that mimic legitimate natural-history knowledge. Unlike deliberate disinformation, fabricated folk-biology is typically produced through affect-driven confabulation rather than strategic deception, and its creators may partially believe their own claims. The production mechanism—emotional interpretation of ambiguous animal behavior, anthropomorphic mapping onto human romantic categories, and presentation as established fact—represents a specific and recurring failure mode in lay biological reasoning that is amplified by social-media architectures rewarding emotional resonance over accuracy.

**EARC Classification: E (Epistemic — fabricated behavioral claim)**

The analytical gap is epistemic: resolving the claim requires ethological knowledge about red fox vocal repertoires and the functional significance of specific call types—knowledge that is available in the scientific literature but inaccessible to most social-media users. The gap is compounded by the brevity and decontextualization of the source video, which provides insufficient behavioral context for even an informed viewer to make a confident interpretation without additional information.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**

- The specific identities and relationship of the two foxes in the original Vine video (captive, semi-wild, or wild; related or unrelated individuals)
- The full behavioral context of the interaction (what preceded and followed the three-second clip)
- The geographic location and circumstances of the original filming
- Quantitative data on the total reach and persistence of the claim across platforms after the retraction and fact-check
- Whether the claim originated independently on other platforms or exclusively through the Tumblr-to-other-platforms pathway
