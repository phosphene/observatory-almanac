## The Claim

The claim is simple and carries a striking headline: Coca-Cola was once considered anti-Semitic for refusing to do business with Israel. The charge went beyond informal suspicion. In April 1966, at a press conference in Tel Aviv, businessman Moshe Bornstein publicly accused Coca-Cola of a deliberate, market-driven exclusion of Israel. Within a week, the Anti-Defamation League of the B'nai B'rith had issued a statement backing Bornstein's charges. American newspapers ran the story. Coca-Cola found itself in a full public relations crisis.

This is not a vague allegation of cultural insensitivity. The specific claim is that Coca-Cola — a globally dominant soft drink company with franchise operations in dozens of small and medium-sized markets worldwide — conspicuously refused to operate in Israel for a period spanning roughly from the company's global expansion in the postwar era through 1966, and that the reason for this refusal was the company's preference for the much larger Arab market, a preference structured by the Arab League's threat to boycott any company that did business with the Jewish state.

Snopes covered this history in a 1999 fact-check by Barbara Mikkelson, later updated in 2011. The article is less a "true or false" verdict than an origins investigation — a genre of Snopes content common in the site's early years, when the platform was still developing its rating vocabulary. The underlying historical record, however, is clear enough to support a firm conclusion: the charges against Coca-Cola were substantially accurate, and Coca-Cola's subsequent behaviour under public pressure confirmed rather than refuted them.

## What's Actually True

The history of Coca-Cola and Israel during the postwar decades is a case study in how corporate actors navigated the structural pressures of Cold War-era Middle Eastern geopolitics. The Arab League's boycott of Israel — formally initiated in 1945 and significantly expanded in subsequent decades — created a direct commercial dilemma for any multinational seeking access to both the Israeli and Arab markets. The boycott operated through several mechanisms: a primary boycott of Israeli goods, a secondary boycott of companies doing business with Israel, and a tertiary boycott of companies doing business with companies that did business with Israel. A multinational operating in Israel could find itself shut out of not one but many Arab markets simultaneously.

Coca-Cola's involvement with this structure was extensive and, for a time, self-serving. In 1949, the company had genuinely attempted to open a bottling plant in Israel, but the effort was blocked by the Israeli government — the reasons for this are not fully documented in the public record, though trade and regulatory considerations were plausibly involved. After this initial attempt, Coca-Cola settled into a quiet accommodation with the Arab market and did not pursue Israeli operations for seventeen years.

The fragility of this façade first showed in 1961. A Cairo civil servant named Mohammad Abu Shadi came into possession of a Coca-Cola bottle manufactured in Ethiopia. He mistook the Amharic lettering on the bottle's label for Hebrew script and publicly accused Coca-Cola of secretly doing business with Israel. The manager of Coca-Cola's Egyptian bottling operations responded to the press by assuring them, categorically, that Coca-Cola would never allow the Israelis a franchise. This statement — made in haste, apparently without guidance from Atlanta — forced the company to retroactively invent a rationale. Company officials subsequently claimed that Israel was simply too small a market to support a franchise operation, and that the decision was economic rather than political.

The economic explanation held for a few years, but its implausibility was fully exposed by 1966. By that point, Coca-Cola was operating a franchise in Cyprus — a Mediterranean island nation with roughly one-tenth the population of Israel. If Cyprus could sustain a franchise, the population-size explanation for Israel's exclusion became incoherent. Bornstein's charges and the ADL's endorsement of them were therefore not inflammatory accusations unsupported by evidence; they were the direct inference of a pattern that the company's own prior statements had made it impossible to explain away.

The consequences for Coca-Cola were immediate. The administrators of Mount Sinai Hospital in Manhattan announced they would stop serving Coca-Cola. Nathan's Famous, the legendary Coney Island hot dog emporium, followed suit. The threat of a sustained Jewish consumer boycott in the American market — Coca-Cola's single largest and most profitable market — made Israel suddenly commercially viable. The company announced it would open a bottling plant in Tel Aviv.

The Arab League responded by placing Coca-Cola on its official boycott list in August 1968. The boycott lasted until May 1991 for most Arab League member states, with Egypt breaking ranks in 1979 under its own trade logic following the Camp David Accords.

Pepsi-Cola faced a parallel trajectory. Prior to Coca-Cola's entry into Israel in 1966, Pepsi had taken a different path: rather than face accusations of anti-Israel bias while also serving the Arab market, Pepsi simply stayed out of Israel entirely, serving the Coke-less Arab markets as a de facto replacement for the Middle East. The Anti-Defamation League investigated Pepsi for alleged participation in the Arab boycott, which was illegal under American law, but the relevant statutes were ambiguous and no violation was ever formally proven. American consumers drew their own conclusions. A significant segment of the post-1966 US consumer market grew up treating Pepsi as the Arab-market substitute for Coke, and a corresponding soft boycott of Pepsi among Jewish American consumers persisted for years.

Pepsi's entry into Israel in 1992 was troubled. An evolution-themed ad campaign depicting man evolving from monkey to Pepsi drinker offended haredi Orthodox communities, who viewed the evolutionary framing as religiously objectionable. The campaign was withdrawn. A 1993 Michael Jackson tour co-sponsored by Pepsi generated additional controversy when Jackson arrived at a location on the Sabbath, which the observant community viewed as deliberate desecration. Pepsi temporarily lost its kashrut certificate. None of this was inevitable; it was the product of a company entering a market it had avoided for decades and encountering a community that had reason to be suspicious of it.

Mark Pendergrast documented much of this history in his 1993 corporate history *For God, Country, and Coca-Cola* (Charles Scribner's Sons, 1993, ISBN 0-684-19347-7). Frederick Allen's *Secret Formula* (HarperCollins, 1994, ISBN 0-88730-672-1, pp. 339-341) provides additional detail on the internal corporate dynamics. Contemporary news coverage in the Jerusalem Post, Washington Post, Chicago Tribune, and Houston Chronicle confirms the sequence of events surrounding both companies' market entries and exits.

## Why People Believe This

The persistence of this story — and the fact that it circulates in forms ranging from outright condemnation to grudging acknowledgment — reflects several distinct epistemic dynamics worth examining separately.

**The structural plausibility of corporate complicity.** The core of the claim is not that Coca-Cola was ideologically committed to anti-Semitism. The claim is that Coca-Cola calculated that the Arab market was larger than the Israeli market and arranged its franchise practices accordingly. This is a profoundly plausible claim about corporate behaviour. It asks nothing more of Coca-Cola than the kind of market-responsive pragmatism that corporations routinely exhibit. Corporations have no inherent political commitments; they have revenue commitments. The claim that Coca-Cola chose Arab market access over Israeli market access because the math favoured it requires no special malice — only the ordinary operations of profit-maximising corporate decision-making. This structural plausibility is precisely what made the 1966 charges so difficult for Coca-Cola to deflect.

**The epistemic novelty of documented hypocrisy.** What distinguishes this story from ordinary corporate misconduct narratives is the paper trail. Coca-Cola did not merely decline to enter Israel; it repeatedly offered disingenuous explanations that were demonstrably refuted by its own market behaviour elsewhere. The Cyprus example — one-tenth of Israel's population, fully served — was not a smoking gun that required inference or interpretation. It was a direct falsification of the stated rationale. When the "too small a market" explanation was available for refutation by a single counter-example, the claim became one of the cleaner cases of documented corporate misrepresentation in the post-war era. The story circulates precisely because it is so clearly substantiated.

**The psychology of discovering that "respectable" institutions behave badly.** Coca-Cola occupied a particular symbolic position in mid-20th century American culture. It was not merely a beverage; it was marketed as a symbol of American values — freedom, refreshment, democratic accessibility. The revelation that this institution had, for commercial reasons, excluded an ally state that was itself associated with the post-Holocaust assertion of Jewish sovereignty carried a specific charge. The shock was proportional to the symbolic gap between Coca-Cola's self-presentation and its actual market conduct. The pleasure that many consumers took in discovering this gap — the particular satisfaction of "the idealist corporation that wasn't" — is a recognisable psychological reward that helps explain why such stories have long shelf lives.

**The substitution of commercial outcome for ideological motivation.** A genuine analytical risk in this story is the conflation of commercial calculation with ideological anti-Semitism. Coca-Cola did not refuse the Israeli market because its executives wished Israel harm or harboured personal anti-Jewish prejudice. They refused it because the Arab League's coercive boycott machinery made the calculation of gains and losses come out against Israeli market entry. This is a morally and politically complex distinction: is a company that complies with coercive antisemitic boycott structures "anti-Semitic"? The ADL's decision to call Coca-Cola out using the language of anti-Semitism reflected a judgment that complicity in boycott structures — regardless of ideological motivation — constitutes a form of discriminatory conduct that deserves the same label as ideological animus. This is a defensible position, but it requires holding the distinction between complicity and conviction clearly in mind to evaluate accurately.

**The lingering arithmetic of old wounds.** The Snopes article concludes with the observation that there are still those who follow the informal rule "Coke is for Jews; Pepsi is for Arabs," and that old wounds are not necessarily healed wounds. This is ethnographically accurate. Long-standing consumer loyalty and aversion patterns, once formed around political and ethnic affiliations, decay slowly. The geography of American cola preference maps, in residual ways, onto communal histories that most individual consumers in those communities have only partial knowledge of.

## Verdict

**The claim is true.** Coca-Cola did systematically exclude Israel from its franchise network for decades following the company's postwar global expansion, and the evidence strongly supports the conclusion that this exclusion was motivated by the commercial logic of the Arab League boycott rather than by any genuine finding that Israel was too small a market to support franchise operations. The Cyprus counter-example makes the "too small" rationale impossible to defend, and Coca-Cola's rapid about-face once faced with American consumer pressure confirms that the commercial logic was always driving the decision.

The characterisation of this behaviour as "anti-Semitic" is a judgment call that depends on how broadly one construes that term. If anti-Semitism requires ideological animus against Jewish people, Coca-Cola's conduct was not straightforwardly anti-Semitic — it was market accommodation to an external coercive structure. If anti-Semitism includes complicity with discriminatory structures that target Jewish people and Jewish states, the ADL's characterisation is warranted.

What is not in dispute is the historical record: Coca-Cola, for commercial reasons, excluded Israel from its franchise map while simultaneously serving the Arab markets that the Arab League was protecting through its boycott of Israeli commerce. This decision generated charges of anti-Semitism that were substantially but not entirely accurate. Coca-Cola capitulated under consumer pressure. The Arab League boycotted Coca-Cola. That entire sequence is documented fact, not urban legend.

## The Wider Picture

The Coca-Cola-Israel episode sits within a considerably larger history of the Arab League boycott and its effects on multinational corporate behaviour in the midcentury period. The boycott was one of the twentieth century's more ambitious and, in its early decades, more effective instruments of economic coercion — a systematic attempt to weaponise market access against companies choosing to do business with a state the Arab League refused to recognise.

The boycott operated through a centralised registry, the Central Boycott Office based in Damascus, which maintained lists of blacklisted companies and periodically updated them. Membership on the blacklist had direct commercial consequences: Arab League states (representing a combined market of substantial size in the oil-revenue years from the 1970s onward) would refuse to do business with or through blacklisted entities. For multinational companies with significant regional ambitions, the calculus of Israeli market entry was always triangulated against the more substantial Arab market access at risk.

Pendergrast's research in *For God, Country, and Coca-Cola* (1993) documents how Coca-Cola's internal discussions framed the Israeli market question in explicitly commercial terms — not in terms of political sympathies but in terms of revenue projections on both sides of the ledger. This framing is itself revealing: it shows that the question of whether to do business with Israel was, for the company, categorically the same kind of question as whether to do business with any other market. The Israeli state existed, for Coca-Cola's internal deliberations, as a line item.

The broader context also includes the activism of the Anti-Defamation League, which played a significant role in both surfacing the pattern of boycott compliance and mobilising consumer pressure against it. The ADL's investigation of Pepsi was part of a systematic effort to identify and expose American companies participating in the boycott, which American law — through the Export Administration Act and subsequent antiboycott regulations — formally prohibited. The antiboycott laws were imperfect and enforcement was limited, but their existence signalled a federal recognition that the Arab League boycott implicated American anti-discrimination norms.

The story also illuminates the competitive dynamics between Coca-Cola and Pepsi during this period. The two companies were competing globally, and each found itself in a structurally similar position with respect to the Arab-Israeli market split. Coca-Cola entered Israel first, under duress, in 1966. Pepsi held out until 1992 — for twenty-six years, it occupied the Coke-less Arab markets as a de facto regional champion. When Coca-Cola was reinstated after the end of the Arab League boycott in 1991, Pepsi entered Israel the following year, its reentry complicated by twenty-six years of market absence and a reputation it had never fully rehabilitated in the American Jewish community.

The lasting commercial lesson that the episode taught — that consumer markets organised around ethnic, religious, and political identities can generate significant pressure on corporate behaviour — anticipates the contemporary landscape of boycott, divestment, and sanctions movements, brand-oriented consumer activism, and politically-motivated consumer choice. The mechanisms are the same; only the specific targets and underlying political positions vary.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Barbara Mikkelson's 1999 Snopes article on this topic is an origins piece rather than a standard True/False verdict — a format common in Snopes's early content that reflected the site's origins as an investigation into folklore and rumour rather than a systematic fact-checking operation. The article does not render a single verdict but instead reconstructs the historical sequence with considerable care, drawing on Pendergrast's and Allen's corporate histories alongside contemporary newspaper coverage.

The article's methodology is essentially historical journalism: check the claims against documented sources, reconstruct the sequence of events, and present the result. For a historical claim of this kind — where the primary evidence consists of corporate behaviour, market records, and contemporary news reports rather than a single verifiable assertion — this approach is appropriate. Binary rating systems struggle with claims that are substantially true but require contextual qualification, and this is one of them.

What the Snopes treatment does particularly well is present the parallel Pepsi narrative alongside the Coca-Cola account. This comparative structure is analytically valuable: it shows that Coca-Cola's boycott-driven market exclusion was not an isolated aberration but one instance of a structural pattern affecting multiple American corporations. The "same skirt, just a bit older" formulation at the article's end captures this symmetry well — Coca-Cola appeared heroic by entering Israel in 1966 only because it had done the same thing as Pepsi, just slightly earlier, and then been publicly shamed into reversing course.

The article's age (1999, updated 2011) means it predates the sharp expansion of digital media misinformation and does not engage with the specific social media vectors through which this story now circulates. Contemporary iterations of the claim sometimes strip the historical context, presenting Coca-Cola as simply "anti-Semitic" without the structural context of the Arab League boycott that explains the commercial logic behind the market exclusion. A fuller contemporary treatment would emphasise that the story is not about corporate ideology but about the coercive power of large-scale commercial boycott structures — a point with current relevance in multiple geopolitical contexts.

The fact-check also leaves some open questions that subsequent scholarship might address: the exact role of the Israeli government in blocking Coca-Cola's 1949 market entry attempt, the internal deliberations within Coca-Cola's executive leadership during the 1950s and 1960s, and the precise commercial calculations that drove the 1966 reversal. These are not deficiencies in the fact-check so much as they are invitations to the deeper historical research that corporate history archives might eventually support.
