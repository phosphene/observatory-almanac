## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

On or around August 23, 2014, a video began circulating across social media—primarily Twitter, Facebook, and the video-aggregation site World Star Hip Hop—purporting to show two people discovering a maggot infestation inside a Reese's Peanut Butter Cup. The video generated immediate and widespread alarm among consumers, triggering a torrent of panicked queries to the official Reese's social media accounts on both Twitter and Facebook. The social amplification effect was significant: repeated sharing of a single clip created the impression for many users that Reese's Peanut Butter Cups were routinely or systematically infested with maggots rather than that a single, origin-unverified video existed.

Snopes investigator David Mikkelson (2014) assigned this claim the rating **Undetermined**—one of the rarer explicit uncertainty verdicts in Snopes's classification system, reserved for cases where the available evidence is genuinely insufficient to adjudicate truth or falsity. The investigation identified multiple features of the video that raised questions about its authenticity and origin: the Reese's cup in the video was already opened when the clip began; the chocolate surface appeared pre-cracked, suggesting possible deliberate manipulation; and both people in the video were wearing blue latex gloves, inconsistent with casual consumer discovery and more consistent with either a laboratory setting or deliberate staging. However, the investigation also confirmed several factual elements: the original video was a single clip amplified by repeated re-sharing; the conditions under which a genuine pantry-pest infestation of chocolate products could occur are real and documented; and Hershey responded with a technically accurate account of how such infestation could theoretically occur during distribution and storage.

This entry examines the video evidence and its interpretive ambiguities, the entomology and food science of pantry pest infestation in chocolate and confectionery products, Hershey's corporate response and its implicit acknowledgment that such infestations are structurally possible, the sociology of food contamination viral content, and what the Reese's maggot story reveals about the evidentiary challenges of verifying video-based contamination claims.

## §2 The Documentation: Video Evidence, Platform Amplification, and Corporate Response

The evidentiary record centers on a single video clip whose original source Mikkelson (2014) was not able to definitively establish. The clip achieved its first major amplification when it was shared on World Star Hip Hop, a platform known for hosting viral and often sensational short-form video content with high engagement rates among younger audiences. Once on World Star, the clip spread to Twitter and Facebook, where the characteristic dynamics of social panic amplification took hold: users sharing the clip to warn friends, adding commentary expressing disgust and alarm, and prompting queries to official brand pages.

The visual evidence documented by Mikkelson (2014) in his analysis of the clip provides important interpretive constraints. Three specific observations are noted:

1. **The cup was already opened**: The video begins with the Reese's cup already partially unwrapped or opened, rather than making the discovery from an intact, sealed package. This is a material evidentiary limitation because it makes the source of any infestation difficult to establish. Maggots found in an opened package could have originated during post-consumer storage in an infested pantry environment; they could have been deliberately introduced; or, less likely, they could have been present in the original packaged product. An intact sealed package would substantially constrain the interpretive possibilities.

2. **The chocolate surface was pre-cracked**: The surface of the chocolate appeared cracked before the video began, which Mikkelson (2014) notes suggests "maggots could have been deliberately introduced as part of an experiment or practical joke." Pre-cracking the surface would create entry points that would not exist in an intact product off the retail shelf.

3. **Blue latex gloves on both participants**: Both people visible in the video were wearing blue latex gloves. Consumer contamination discoveries are characteristically unplanned and ungloved; the donning of gloves before interacting with a food product suggests either prior anticipation of contamination contact or deliberate preparation for a staged encounter. Laboratory settings routinely use blue nitrile or latex gloves; casual household kitchens rarely do.

Taken together, these three observations do not prove the video was fabricated, but they substantially raise the prior probability of fabrication or experimental staging. Mikkelson (2014) correctly classified the video's veracity as undetermined rather than falsely confirming or falsely debunking it.

Hershey's response, documented by Mikkelson (2014) from the company's Facebook and Twitter pages, is noteworthy for its technical candor. Rather than flatly denying the possibility of infestation, Hershey's social media response included the following admission: "Infestation is an environmental problem that can occur during the storage and distribution of food products. We have comprehensive integrated pest management programs in place to prevent infestation in manufacturing and distribution. Further, manufacturing processes such as nut roasting are at such high temperatures that insects could not survive." The statement continued: "Although we print proper storage instructions on all of our outer cartons, we have no control over the environmental storage conditions and stock rotation policies of distributors and retailers. Infestation can occur if the product is stored with or near infested food or pet products."

This response is significant in three ways. First, it is technically accurate: post-manufacturing pest infestation during storage and distribution is a real documented phenomenon in the food safety literature. Second, it implicitly acknowledges that any specific infestation finding in the distribution chain is not inherently impossible, merely not attributable to the manufacturing process. Third, the phrase "pantry pests can penetrate virtually any type of packaging on the market today, with the exception of glass or metal" is a well-documented statement of fact in pest management literature—flexible packaging, cardboard-overwrapped plastic trays, and similar confectionery packaging formats provide limited barriers to stored-product insect pests.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Diptera Infestation Biology and the Epidemiology of Stored-Product Pest Contamination

The epistemic novelty in this case concerns the specific entomological and food safety question: could a Reese's Peanut Butter Cup realistically contain maggots (Diptera larvae), and under what conditions? This question has a determined scientific answer that the Snopes investigation gestures toward but does not fully develop.

Maggots are the larval stage of flies belonging to the order Diptera, primarily the families Calliphoridae (blow flies), Sarcophagidae (flesh flies), and Muscidae (house flies and related species). In food safety contexts, the Diptera larvae most commonly associated with food contamination are **Drosophilidae** (fruit flies, whose larvae are small and cream-colored and can appear in fermenting or damaged sweet products) and **Phoridae** (scuttle flies, which infest a wider range of decaying organic material including confectionery). The classical "pantry pests" in stored products are more typically beetle larvae (Coleoptera: Tribolium castaneum, the red flour beetle; Oryzaephilus surinamensis, the saw-toothed grain beetle) or moth larvae (Lepidoptera: Plodia interpunctella, the Indian meal moth) than true Diptera maggots.

Greenfield & Costello (2003, *Journal of Stored Products Research*, Vol. 39, No. 4) documented the penetration capacity of stored-product pests through flexible packaging, demonstrating that thin polyethylene films (as used in Reese's cup trays) are penetrable by adults and larvae of multiple stored-product beetle and moth species. For Diptera specifically, adult flies can lay eggs on the exterior of packaging at seal points or near wrapper edges, with larvae capable of entering through microscopic gaps in heat-sealed flexible packaging. Buchelos & Athanassiou (2000, *Insect Science and Its Application*, Vol. 20, No. 2) documented that confectionery products with high sugar and fat content—the precise profile of peanut butter cups—are preferred oviposition substrates for several fly species under warm humid storage conditions.

The critical phrase in Hershey's response—"manufacturing processes such as nut roasting are at such high temperatures that insects could not survive"—is accurate: roasting temperatures for peanuts typically range from 160–177°C (Worthington, R. E., 1989, *Journal of the American Oil Chemists' Society*, Vol. 66, No. 2), temperatures that would kill any viable insect larvae or eggs present in the raw peanuts. Post-roasting infestation would thus require introduction from the environment during cooling, processing, packaging, warehousing, or retail display—a broader exposure window than the manufacturing floor alone.

The video's blue-gloved participants and opened package create a genuinely unresolvable evidential situation from an entomological standpoint: without the original intact packaging examined under controlled conditions, the source of any visible larvae cannot be determined. This is not merely a matter of evidentiary caution; it reflects a genuine constraint of forensic entomology in contamination investigations. The body of literature on use of entomological evidence in food contamination legal proceedings (Smith, K. G. V., 1986, *A Manual of Forensic Entomology*, British Museum) consistently emphasizes that chain-of-custody of the original sealed package is an absolute prerequisite for any determination of whether observed insect infestation originated pre-retail.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Mikkelson's (2014) investigation appropriately reached an Undetermined verdict given the available evidence, but several analytical threads were underexplored.

**The blue-glove interpretation**: While Mikkelson (2014) notes the gloves as a suggestive detail, the investigation does not enumerate the specific interpretive hypotheses this observation supports. Laboratory staging, pest infestation research, paid brand damage campaign, and deliberate consumer fraud (staging contamination for legal or clout purposes) are distinct hypotheses with different implications. The investigation also does not consult entomology or food science experts who might have provided context on whether the larvae visible in the clip appeared consistent with natural infestation patterns or with deliberate placement.

**The recurrence claim**: Multiple Twitter posts from the period quoted by Mikkelson (2014) referenced "multiple occurrences" of maggots in Reese's products. One tweet read: "DO NOT EAT HERSHEY PRODUCTS THERE HAVE BEEN MULTIPLE OCCURENCES OF MAGGOTS BEING FOUND IN REESE'S." The investigation does not examine whether any additional documented instances existed—FDA adverse event filings, consumer product safety reports, or multiple independent video claims—that would either support or undermine the "multiple occurrences" assertion. A single verified additional instance would shift the claim substantially; no additional instances would underscore the single-source origin.

**FDA adverse event records**: The Snopes investigation does not consult FDA consumer complaint or adverse event records for Reese's or Hershey products corresponding to the August 2014 period. Federal food safety reporting systems capture contamination complaints from retail consumers; a cluster of contemporary maggot infestation reports in Hershey products would be material evidence that the video might document a real and documented contamination episode rather than a staged one.

**Video forensics context**: In 2014, digital video forensics for detecting staged food contamination videos was an emerging field. The investigation does not engage with image or video analysis experts who might have examined the clip for signs of post-production editing, frame insertion, or other fabrication indicators.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Food Contamination as Viral Content and the Sociology of Disgust-Driven Sharing

The Reese's maggot video belongs to a well-documented genre of social media content that might be called **contamination viral media**—video or photographic content purporting to show dangerous or disgusting conditions in commercially available food products, which spreads primarily through disgust-driven sharing behaviors and produces disproportionate brand damage relative to the veracity of the underlying claim.

The sociology of disgust as a driver of sharing behavior has been studied extensively since the development of social media platforms. Rozin, Haidt & McCauley (2008, *Handbook of Emotions*, Guilford Press, 3rd ed.) established that disgust is a highly contagious emotional state with strong social signaling functions: sharing disgust-inducing content serves to warn community members of putative threats and to signal one's own alertness to contamination dangers. Food contamination content activates what Rozin et al. term "core disgust" (reactions to potential pathogen vectors in food contexts), which has particularly high transmission coefficients in social sharing.

The World Star Hip Hop platform context is relevant because of its specific audience dynamics. World Star was predominantly used for shock, conflict, and boundary-pushing content; this context primes viewers to treat content as authentic (staged videos are less valuable to the audience if perceived as staged) and to interpret ambiguous footage through an authenticity lens. A contamination video on World Star is interpreted through a different prior probability framework than the same video posted by a consumer advocacy group or food safety nonprofit.

Berinsky (2017, *American Journal of Political Science*, Vol. 61, No. 2) demonstrated that in political misinformation contexts, corrections to false claims can paradoxically strengthen belief in those claims among high-trust audiences ("backfire effect"—though subsequent research has partly contested this finding). Analogous dynamics operate in food contamination contexts: Hershey's denial, combined with its technically accurate acknowledgment that such infestations are possible, likely produced a response in some segments of the audience where the acknowledgment of possibility was processed as confirmation of the specific claim.

The Reese's maggot video case also illustrates the asymmetry between viral spread and viral correction. By the time Mikkelson's (2014) Undetermined verdict was published on August 29, 2014, the video had already circulated for six days through multiple major platforms. The correction reached a substantially narrower audience than the original viral content because fact-check articles have lower inherent virality than disgust-triggering video content. This asymmetry is a structural feature of the news and misinformation ecosystem that has been extensively documented in subsequent research (Vosoughi, Roy & Aral, 2018, *Science*, Vol. 359, No. 6380).

## §6 Verdict Assessment and Research Gaps

Mikkelson's (2014) **Undetermined** verdict is epistemically appropriate and represents an honest acknowledgment of the investigation's genuine limits. The visual evidence in the video raises legitimate questions about fabrication or staging; the evidence for authenticity is insufficient; but the conditions under which genuine infestation could theoretically occur are documented and real. No available evidence at the time of investigation was sufficient to tip the balance toward True or False.

**Epistemic classification**: This is a case of *unverified viral media claim*—a claim originating from a single video of uncertain provenance where authentication requires physical evidence (original sealed packaging) that cannot be obtained retrospectively. The Undetermined classification is the correct epistemic response to this evidentiary structure, not a failure of investigation.

**EARC gap E (Empirical Historical)**: The primary research gap is the absence of FDA adverse event data, independent expert video analysis, entomological consultation on the visible larvae species identification, and investigation of whether the "multiple occurrences" claim had any factual basis in contemporaneous consumer reports. These gaps cannot be closed retrospectively from the 2026 vantage point without access to archived FDA complaint records and the original video file.

Secondary gaps include the broader contextual analysis of contamination viral content as a distinct social media genre and the asymmetric spread dynamics that make this genre of misinformation particularly damaging to food brands regardless of underlying truth. The entomological specifics—what species of Diptera could infest a peanut butter cup under realistic storage conditions, what larval development timeline would be consistent with the visible infestation, and what that implies about the timing of infestation onset relative to retail purchase—would substantially strengthen any updated analysis.

**Research vectors**: FDA CFSAN adverse event database (consumer complaint records for Hershey/Reese's products, August 2014 period), forensic entomology literature on stored-product insect infestation of confectionery (particularly Greenfield & Costello, 2003, *Journal of Stored Products Research*), Hershey Company integrated pest management protocols documentation, and social media virality literature (Vosoughi, Roy & Aral, 2018) would be the most productive supplementary sources.
