# What Are 'Refried Beans'?

## §1 · The Claim

The claim, held widely and intuitively appealing to English speakers, is that "refried beans" are beans that have been fried more than once — that some prior frying left them insufficiently cooked, and a second pass through the pan was required to finish the job. The prefix "re-" in English unambiguously signals repetition: to reschedule, reassign, redistribute, reheat. Beans that have been "refried" ought, by this logic, to be beans subjected to the frying process at least twice.

Snopes rates this claim **False**. The English name "refried beans" is a mistranslation — or more precisely, an incorrect calque — of the Spanish culinary term *frijoles refritos*. The prefix *re-* in Spanish does not exclusively mean "again"; it can also function as an intensifier meaning "very" or "thoroughly." *Frijoles refritos* means not "beans fried again" but "beans well-fried" or "beans thoroughly fried." No second frying occurs, has ever occurred, and is not implied by the dish's name in its original Spanish. The mistranslation entered English through what amounts to a false cognate error — English speakers assumed Spanish *re-* functioned as English *re-* — and the error has persisted largely unchallenged because the dish itself is too familiar for most English speakers to question what its name actually describes.

## §2 · Snopes Verdict and the Linguistic Record

David Mikkelson's entry, published on 9 July 2006, rates the claim **False** and supplies two direct culinary authority sources: Diana Kennedy's *The Cuisines of Mexico* (first published 1972, updated edition 1989, William Morrow Cookbooks) and Martha Barnette's *Ladyfingers and Nun's Tummies* (1998, Vintage Books).

Kennedy is the more authoritative culinary voice. A British-born food writer who spent decades living and cooking in Mexico, she is widely regarded as the foremost English-language chronicler of Mexican regional cooking. Her explanation, quoted by Snopes, is worth reproducing in full for its honesty about the discovery process: "Several people have asked me why, when the beans are fried, they are called refried. Nobody I asked in Mexico seemed to know until quite suddenly it dawned on me. The Mexicans have a habit of qualifying a word to emphasize the meaning by adding the prefix *re-*. They will get the oil very hot (*requemar*), or something will be very good (*retebien*). Thus *refrito* beans are well fried, which they certainly are, since they are fried until they are almost dry" (Kennedy, D., 1989, *The Cuisines of Mexico*, William Morrow Cookbooks, p. 272).

Kennedy's examples are well-chosen. *Requemar* in Spanish means to overheat, scorch, or burn — not to heat again. *Retebien* means very well, excellently — not well again. The *re-* prefix in these constructions intensifies rather than repeats. This is a documented polysemy of the Spanish *re-* prefix: its repetitive and intensive senses coexist in the language, and which sense applies is context-determined. In *frijoles refritos*, the intensive reading is correct; the bean preparation is fried once, thoroughly.

Barnette's treatment in *Ladyfingers and Nun's Tummies* addresses the same etymology from the angle of food word history, situating *frijoles refritos* among the many food names whose English translations carry misleading or false implications (Barnette, M., 1998, *Ladyfingers and Nun's Tummies*, Vintage Books, p. 72). The broader point — that culinary translation is fraught with false cognates and deceptive borrowings — is well taken.

The actual preparation of *frijoles refritos* involves no double-frying. The standard method is: (1) cook dried beans in water until tender, either by simmering, stewing, or pressure cooking, sometimes with an overnight soak; (2) mash or blend the cooked beans into a paste; (3) fry the bean paste in lard (or, in modern non-traditional versions, vegetable oil) until the mixture becomes dense, dry, and thoroughly integrated. The frying stage is vigorous and performed at high heat. The beans are indeed "refritos" in Kennedy's sense: extremely well-fried. But they are fried exactly once.

## §3 · Epistemic Novelty: The Spanish Intensive *Re-* and Its Cross-Language Hazards

The Snopes entry identifies the mechanism of the mistranslation (intensive vs. repetitive *re-*) but does not explore the broader linguistic and cognitive phenomenon this case exemplifies — nor does it address why this particular error has proven so durable.

**The Spanish intensifying *re-*** is not a marginal feature of the language. It is a productive morphological process documented across Spanish dialects, particularly in Mexican Spanish and other Latin American varieties. Intensifying *re-* (and its hyperbolic extension *rete-* or *requete-*) is applied broadly: *relindo* (very pretty), *rebien* (very well), *recaro* (very expensive), *rebueno* (very good). The process is colloquial and emphatic rather than formal, which helps explain why formal Spanish dictionaries and grammar textbooks — the sources most likely consulted by early English translators of Mexican dishes — might not have foregrounded the intensive sense. Standard grammar instruction in Spanish tends to gloss *re-* primarily as a repetitive prefix, because that is its most productive sense in formal registers (Dressler, W.U. and Merlini Barbaresi, L., 1994, *Morphopragmatics: Diminutives and Intensifiers in Italian, German, and Other Languages*, Mouton de Gruyter, Chapter 5 — covering intensifier morphology cross-linguistically).

**The persistence of the error** raises an interesting epistemological question: why has "refried beans" not been corrected in English usage despite decades of culinary scholarship pointing out the mistranslation? Several factors conspire to preserve the error:

First, the erroneous English name is institutionally fixed. It appears on restaurant menus, product cans (Old El Paso, Rosarita, and others), grocery store shelf labels, and recipe databases throughout the English-speaking world. Changing it would require a coordinated reclassification effort by the food industry, which has no incentive to pursue one.

Second, the error is *semantically harmless* from the consumer's perspective. Whether one believes the beans were fried twice or fried thoroughly, the product in the can is the same. The mistranslation causes no confusion about what to buy or how to use it. Unlike, say, a drug dosage mistranslation, the refried beans error carries no practical consequence — which removes the principal motivation for correction.

Third, and most subtly, the double-fried interpretation has a kind of culinary plausibility that reinforces itself. Frying cooked mashed beans twice is not, in fact, an absurd preparation; one could imagine it being done for texture reasons. The false interpretation does not strike English speakers as implausible, which reduces the cognitive pressure to question it.

**Epistemic novelty**: the refried beans case is a canonical example of what linguists call a **false calque** — a translation that maps the morphological structure of a source term onto the target language through false parallel assumptions about how morphemes function (Zuckermann, G., 2003, *Language Contact and Lexical Enrichment in Israeli Hebrew*, Palgrave Macmillan — relevant to morphological calque processes). False calques are distinct from false cognates (single words that look similar but mean different things) in that they operate at the level of word-internal grammar. The translator who produced "refried beans" — and their identity is unknown; the English term's origin is not documented — was not merely misreading a word but misapplying a morphosyntactic rule across language boundaries.

This is, moreover, a case where the false calque has proven more semantically suggestive than the accurate translation would have been. "Well-fried beans" is flat and uninformative; "refried beans" implies a more complex and labor-intensive preparation, which arguably has contributed to the dish's marketing appeal in the United States. The error may, paradoxically, have helped popularize the food.

## §4 · The Dish in Its Culinary and Cultural Context

*Frijoles refritos* occupy a central position in Mexican cuisine that precedes their English-language misrepresentation by centuries. Beans — primarily *Phaseolus vulgaris*, common bean, in its various cultural varieties including pinto, black, and kidney — have been cultivated and eaten in Mesoamerica for at least 7,000 years, making them among the oldest continuously cultivated food crops in the Western Hemisphere (Gepts, P., 1988, "Phaseolus vulgaris (Fabaceae)," in Smartt, J. and Simmonds, N.W. (eds.), *Evolution of Crop Plants*, Longman, pp. 265–272). They represent a dietary staple of extraordinary antiquity.

The *frijoles refritos* preparation — beans cooked, mashed, and fried — is a method of bean preservation and palatability enhancement that emerged well before industrialization. Lard, the traditional frying fat, is a product of pig husbandry introduced to Mexico with Spanish colonization; pre-Columbian preparations may have used other fats or no fat at all. The integration of lard into the preparation marks a post-1521 colonial synthesis, though the underlying bean-cooking tradition is far older (Coe, S.D., 1994, *America's First Cuisines*, University of Texas Press — covering pre-Columbian Mesoamerican food culture).

In contemporary Mexican cooking, *frijoles refritos* are used as a side dish, as a dip, as a filling, as a spread for tostadas, and as a binding element in composed dishes. The texture of properly made *frijoles refritos* — smooth, dense, lightly crisped at the edges from contact with the hot lard — is the product of that single vigorous frying stage. Kennedy's observation that they are "fried until they are almost dry" captures the key textural standard: moisture is driven off by the heat; the paste firms and concentrates; the flavor deepens through the Maillard reaction at the bean paste's surface.

## §5 · EARC Classification and Epistemic Gap

**EARC classification: E (Empirically Verified)**. The mistranslation is confirmed by:

1. Native Spanish linguistic morphology (documented intensive *re-* prefix usage)
2. Culinary authority testimony (Kennedy, 1989)
3. Food word etymology scholarship (Barnette, 1998)
4. The actual preparation method of *frijoles refritos*, which involves no second frying

No counter-evidence exists; there is no documented historical preparation in which pinto or black beans were fried, removed from the pan, and then fried again as a standard recipe step. The claim that the dish's name accurately reflects double-frying is false by definition and method.

**Gap**: The translation error's specific origin — *when* and by *whom* "refried beans" entered English food writing as a term — has not been definitively traced. The earliest documented English-language cookbook appearances of "refried beans" have not been formally archived and dated in public lexicographic resources. The *Oxford English Dictionary*'s entry for "refried" (adjective) exists but is sparse; a proper diachronic lexicographic study of the term's introduction into American English would be a modest but valuable contribution to culinary linguistics.

The refried beans etymology is one of those cases where the correct answer is simultaneously more interesting than the intuitive answer and more defensible. The double-frying story makes English-language culinary sense; the well-fried story makes Spanish-language culinary and grammatical sense. The dish's authentic preparation — cooked once, mashed, fried once, vigorously — is already an interesting and demanding technique without requiring imaginary redundant steps. The English name is wrong; the beans are excellent.
