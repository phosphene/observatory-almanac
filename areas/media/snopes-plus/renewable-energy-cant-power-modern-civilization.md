# "Renewable Energy Can't Power Modern Civilization"


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: Renewable energy sources — primarily solar photovoltaic, wind turbines, and hydropower — are fundamentally incapable of powering the complex, continuous, high-demand energy needs of modern industrial civilization. Under this claim, the intermittency of solar and wind (which produce electricity only when the sun shines and the wind blows), combined with inadequate storage technology and grid infrastructure constraints, means that fossil fuels remain thermodynamically necessary for reliable modern electricity supply. The corollary is that decarbonization targets that rely on replacing fossil fuel generation with renewables are technically infeasible, economically ruinous, or both.

**Extended variants**: The claim appears in several intensities. The strong version holds that 100% renewable electricity is physically impossible given current technology — an absolute impossibility claim. A softer version asserts that while some renewable penetration is achievable, going beyond 30–40% share creates grid instability problems that make further expansion impractical without orders-of-magnitude storage breakthroughs. A third variant focuses on cost: even if technically possible, 100% renewables would be economically catastrophic because backup redundancy and storage would make electricity unaffordable. A fourth variant concedes electricity but argues that industrial processes requiring high-temperature heat, aviation fuel, and chemical feedstocks require fossil fuels in ways electricity cannot replace.

**The political and rhetorical context**: The claim circulates most actively in climate policy debates. It has been promoted by fossil fuel industry groups, contrarian think tanks, and politicians opposed to decarbonization timelines. It is also sometimes articulated in good faith by engineers, energy analysts, and physicists with legitimate concerns about grid reliability who believe that the difficulty of the transition is being underestimated by enthusiastic green advocates. Separating the bad-faith lobbying variant from the legitimate technical concern requires engaging with the specific empirical and modeling evidence.

**The definitional question**: The claim often leaves ambiguous what it means for renewables to "power modern civilization." If the standard is delivering electricity to consumers reliably on a 24/7 basis, that is a different question from whether renewables can power all energy uses including heat, transport, and industrial processes. The claim is most confidently refuted in its electricity-provision form; the broader "all energy" claim involves more complex analysis, though the overall direction of the evidence still contradicts the impossibility framing.

**Why this matters now**: The pace of renewable energy deployment has accelerated dramatically in the 2020s due to cost declines from learning curves in solar and wind manufacturing. Solar photovoltaic module costs have fallen by more than 90% since 2010 (IRENA, 2023, *Renewable Power Generation Costs*), making new solar capacity the cheapest source of electricity generation in history by levelized cost in most geographies. Whether the claim is correctly calibrated shapes whether decarbonization is treated as technically tractable or as requiring technological breakthroughs not yet available — a question with enormous policy consequences.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Functioning high-renewable grids already exist**: Multiple national and regional electricity grids currently operate with majority or near-total renewable energy shares. Iceland generates approximately 99%–100% of its electricity from renewables (predominantly geothermal and hydroelectric) and has done so for decades (Statistics Iceland, 2023). Norway generates approximately 90%–95% of its electricity from hydropower and has done so sustainably since the early 20th century (Norwegian Water Resources and Energy Directorate, 2023). Portugal has repeatedly set records for renewable electricity generation, including periods where renewables exceeded 100% of domestic electricity demand (REN, 2023). Uruguay generates approximately 97–98% of its electricity from renewables. Costa Rica has run its electricity grid on more than 98% renewables for multiple consecutive years (ICE, 2022, *Annual Electricity Statistics*). Germany has reached instantaneous renewable share of over 100% of electricity demand on multiple occasions while exporting surplus to neighboring countries (Fraunhofer ISE, 2023, *Energy Charts*). Denmark regularly generates more wind electricity than its domestic demand and exports the surplus to neighboring countries.

**100% renewable electricity modeling**: A substantial body of peer-reviewed energy systems modeling — using different methodologies, for different geographies, published in leading journals — has consistently concluded that 100% renewable electricity supply is technically achievable with current or near-current technology, at costs comparable to or lower than continued fossil fuel operation when system costs and externalities are properly accounted (Jacobson et al., 2015, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*; Heard et al., 2017, *Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews*, which critiques but does not disprove; Blanco et al., 2018, *Joule*; Brown et al., 2018, *Joule*; Bogdanov et al., 2021, *Nature Communications*). These models vary in their assumptions about storage costs, transmission infrastructure, demand flexibility, and backup generation, but converge on feasibility.

**The intermittency problem and its solutions**: Solar and wind generation are indeed intermittent — they produce electricity when solar radiation and wind speed are adequate, which varies by time of day, season, and weather. This is a real engineering challenge. However, the challenge has multiple complementary solutions that are already commercial or near-commercial: (1) Grid-scale battery storage, primarily lithium-ion and increasingly longer-duration chemistries, has fallen sharply in cost (90%+ since 2010 per BloombergNEF, 2023) and is being deployed at scale globally. (2) Pumped-storage hydropower, a mature technology in continuous operation since the 1890s, stores energy by pumping water uphill when electricity is cheap and releasing it through turbines when demand peaks. (3) Geographic diversification of renewable generation — wind blows somewhere even when it's calm at any single location, and solar generation at different latitudes and longitudes is offset in time. Long-distance high-voltage direct-current (HVDC) transmission enables energy sharing across large geographic areas. (4) Demand flexibility: shifting electricity consumption to times of high renewable generation (vehicle charging, industrial processes, water heating) reduces the storage burden. (5) Hydrogen produced by electrolysis when surplus renewable electricity exists can be stored and used later for industrial processes or converted back to electricity via fuel cells.

**Solar and wind resource abundance**: The Sun delivers approximately 173,000 terawatts (TW) of power to Earth's surface, while global total energy consumption is approximately 18–20 TW and global electricity consumption is approximately 25,000 terawatt-hours (TWh) per year (IEA, 2023, *World Energy Outlook*). Wind and solar resources globally exceed current energy demand by factors of several hundred to several thousand. Resource availability is not a constraint to 100% renewable power; harvesting, converting, and storing that energy is the engineering challenge.

**Cost evidence**: Levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) calculations from the International Renewable Energy Agency show that utility-scale solar PV and onshore wind are now cheaper to build and operate than new natural gas or coal plants in most of the world (IRENA, 2023, *Renewable Power Generation Costs*). In 2023, new solar capacity added globally cost between $24–$48/MWh on average, compared to $57–$148/MWh for new gas-peaker plants. Offshore wind LCOE has also declined substantially and is competitive with new combined-cycle gas in many regions. The cost argument against renewables applies to a past technology environment, not the present one.

**Grid stability evidence from high-penetration systems**: The critique that high renewable penetration destabilizes grid frequency and reliability has been empirically tested in operating grids. In Australia's National Electricity Market, South Australia has operated with renewable generation (primarily wind and solar) exceeding 60–70% of electricity demand at times, and grid managers have developed operational protocols — including synchronous condensers for inertia, demand response, and battery storage systems like the Hornsdale Power Reserve — that maintain grid stability at these penetration levels (AEMO, 2022, *2022 Integrated System Plan*). California's grid routinely handles >50% renewable share without reliability events attributable to renewable intermittency.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty — the frozen technology fallacy and the extrapolation failure of engineering intuitions**:

The most important epistemic mechanism behind the persistence of the "renewables can't do it" claim is what might be called the frozen technology fallacy: assessing the capabilities of a technology based on its historical cost and performance trajectory at a fixed past point, rather than its present or projected future trajectory. The history of solar energy is perhaps the clearest case in modern technology of a cost curve that has defied pessimistic extrapolation with near-total consistency.

In 2010, solar PV modules cost approximately $2 per watt to produce. In 2023, they cost approximately $0.15–0.20 per watt — a greater than 90% decline in 13 years. This cost trajectory is one consequence of Swanson's Law, the empirically observed pattern that photovoltaic module costs have fallen approximately 20% with every doubling of cumulative manufactured volume since the 1970s (Swanson, 2006, *Progress in Photovoltaics*). Energy analysts who in 2010 concluded that solar would remain expensive and marginal were not wrong based on 2010 data — but their projections failed to account for the learning curve dynamics that had been visible in the data for decades. Meanwhile, analysts who did correctly extrapolate from learning curves were consistently told they were being naïvely optimistic. The predictions of the Jacobson-type 100% renewable modeling community — widely dismissed as technically utopian in 2010 — are now being validated by cost curves that have in many cases beaten even their optimistic projections.

This is a deep epistemic problem for non-specialists evaluating expert claims. Both the skeptics and the optimists cited real data and offered genuine modeling. The difference was not in the quality of data access but in the assumptions about technology learning rates and system integration costs. Non-specialist evaluators — policymakers, journalists, the public — often lack the specialized knowledge to assess which set of assumptions was more warranted by the learning curve literature, and so they default to the institutional consensus or the side that sounds like "realistic engineering" versus "wishful thinking." Skepticism about optimistic technological projections is epistemically virtuous in general; it becomes a systematic error specifically in the domain of technologies on steep learning-curve cost trajectories.

There is also a strong asymmetry in how engineering intuition works in energy systems. Physical intuitions about how electricity grids work were built during the fossil fuel era, when the engineering optimization problem was: maintain enough dispatchable generation (generators you can turn on and off at will) to meet variable demand. In this mental model, intermittent generation creates a problem by definition, because it produces power on its own schedule rather than on demand's schedule. The engineering intuition trained on decades of fossil fuel grid management naturally categorizes intermittency as a near-fatal flaw. The resolution — that the problem can be managed by combining geographic distribution of generation, temporal storage, demand flexibility, and some firm dispatchable backup — requires rethinking the optimization problem itself, from "match variable demand with dispatchable supply" to "match supply and demand using a portfolio of resources including storage and flexibility." This reconceptualization is not obvious to people whose engineering intuitions were formed in the previous paradigm.


## 4. Verdict

**FALSE as stated**: The claim that renewable energy cannot power modern civilization is refuted by:
1. Existence proofs: countries and regions already operating near-100% renewable electricity grids reliably and affordably.
2. Rigorously peer-reviewed modeling studies from multiple independent research groups in multiple countries showing 100% renewable electricity feasibility.
3. Cost data showing renewables are now cheaper than fossil fuels as new builds in most geographies.
4. Empirical operation of grids at 50–70% renewable share without the reliability failures predicted by the "can't do it" claim.

**Important nuance on the timeline and path**: The claim is false as an impossibility assertion, but this does not mean the transition is costless, trivial, or guaranteed to succeed at the required pace for climate targets. There are genuine difficult problems: long-duration storage (days to weeks), seasonal storage for energy systems in cold climates, decarbonization of high-temperature industrial heat (steel, cement, glass), aviation and shipping fuels, and the political economy of transitioning away from incumbent fossil fuel industries. These are real, hard engineering and economic challenges — but they are the challenges of an achievable transition, not evidence of an impossible one.

**The industrial heat caveat**: The claim is most defensible (though still overstated) in its application to industrial processes requiring sustained temperatures above 1,000°C, such as steel smelting, cement kiln operations, and glass manufacturing. A significant portion of current industrial heat is technically difficult to electrify directly, and hydrogen or other electro-fuels are required for some pathways. The claim is false as applied to electricity generation; it is a more legitimate concern as applied to the full energy system transition, though the "impossible" framing remains too strong given active development of high-temperature heat pumps, green hydrogen, and electric arc furnaces.

**The timeline question**: It is a separate question — consistent with the feasibility claim being true — whether the transition can happen fast enough to meet climate warming targets of 1.5°C or 2°C above pre-industrial temperatures. Grid buildout, permitting, manufacturing supply chains, and financing all constrain deployment rates. The IPCC's sixth assessment report (IPCC, 2022, *Mitigation of Climate Change — Working Group III*) concludes that the required energy transition is technically and economically feasible but requires policy interventions to accelerate deployment beyond current trajectories. The word "feasible" in that context means "not physically impossible and economically rational"; it does not mean "already happening fast enough."


## 5. The Wider Picture

**The historical record of energy transition doubts**: Skepticism about major energy transitions is historically consistent and historically consistent in being wrong. When coal began displacing wood and biomass in the 18th century, there were doubts about scalability. When oil began displacing coal in transportation in the late 19th century, there were questions about whether oil could be extracted in sufficient quantities. When nuclear power was proposed in the 1950s, early optimism was succeeded by skepticism about economics and safety. In each case, the technical challenges were real but ultimately resolved — though timing and path were not always as optimists predicted. The current skepticism about renewable energy scalability is the latest instance of this recurring pattern, occurring against the backdrop of an already-validated cost decline that has already resolved the most-cited objections of a decade ago.

**The nuclear energy debate within decarbonization**: A significant current debate within the decarbonization community is about the role of nuclear power in a net-zero energy system. Some analysts (Lovering et al., 2016, *Energy Policy*; Lazard, 2023, *Levelized Cost of Energy Analysis*) argue that maintaining existing nuclear capacity and potentially building new nuclear plants is important for reliable decarbonized electricity in regions without strong renewable resources or geographic interconnection. Others argue that the cost trajectories favor renewables over new nuclear construction. This debate is genuine and ongoing — but it is a debate about the optimal decarbonization pathway, not about whether decarbonization is possible. Both sides agree that a decarbonized electricity system is achievable; they disagree about the mix of technologies.

**The fossil fuel industry's claim history**: Fossil fuel industry trade groups and affiliated think tanks have funded and promoted versions of the "renewables can't do it" argument for decades, often updated to address whatever the most recent objection was after previous objections were resolved. In the 1980s, the claim was that solar was too expensive at any scale. In the 2000s, it was that grid integration at >10% was impossible. In the 2010s, it shifted to >30% being unreliable. Each threshold has been exceeded in operating grids without the predicted reliability collapses. The pattern of retreating thresholds is characteristic of arguments driven by motivated reasoning rather than engineering analysis (Oreskes & Conway, 2010, *Merchants of Doubt*).

**Storage technology trajectory**: One of the few genuinely remaining technical challenges to a fully renewable electric system is long-duration storage — energy storage capable of holding electricity over periods of days to weeks to balance seasonal generation mismatches. Current lithium-ion batteries are well-suited to 4–8 hour balancing but are not yet cost-effective at longer durations. However, multiple storage technologies in various stages of commercial development address this need: pumped hydro, compressed air energy storage, vanadium flow batteries, gravity-based storage, and green hydrogen. The specific technology that will win is unclear; that the problem is being solved is documented by the investment and deployment data (BloombergNEF, 2023, *Energy Storage Market Outlook*). Characterizing the current state of long-duration storage as permanently blocking 100% renewables is equivalent to characterizing the first laptops in 1980 as permanently blocking portable computing because batteries were too heavy and short-lived.

**Grid decarbonization as an enabling sector**: A final point often missed in the "renewables can't do it" framing is that electricity grid decarbonization enables decarbonization of other sectors through electrification. When a grid is predominantly powered by fossil fuels, electrifying transportation or heating increases fossil fuel consumption (at the generator). When a grid is predominantly powered by renewables, electrifying transportation and heating dramatically reduces fossil fuel consumption and greenhouse gas emissions. This means that grid-level renewable success is not just good for its own sake but is a force multiplier for the entire decarbonization agenda — and makes the impossibility claim more consequentially wrong, not merely technically wrong.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**The scientific and energy institution consensus**: The IPCC, the International Energy Agency, the International Renewable Energy Agency, BloombergNEF, Wood Mackenzie, Lazard, and every major energy-sector research institution publishes analyses that treat 100% renewable electricity as technically achievable by mid-century or sooner using technologies already commercial or near-commercial. None of these institutions use "impossibility" language. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Cycle specifically includes 100% renewable scenarios as technically feasible in multiple national and global energy models (IPCC, 2022, *Working Group III Report*).

**Snopes and related outlets**: Fact-checkers addressing specific versions of this claim — that Germany's Energiewende has failed, that California's grid will collapse from renewables, that renewables are inherently unreliable — have consistently found the specific impossibility claims false while acknowledging genuine transition challenges. The framing of these checks typically mirrors the verdict here: the specific claim of impossibility is false, the challenges of the transition are real, and the direction of travel for costs and feasibility is consistently toward renewables being more capable than critics predicted.

**The EROEI debate**: A technical variant of the "can't do it" claim invokes energy return on energy invested (EROEI) — the ratio of energy produced by an energy source over its lifetime to the energy required to build and operate it. Critics have argued that solar and wind have low EROEI ratios that make them thermodynamically incapable of sustaining a complex industrialized civilization. Meta-analyses of EROEI calculations for solar PV and wind turbines consistently find life-cycle EROEI ratios in the range of 15–40:1 for solar and 18–50:1 for wind — lower than some fossil fuels but substantially above the ~7:1 EROEI threshold typically identified as the minimum for sustaining modern industrial civilization (Hall et al., 2014, *Sustainability*; Raugei et al., 2012, *Energy Policy*). The EROEI-based impossibility claim is false on these numbers even using critics' own analytical framework.

**The calibrated uncertainty position**: Intellectually honest evaluation of this claim acknowledges: it is false as an impossibility claim for electricity; it is a legitimate area of challenge for certain hard-to-electrify industrial applications; the timeline for a complete energy transition is uncertain and may not be fast enough for the most aggressive climate targets without significant policy intervention; and the specific technology solutions for long-duration storage and industrial heat are not yet commercially mature at scale. These honest uncertainties do not rehabilitate the "can't do it" claim — they characterize a feasible but difficult transition, not an impossible one.
