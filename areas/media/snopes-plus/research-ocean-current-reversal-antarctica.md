# Scientists warn that ocean currents around Antarctica are reversing


## 1. The Claim

The claim has circulated in multiple distinctive forms since approximately 2023, coinciding with the publication of peer-reviewed research on Antarctic Bottom Water (AABW) formation and the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC). The core version states that scientists have warned — often described as an "urgent warning" or an "alarm" — that ocean currents around Antarctica are reversing or have begun to reverse, with catastrophic consequences for global climate, sea level, and marine ecosystems.

Popular versions of the claim include: that the overturning circulation in the Southern Ocean is moving in the wrong direction; that deep ocean currents that have flowed in consistent directions for millennia are now running backward; that the conveyor belt of global ocean circulation is buckling under the pressure of Antarctic ice melt and freshwater influx; and that this reversal is triggering a cascade of climate feedback effects that will accelerate warming across the Southern Hemisphere and beyond. The claim is frequently illustrated with dramatic oceanographic diagrams showing circular current patterns with arrows added to suggest reversal, or with satellite imagery of Antarctic ice shelf collapse juxtaposed with global ocean circulation maps.

The claim has appeared in major news outlets, social media science accounts, climate advocacy content, and documentary and podcast discussions of Antarctic climate science. It has become embedded in a broader genre of existential climate alarm that has grown significantly in public prominence following the high-visibility collapse of portions of the Thwaites Glacier catchment and the record-breaking Antarctic sea ice minimum values recorded in 2023 and subsequent years.

The claim draws on real published science. There are genuine peer-reviewed findings about dramatic changes in Antarctic ocean dynamics that have serious implications for global climate. The question is whether "reversing" accurately captures what that science says, and whether the popular transmission of the underlying research has faithfully represented the nature, magnitude, and mechanism of the change.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The foundational science: what Antarctic ocean circulation is and why it matters.** The ocean circulation system adjacent to Antarctica is central to global climate regulation. Cold, dense bottom water forms in specific coastal polynyas around the Antarctic continent — areas of open water surrounded by sea ice — where intense cold and evaporation produce water of exceptional density that sinks to the ocean floor and spreads northward across the deep ocean basins. This Antarctic Bottom Water is the densest large water mass in the ocean and drives the deepest layer of the global thermohaline circulation, sometimes called the ocean conveyor belt.

Above the AABW layer, Antarctic Intermediate Water and Antarctic Circumpolar Current dynamics complete a multi-layered circulation system that exchanges heat, carbon dioxide, and nutrients between the deep ocean and the surface on timescales ranging from decades to centuries. The Southern Ocean accounts for a disproportionate fraction of the ocean's uptake of anthropogenic CO₂ and heat — estimates suggest the Southern Ocean absorbs approximately 40% of the ocean's anthropogenic carbon uptake and approximately 75% of the ocean's heat uptake from human-caused warming (DeVries et al., 2019, *Annual Review of Marine Science*).

**The actual scientific findings.** The research that generated the "reversal" headlines was primarily published in two landmark papers in 2023. Matthew England and colleagues (2023, *Nature Climate Change*) used a suite of climate model simulations forced with observed and projected Antarctic ice melt to project substantial slowdown of the overturning circulation in the Southern Ocean under continued greenhouse gas emissions. Their headline finding, widely reported, was that the overturning circulation could slow by more than 40% over the coming decades, reducing the formation and export of Antarctic Bottom Water at rates without precedent in the observed record.

Concurrently, Casimir de Lavergne and colleagues, and separate work by Cessi and collaborators, documented observational evidence from Argo float arrays and moored current measurements that AABW formation in several key regions had already declined measurably in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries. Shimada and colleagues (2012, *Deep-Sea Research*) had established earlier observational evidence of AABW warming and volume decrease, and the 2023 papers built substantially on that foundation.

What England et al. (2023) and associated studies describe is a *slowdown* and a *weakening* of the Southern Ocean overturning — a reduction in the rate and volume of AABW formation and export — driven primarily by freshwater input from melting Antarctic ice sheets. The freshwater reduces the density of surface water in the polynya formation zones, making it less likely to sink. This weakens the vertical exchange that drives the overturning. It does not reverse the direction of flow.

**What "sloshing" and feedback studies actually found.** Additional research that may have contributed to the "reversal" framing includes work on transient circulation responses to freshwater perturbations. Lago and England (2019, *Geophysical Research Letters*) modelled the response of Southern Ocean circulation to sudden large freshwater events and found that localised circulation cells adjacent to the ice sheet margin could reorganise or temporarily reverse as density stratification changed around ice-melt point sources. This is a real modelled phenomenon, but it applies to small-scale local circulation features in the immediate vicinity of major melt events, not to the large-scale basin-wide thermohaline circulation structure.

Similarly, Mackie and colleagues (2020, *Journal of Climate*) documented observations of Southern Ocean circulation changes including some short-term anomalous period in limited sectors of the Antarctic coastal boundary current structure. Changes in eddy activity, shelf water exchange, and intermediate water ventilation do involve complex reorganisation of flow patterns, and in some limited areas and periods, localized current measurements have shown anomalous reversals of expected patterns. These are real observational findings; they are also highly localised and physically distinct from the global-scale circulation reversal that popular coverage describes.

**The AMOC confusion.** Part of the distortion in the popular claim may derive from conflation of two separate but related circulation concerns: the Southern Ocean overturning discussed above, and the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), which is the North Atlantic component of global thermohaline circulation that includes the Gulf Stream system. AMOC has also been the subject of major research attention documenting its weakening (Caesar et al., 2018, *Nature*; Boers, 2021, *Nature Climate Change*), and AMOC disruption has been the subject of numerous popular science articles and a Hollywood movie (*The Day After Tomorrow*, which dramatised an imagined reversal). The "reversal" framing in popular climate discourse may partly be a cross-contamination from fictionalized accounts of AMOC disruption being applied to the separate and differently mechanised Southern Ocean findings.

**What the actual risk is.** The real findings are genuinely alarming and deserve serious public attention. A 40%+ slowdown of Southern Ocean overturning circulation would have major consequences for: deep ocean heat and carbon uptake; nutrient cycling and the marine food webs of the Southern Ocean and beyond; sea level change (because weaker deep bottom water circulation affects the thermal expansion profile of the ocean); and the stability of ice shelves around Antarctica (because warmer Circumpolar Deep Water accessing ice shelf cavities drives accelerating basal melt). England et al. (2023) describe a feedback system in which the freshwater-induced slowdown reduces vertical mixing that would otherwise bring cold deep water upward, allowing surface warming to intensify near ice margins and accelerate ice melt, further freshening the surface and further suppressing deep convection. This is a real, documented, and concerning feedback mechanism.

The risk is not that currents are running backward. The risk is that a critical circulation system is weakening in ways that will reduce the ocean's capacity to absorb heat and carbon, accelerate Antarctic ice loss, and alter Southern Ocean nutrient dynamics with cascading effects on marine productivity and climate feedbacks. This is a more complex and in some ways more insidious risk than a dramatic reversal, because it does not produce a single catastrophic threshold event but rather a gradual degradation of critical Earth system functions.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Reversal is a more intuitive and dramatic concept than weakening.** The actual scientific concern — a quantitative reduction in overturning circulation volume transport, measured in Sverdrups — does not translate naturally into everyday language. To understand why a 40% slowdown in a number measured in millions of cubic metres per second per year matters, an audience needs a fairly detailed mental model of how the thermohaline circulation regulates global climate, how deep water formation controls ocean heat uptake, and why the Southern Ocean is particularly important in the global carbon cycle. "AABW overturning transport weakens by 40% under RCP 8.5 forcing" is accurate, important, and essentially unintelligible to a general audience without considerable contextualisation.

"Ocean currents reversing" communicates a sense of dramatic abnormality, a world-turned-upside-down physical event, that matches the emotional weight the underlying story carries but does so by misrepresenting the mechanism. The reversal framing activates a cognitive model of catastrophic disruption that "weakening" does not activate with the same force. This is a case where the inaccurate framing is emotionally more appropriate to the underlying concern than the accurate framing, which creates systematic pressure toward the inaccuracy in every retelling.

Climate psychologists and science communication researchers have documented this pattern: audience comprehension of and concern about climate risks is higher when the risks are described in terms of dramatic qualitative change (reversal, collapse, shutdown) than when described accurately in terms of quantitative reduction (slowdown, weakening, reduction). Neville and colleagues (2021, *Global Environmental Change*) found in audience studies that threshold-language descriptions of climate tipping points produced substantially higher engagement and reported concern than probabilistic descriptions of the same changes. This audience response dynamic creates incentive for science communicators and journalists to adopt more dramatic language even when that language is technically inaccurate.

**Media coverage that conflates AMOC and AABW.** News articles about Antarctic overturning changes frequently appear in close temporal proximity to articles about AMOC, which is a better-known system with a longer popular communication history. Readers and commentators moving between articles about the two systems easily conflate them, and a "reversal" frame established in AMOC-associated coverage (particularly from *The Day After Tomorrow*-influenced discourse) transfers to Southern Ocean stories that do not involve reversals. The technical distinction between AMOC and AABW-driven Southern Ocean overturning is not well-established in general public understanding, and the shared vocabulary of "conveyor belt," "thermohaline circulation," and "ocean currents" makes conflation effortless.

**The urgency legitimisation function.** Climate communication operates in an environment where advocates for climate action have repeatedly found that overstating certainty or severity generates more public attention and policy engagement than careful qualified statements. This is a documented dynamic in the sociology of climate communication (Nisbet and Mooney, 2007, *Science*). The "reversal" framing of Antarctic ocean circulation change serves a legitimisation function for urgency in climate discourse: if currents are *reversing*, we are well past the point of gradual change and into acute planetary crisis. If currents are merely *slowing*, the same level of urgency is harder to communicate. This motivational dynamic does not excuse the inaccuracy but does explain its persistence among audiences with genuine concerns about climate change.


## 4. Verdict

**The underlying science is real and serious; the "reversal" framing is inaccurate.** Scientists have published robust peer-reviewed findings documenting concerning and accelerating slowdown of Antarctic Bottom Water formation and export, driven by freshwater input from melting Antarctic ice. The consequences of continued weakening of this circulation are significant for global climate regulation, deep ocean carbon and heat uptake, and Antarctic ice dynamics. The science merits exactly the level of public concern and policy attention it has generated.

The claim that ocean currents "are reversing" misrepresents the scientific findings. The documented change is a reduction in volume transport — a weakening of existing circulation — not a reversal of flow direction. The distinction is not merely semantic: a reversal would imply a different set of physical mechanisms, different consequences, different feedback dynamics, and different potential intervention points than a weakening. Accurate communication about the nature of the change is prerequisite to accurate public understanding of what is at stake and what responses are appropriate.

The story belongs in the category of distorted-but-grounded: a genuine and serious scientific finding, communicated with a framing that introduces material inaccuracy in the name of impact, resulting in public understanding of the risk that is qualitatively wrong even if the directional concern (things are getting worse, and fast) is correct.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Antarctic ocean current story is among the most consequential examples of a widespread problem in climate science communication: the conversion of complex gradient changes into dramatic qualitative thresholds in order to communicate urgency. The scientific literature on tipping points, domino effects, and abrupt change in the climate system provides legitimate vocabulary for discussing threshold behaviour in some Earth system components. But it also provides scaffolding for overstating the threshold character of changes that are actually continuous and quantitative.

The distinction between "weakening" and "reversal" in ocean circulation matters because the two scenarios have different implications for timeline, for detectability, and for interpretations of what a successful climate policy response would look like. A weakening circulation responds gradually to greenhouse gas forcing and is theoretically reversible if forcing is reduced; reversal implies qualitatively different behaviour that may be self-sustaining even after forcing is removed. Public support for ambitious climate policy may benefit in the short term from the more alarming framing, but it suffers in the long term from the credibility costs when predictions framed in reversal language fail to materialise on the timeline implied.

The England et al. (2023) paper and related research has been, by any measure, a significant contribution to ocean science and climate assessment. The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report incorporated Southern Ocean circulation change as a high-confidence finding with potential high-impact consequences (IPCC, 2021, *Chapter 9: Ocean, Cryosphere and Sea Level Change*). The policy stakes are real, the scientific basis is solid, and the communication challenge of conveying the severity of a quantitative gradient change without distorting its qualitative character is genuinely difficult. Accuracy in this domain is not pedantry; it is the foundation of public trust in climate science at a moment when that trust is under sustained political attack from multiple directions.

The broader media literacy principle instantiated by this case is the importance of direction-versus-magnitude distinctions in scientific claims. A claim that something is *going in the wrong direction* is categorically different from a claim that it is *moving in the reversed direction.* Natural language encourages collapsing this distinction because the word "reverse" can mean either "deteriorate" or "run backward," and speakers who intend the former often produce sentences that listeners interpret as the latter. Precision in the direction of "slowdown" and "weakening" rather than "reversal" is achievable without sacrificing the urgency the findings warrant.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The "ocean currents reversing" claim has been addressed, partially, by several science communication outlets and fact-checking adjacent publications, though it has not received a formal fact-check verdict from major platforms. Climate Feedback — a specialist fact-checking network for climate science — has reviewed adjacent claims about AMOC collapse and Southern Ocean circulation and generally found that popular coverage overstates the reversal character of documented changes while correctly characterising the directionality of concern. Their typical verdict on this class of claim is "partially inaccurate framing of a real phenomenon," which captures the situation accurately but does not reduce to a clean true/false that general audiences find satisfying.

The underlying peer-reviewed research is directly accessible: England et al. (2023) in *Nature Climate Change* is open-access and accompanied by a press release from the University of New South Wales that is fairly careful about the language of "slowdown" versus "reversal." Readers who consult the original paper will encounter accurate technical language; the distortion occurs primarily in the secondhand retelling. This highlights the importance, for both journalists and general readers, of tracing claims about scientific findings to the primary source before amplifying them.

For educators and fact-checkers working in this space, the practical challenge is that correcting the "reversal" framing requires explaining enough Southern Ocean circulation physics to make the distinction meaningful — a significant cognitive demand compared to simply asserting that things are indeed alarming. This asymmetry between the complexity of the correction and the simplicity of the distortion is a structural feature of many science communication failures and directly informs why the accurate framing is systematically outcompeted in the attention economy. Addressing it effectively requires sustained investment in baseline ocean literacy, not just case-by-case fact-check corrections.
