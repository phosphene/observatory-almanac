# "Humans Are Getting Dumber / IQ Scores Are Declining"


## 1. The Claim

The popular assertion that "humans are getting dumber" or that "IQ scores are declining" has become a fixture of cultural pessimism, appearing in everything from social media discussions about "kids these days" to more serious academic debates about cognitive trends. This claim typically manifests in several forms: that younger generations are less intelligent than their predecessors, that standardized test scores are falling, that cognitive abilities are deteriorating due to technology dependence, or that intelligence quotient measurements show a measurable decline in human cognitive capacity over recent decades.

The claim often carries implicit explanations ranging from educational system failures and increased screen time to demographic changes and environmental toxins. In its most alarmist versions, it suggests an impending cognitive collapse that threatens technological civilization itself. More moderate versions simply assert that measurable intelligence has peaked and begun to decline, reversing historical trends of cognitive improvement.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim demonstrates both empirical grounding and significant distortion. The core factual assertion — that IQ scores have declined in some developed countries since the mid-1990s — is supported by peer-reviewed research. However, popular discussions typically strip away crucial context about what this decline means, why it might be occurring, and how it relates to broader concepts of human intelligence.

The distortion operates at several levels. First, the specific finding of declining scores in particular cognitive subtests within specific populations becomes generalized to "humans getting dumber" globally. Second, IQ test performance becomes conflated with intelligence broadly conceived, ignoring ongoing debates about what these tests actually measure. Third, the decline is often presented as unprecedented when it actually represents a reversal of an earlier trend that itself was historically unusual.

The fidelity gap widens when the claim serves ideological purposes. Conservative commentators may attribute declining scores to educational progressivism or cultural decay, while progressive voices might emphasize socioeconomic inequality or environmental racism. Both interpretations leap from limited data to sweeping cultural diagnoses, transforming a narrow psychometric finding into a comprehensive social narrative.


The epistemological challenges multiply when moving from documented trends to explanatory theories. The decline in measured IQ scores is empirically established in several countries, but explaining why it's happening requires navigating competing theoretical frameworks with different assumptions about intelligence, causation, and social change.

Environmental explanations focus on factors that could affect cognitive development across entire populations. Lead exposure, despite dramatic reductions since the 1970s, remains a candidate because of its established neurotoxic effects and the possibility of threshold effects or delayed consequences. Endocrine disruptor chemicals, particularly those affecting thyroid function during fetal development, represent another plausible environmental pathway supported by toxicological research.

Nutritional factors present a paradox: while severe malnutrition has declined globally, the rise of processed foods and the decline of certain nutrients (like iodine in some regions) could theoretically impact cognitive development. However, these explanations struggle to account for the timing and geographic specificity of the observed declines.

Educational and cultural explanations emphasize changes in how children spend their time and what skills they develop. The shift from print-based to screen-based media, the decline in reading for pleasure, and changes in educational priorities (toward test preparation and away from broader intellectual exploration) could plausibly affect performance on traditional intelligence tests. However, these changes might reflect adaptation to new cognitive demands rather than absolute cognitive decline.

The most contentious explanations involve demographic factors. Some researchers have suggested that differential fertility rates — with higher-educated populations having fewer children — could create population-level declines in average cognitive ability. This "dysgenesis" hypothesis has a troubling historical connection to eugenic thinking, though contemporary proponents argue for purely statistical rather than genetic interpretations.

Immigration-based explanations face similar interpretive difficulties. While large-scale immigration from countries with different educational traditions could theoretically affect population averages, this explanation fails to account for within-family declines and often carries xenophobic implications that distort scientific discourse.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The scientific foundation for investigating intelligence trends rests on the psychometric tradition established in the early 20th century, particularly the development of standardized intelligence tests and the statistical methods for analyzing population-level cognitive performance. The Flynn Effect, documented by James Flynn in the 1980s, provided the historical baseline against which current trends are measured.

Flynn's original research revealed that IQ scores had been rising approximately 3 points per decade throughout the 20th century across developed nations. This finding was robust across different countries, different tests, and different cognitive domains, though the gains were largest in abstract reasoning tasks (like Raven's Progressive Matrices) and smallest in crystallized intelligence measures (like vocabulary tests). The effect was so consistent that test publishers had to periodically renorm their instruments to maintain average scores around 100.

The epistemological framework for studying the Flynn Effect relied on several key assumptions: that intelligence tests measure meaningful cognitive abilities, that population-level changes in test performance reflect genuine changes in cognitive capacity, and that cross-temporal comparisons remain valid despite cultural and educational changes. These assumptions were always contested but became central to interpreting both the original gains and the more recent declines.

Contemporary research documenting declining scores employs the same basic methodology but faces additional interpretive challenges. The most influential study, published by Bratsberg and Rogeberg in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 2018, analyzed Norwegian military conscription data spanning several decades. This dataset offered unusual advantages: nearly universal male participation, consistent testing procedures, and the ability to control for family-level factors by comparing brothers.

The Norwegian study found that IQ scores had declined approximately 0.2 points per year since the mid-1990s, a substantial reversal of previous gains. Crucially, the researchers demonstrated that this decline occurred within families — younger brothers scored lower than their older siblings — ruling out explanations based on demographic composition changes. This within-family finding strengthened causal inferences while deepening the interpretive puzzle.

Similar patterns emerged in other Nordic countries (Denmark, Finland) and the United Kingdom, though the timing and magnitude varied. France showed declines beginning somewhat earlier, while some countries (like the Netherlands) showed continued gains or stability. The heterogeneity of trends across nations complicated any simple explanation while confirming that the Norwegian findings weren't merely statistical artifacts.


## 4. Verdict

**CONTRAST: Documented But Distorted**

The claim that "humans are getting dumber" contains a genuine empirical core — IQ scores have measurably declined in several developed countries since the mid-1990s — but this finding is routinely distorted by oversimplified interpretations that ignore crucial scientific context.

**What the evidence shows:** Peer-reviewed research documents declining performance on standardized intelligence tests in Norway, Denmark, Finland, France, and the United Kingdom over the past 2-3 decades. The Norwegian study is particularly compelling because it demonstrates within-family declines, ruling out simple demographic explanations. The magnitude of decline (approximately 0.2 points per year) represents a significant reversal of the Flynn Effect, which had shown consistent gains throughout the 20th century.

**What the evidence doesn't show:** The research does not demonstrate that humans are becoming less intelligent in any absolute sense, that cognitive abilities are deteriorating globally, or that the decline necessarily represents a crisis for human civilization. Intelligence tests measure specific cognitive skills that correlate with academic and professional success but do not capture the full range of human intellectual capabilities.

**The distortion mechanism:** Popular discussions typically transform narrow psychometric findings into sweeping claims about human nature and social decline. The complexity of intelligence research — including debates about what intelligence tests measure, how environmental factors influence cognitive development, and why scores might change over time — gets compressed into simple narratives that serve various cultural and political agendas.

**Why this matters:** The reverse Flynn Effect represents a genuine scientific puzzle that deserves serious investigation. Understanding why cognitive test performance is declining could reveal important information about child development, educational effectiveness, environmental health, or social change. However, premature conclusions about human cognitive decline risk misallocating resources and attention while obscuring more nuanced questions about how human intelligence adapts to changing technological and social environments.

**The deeper issue:** This case illustrates how scientific findings about human characteristics inevitably become entangled with social anxieties and political arguments. The same data can support narratives about educational failure, environmental degradation, demographic change, or technological alienation, depending on the interpreter's prior commitments. Maintaining scientific rigor in this context requires constant vigilance against the temptation to transform limited findings into comprehensive explanations for complex social phenomena.

The decline in IQ scores is real, but "humans getting dumber" is an interpretation that reveals more about our cultural fears than about human cognitive capacity.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The reverse Flynn Effect sits within broader debates about human cognitive evolution, the nature of intelligence, and the relationship between technological change and mental capacity. These debates have deep historical roots but have intensified as digital technologies reshape how humans process information and solve problems.

The Flynn Effect itself challenged earlier assumptions about the stability of human intelligence. When Flynn first documented rising scores, the finding seemed to contradict both genetic theories of intelligence (which predicted stability) and cultural pessimism about modern life (which predicted decline). The gains were too large and too rapid to reflect genetic evolution, suggesting that environmental factors could substantially influence population-level cognitive performance.

Various explanations for the original Flynn Effect gained currency over time. Improved nutrition, particularly the elimination of severe protein and micronutrient deficiencies, likely contributed to cognitive gains in developing countries. The expansion of formal education exposed larger populations to abstract reasoning tasks similar to those on intelligence tests. Reduced exposure to infectious diseases and environmental toxins, particularly lead, probably supported better brain development.

Perhaps most intriguingly, the complexity of modern life itself seemed to enhance certain cognitive skills. The visual richness of urban environments, the problem-solving demands of new technologies, and the abstract thinking required by bureaucratic institutions all potentially contributed to rising scores on intelligence tests designed to measure these exact capabilities.

The reverse Flynn Effect challenges this narrative of continuous cognitive improvement while raising questions about what intelligence tests actually measure. If scores can rise for decades and then fall, what does this tell us about the stability and meaning of human cognitive abilities? Are the tests measuring something fundamental about thinking capacity, or are they capturing more superficial adaptations to particular cultural and educational contexts?

Contemporary cognitive science offers a more nuanced view of intelligence than the unitary IQ model assumed by early psychometricians. Research on multiple intelligences, emotional intelligence, practical intelligence, and cultural variations in cognitive style suggests that human intellectual capacity is far more diverse and context-dependent than traditional tests capture. From this perspective, declining scores might reflect changing cognitive priorities rather than absolute deterioration.

The digital revolution adds another layer of complexity. Modern humans routinely perform cognitive tasks — like navigating complex information networks, coordinating with distributed teams, or managing multiple information streams — that would have been impossible for earlier generations. These new competencies might not register on traditional intelligence tests, which emphasize particular types of abstract reasoning that may be less relevant in information-rich environments.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly addressed this multifaceted claim in a comprehensive fact-check, likely because it encompasses multiple distinct but related assertions that would require separate evaluation. The absence of a unified Snopes verdict reflects the claim's complexity rather than its insignificance — the question touches on legitimate scientific research while also serving as a vehicle for various cultural anxieties and political arguments.

When Snopes has addressed related claims about educational achievement or generational differences, their approach typically emphasizes the importance of distinguishing between different types of cognitive measurements, the role of demographic factors, and the difference between correlation and causation in observational data. This methodological caution is particularly relevant to intelligence research, where social and political implications have historically led to misinterpretation of scientific findings.
