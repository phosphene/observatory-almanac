# No, That Ballroom Dancing Video Does Not Show Robots at Shanghai Disneyland

## 1. The Claim

In November 2020, a video began circulating widely across social media depicting a man and a woman performing an elegant ballroom dance. The clip was visually striking: the dancers moved with extraordinary precision, their posture immaculate, their turns and footwork executed with a fluidity that seemed almost mechanical in its perfection. The video was shared with captions asserting that the performers were not human at all — they were, the posts claimed, robots manufactured in China and performing at the Shanghai Disneyland Resort.

The claim gained particular traction when it was tweeted by a verified account belonging to an Indian politician and actor, amplifying its reach exponentially. The framing was consistent across posts: these were "robots made in China," capable of realistic facial expressions and human-like movement, delivering a "five minute performance" at Shanghai Disneyland. The implication was clear — Chinese technology had advanced to a stage where humanoid robots could not only walk and gesture but could perform complex, emotionally resonant choreography indistinguishable from that of a trained human dancer.

The video accumulated millions of views across Twitter, Facebook, and WhatsApp. Comment sections overflowed with a mixture of awe, alarm, and speculation. Some viewers expressed wonder at the apparent achievements of Chinese robotics. Others voiced existential anxiety about a future in which machines could replicate the most intimate and expressive of human arts. A smaller contingent questioned the claim, noting inconsistencies, but their voices were largely drowned out by the viral momentum of the original narrative.

The claim was, in every particular, false.

## 2. What Actually Happened

The video that millions watched and shared as evidence of Chinese robotic prowess was, in reality, a recording of a performance from the tenth series of *Strictly Come Dancing*, the long-running BBC One dance competition that has been a fixture of British television since 2004. The performers were Abbey Clancy, the English model and television presenter, and her professional dance partner Aljaž Škorjanec, the Slovenian-born dancer who joined the show's professional roster in 2013.

The specific performance was a Viennese waltz, choreographed for the show and broadcast during the 2013 series. Clancy and Škorjanec's routine was widely praised at the time for its elegance, technical precision, and emotional expressiveness — qualities that, ironically, would later fuel the misidentification of Clancy and Škorjanec as non-human. The performance was available on the official BBC YouTube channel, where it had been publicly accessible for years before the robot claim emerged. Anyone with a search engine and a few minutes of curiosity could have traced the original source.

The context of *Strictly Come Dancing* is critical to understanding both the quality of the performance and the absurdity of the misattribution. The show pairs celebrities with professional ballroom and Latin dancers, training them intensively over weeks before live broadcasts judged by a panel of dance experts. Škorjanec, who trained at the Ljubljana Dance Academy and competed internationally before joining *Strictly*, brought years of professional discipline to every routine. Clancy, while not a professional dancer, proved a remarkably able student and went on to win the 2013 series. Their Viennese waltz — characterised by continuous turning, precise frame, and the illusion of effortlessness that is the hallmark of skilled ballroom dancing — was a high point of the competition.

The transformation of this identifiably human, identifiably British cultural product into supposed Chinese robotic technology required a chain of decontextualisation. The video was stripped of its BBC branding, its audio was sometimes replaced or muted, and it was presented without any identifying information about the performers, the show, or the country of origin. Once severed from its original context, the clip became a blank screen onto which viewers projected whatever narrative was most emotionally compelling — and in 2020, the narrative of uncanny Chinese technological achievement was extraordinarily compelling.

## 3. The Inverse Uncanny Valley

The misidentification of Clancy and Škorjanec as robots is not merely a case of viral misinformation; it is a case study in a specific and under-examined cognitive phenomenon that we might call the *inverse uncanny valley*. The term requires some unpacking.

### The Classical Uncanny Valley

The uncanny valley hypothesis, first articulated by roboticist Masahiro Mori in 1970 and translated into English more broadly in the decades that followed, describes the relationship between the human likeness of an entity and the observer's emotional response to it (Mori, 1970, *Energy*; see also Mori, MacDorman & Kageki, 2012, *IEEE Robotics & Automation Magazine*). As a robot or animated figure becomes more human-like, our affinity for it increases — up to a point. When the likeness is close but not quite right, affinity plunges into a valley of revulsion. The near-human face that is not quite human triggers discomfort, eeriness, a sense of wrongness. This is the uncanny valley proper: the zone where almost-human is worse than clearly-not-human.

Extensive empirical work has since refined Mori's original hypothesis. MacDorman and Ishiguro (2006, *Interaction Studies*) demonstrated that the uncanny valley effect is modulated by the observer's expectations and prior experience with artificial agents. Kätsyri, Förger, Mäkäräinen, and Takala (2015, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*) conducted a meta-analysis that found inconsistent evidence for the valley's depth but consistent evidence for its existence as a phenomenon — the eeriness of near-human artificial agents is real, even if its precise contours vary.

### Inversion: When the Human Becomes Suspect

What happened with the Shanghai Disneyland dancing robots claim is, in structural terms, the uncanny valley hypothesis operating in reverse. Instead of a machine being mistaken for a human and triggering unease, a human was mistaken for a machine and triggering wonder. The cognitive mechanism is the same — a mismatch between perceived category and observed behaviour — but the direction is flipped.

When human performance reaches a certain threshold of technical perfection, it can paradoxically undermine its own legibility as human. Ballroom dancing at the highest levels demands a kind of bodily control — continuous smooth movement, fixed upper-body frame, perfectly timed rotation — that suppresses many of the micro-movements, asymmetries, and improvisational adjustments that we unconsciously use to identify a being as biological. The Viennese waltz, in particular, with its relentless three-quarter time rotation and the partners' mirror-image symmetry, can produce an effect that reads as machinic precisely because the dancers have trained away the visible signs of effort.

Research on action perception supports this mechanism. Calvo-Merino, Glaser, Grèzes, Passingham, and Haggard (2005, *Cerebral Cortex*) showed, using fMRI, that observing actions within one's own motor repertoire activates the premotor cortex more strongly than observing unfamiliar actions. For an untrained observer — someone who has never danced or been close to competitive dance — a Viennese waltz performed by professionals can fall outside the reservoir of motor experience that allows for recognising human movement patterns as such. When the observer cannot kinesthetically simulate what they are seeing, the movement becomes uncategorised, and the category assignment becomes vulnerable to external suggestion.

### The Role of Geopolitical Narrative

This vulnerability is where the geopolitical dimension enters. The claim did not assert that the robots were made in Germany, or Japan, or the United States. It specified China — and this was not incidental.

By 2020, China's technological capabilities had become a subject of intense global fascination and anxiety. The success of companies like Huawei, DJI, and various AI laboratories, combined with viral videos of genuine Chinese robotics demonstrations — such as those by UBTECH Robotics and WF Robot Technology — had established a popular narrative in which China was on the verge of, or had already achieved, breakthroughs that the rest of the world might find unsettling. This narrative was amplified by both admiration and threat perception, depending on the viewer's geopolitical orientation.

The psychological literature on confirmation bias and motivated reasoning is directly relevant here. Nickerson (1998, *Review of General Psychology*) argued that confirmation bias — the tendency to search for, interpret, and recall information in a way that confirms pre-existing beliefs — is "perhaps the best known and most widely accepted notion of inferential error to come out of the literature on human reasoning." When a viewer already believes that Chinese technology is capable of producing lifelike humanoid robots, a video of extraordinarily precise dancing, stripped of context and accompanied by a confident caption, slots neatly into that belief structure. The cognitive cost of accepting the claim is near zero; the cost of investigating it is higher.

Pennycook and Rand (2019, *Cognition*) have further demonstrated that susceptibility to misinformation is predicted less by ideological bias than by a failure to engage in deliberative, analytical thinking. The viewers who shared the robot dancing video were not necessarily ideologically motivated; many were simply failing to pause and ask the most basic evaluative question: *Is this actually what the caption says it is?* In the attention economy of social media, where content is consumed in seconds and sharing requires a single tap, the analytical pause required to detect misinformation is a scarce cognitive resource.

The convergence of these three factors — the inverse uncanny valley effect, the geopolitical priming of Chinese technological supremacy, and the structural incentives of social media against analytical thinking — produced an environment in which a seven-year-old clip from a British television dance competition could be seamlessly repackaged as evidence of Chinese robotic ingenuity. The transformation was not the work of a single bad actor; it was an emergent property of how information degrades as it passes through networks optimised for engagement rather than accuracy.

## 4. The Real State of Humanoid Robotics in 2020

To understand why the claim was not merely false but implausible, it is necessary to assess what humanoid robotics could actually accomplish in 2020 — and how far those capabilities were from the performance shown in the viral video.

### What Chinese Robots Could Do

China was, by 2020, a genuine leader in several domains of robotics. Industrial robots, deployed in manufacturing at scale, were a major sector of Chinese technological development. Service robots — machines designed for reception desks, guided tours, and simple interactive tasks — were increasingly visible in Chinese public spaces. Companies like UBTECH Robotics, headquartered in Shenzhen, had produced humanoid robots such as the Walker series, capable of bipedal locomotion, basic manipulation tasks, and scripted interactions. These robots were genuinely impressive on their own terms and drew significant attention on social media and at technology exhibitions.

However, the capabilities of these robots were profoundly limited compared to what the viral video depicted. The state of bipedal humanoid locomotion in 2020 was characterised by slow, cautious movement, with significant stability constraints. Even the most advanced bipedal robots — including those from Boston Dynamics in the United States — moved with visible deliberation, their gait visibly computed rather than fluid. Boston Dynamics' Atlas robot, arguably the world's most agile humanoid platform at the time, had demonstrated impressive gymnastic routines in controlled environments, but these were the product of years of engineering, extensive environmental preparation, and movements that, while spectacular, bore no resemblance to the continuous, partner-dependent, musically synchronised movement of ballroom dance.

### The Specific Impossibility of Robotic Ballroom Dancing

Ballroom dancing — particularly the Viennese waltz — places demands on a physical system that were flatly beyond the capabilities of any robot in 2020, and remain extraordinarily challenging even years later. Consider the requirements: continuous, high-speed rotation while maintaining a fixed upper-body frame; real-time adaptation to a partner's weight shifts, momentum, and micro-adjustments; musical synchronisation at a tempo of approximately 180 beats per minute; the controlled deceleration and acceleration required for rises and falls; the management of centrifugal force during turns; and — perhaps most importantly — the aesthetic dimension of softness, breath, and emotional expression that distinguishes dancing from mere locomotion.

Robotics researchers had long identified partner dance as a frontier challenge. Kosuge and Hirata (2004, *Proceedings of the IEEE/RSJ International Conference on Intelligent Robots and Systems*) described a system for a robot to serve as a dance partner, but the resulting motion was a simplified lead-and-follow exercise at slow tempo with a rigidly programmed routine, far removed from the expressive, high-speed performance in the viral clip. The problem of real-time haptic communication between dance partners — the continuous, subtle exchange of force and intention through physical contact — remained an open research question.

### The Viral Chinese Robot Videos That Were Real

It is worth distinguishing the false claim from the genuine robotic achievements that gave it plausibility. In the years leading up to 2020, several Chinese robotics demonstrations had gone legitimately viral. UBTECH's large-scale synchronised robot dance performances — involving dozens of small bipedal robots moving in choreographed unison — were widely shared and genuinely impressive as feats of coordination and programming. WF Robot Technology developed service robots with realistic silicone faces capable of a range of expressions, and videos of these robots interacting with humans attracted significant attention.

These real achievements contributed to the ambient belief that Chinese robots could do things that closely mimicked human behaviour. The error lay not in overestimating these achievements per se, but in extrapolating from choreographed group routines by small robots or scripted facial expressions by stationary humanoids to the wholly different and vastly more demanding task of partnered ballroom dance by a full-sized humanoid robot.

## 5. Strictly Come Dancing and the Record

*Strictly Come Dancing* occupies a unique position in British cultural life. Launched on BBC One in 2004 as a revival of the earlier *Come Dancing* series (which ran from 1949 to 1998), the show has become one of the most watched programmes on British television, regularly attracting audiences of over ten million viewers. Its format — pairing celebrities with professional dancers for weekly live performances judged by a panel — has been exported globally as the *Dancing with the Stars* franchise, making it one of the most successful television formats in broadcasting history.

The show's production values are high, with professional lighting, costuming, and camera work designed to present the dancing in the most visually compelling manner possible. This production quality, paradoxically, contributed to the misidentification: when the video was extracted from its BBC context, the polished, well-lit footage could appear to have been produced in a high-tech demo environment rather than a television studio.

Abbey Clancy's journey on the show was itself a notable story. A model by profession, Clancy had no prior formal dance training but demonstrated exceptional aptitude throughout the 2013 series. Paired with Aljaž Škorjanec — who was in his debut year on the show but brought a lifetime of competitive dance experience from Slovenia — Clancy progressed through each round and ultimately won the series. Their Viennese waltz, performed to the Bee Gees' "If I Can't Have You," was singled out by judges and viewers alike as one of the finest performances of the series.

Škorjanec's technique is relevant to the misidentification. Trained in the Central European tradition of competitive ballroom dancing, which emphasises strong frame, continuous movement, and a smooth, almost floating quality of locomotion, Škorjanec's lead produced in Clancy a quality of movement that was strikingly uniform and controlled. For a viewer unfamiliar with elite ballroom dance, the result could plausibly appear too smooth, too controlled, too perfect to be human — and thus, in the logic of the viral claim, robotic.

The BBC's official YouTube upload of the performance remained publicly available throughout the period of the viral claim. A reverse image or video search, or even a simple text search for "ballroom dance Strictly Come Dancing 2013," would have immediately revealed the true origin of the footage. That such searches were not performed by the millions who viewed and shared the misattributed clip speaks to the broader dynamics of low-engagement information consumption on social media, a pattern extensively documented by Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral (2018, *Science*), who found that false news on Twitter spreads significantly faster, farther, and more broadly than true news — not because of bots, but because of human sharing behaviour driven by novelty and emotional arousal.

## 6. Verdict and Significance

**Verdict: FALSE.** The viral video does not depict robots dancing at Shanghai Disneyland. It depicts Abbey Clancy and Aljaž Škorjanec, two human beings, performing a Viennese waltz on *Strictly Come Dancing* in 2013. The claim is fabricated, and the footage was stripped of its original context and repackaged with a false narrative.

### The Pattern of Shanghai Disneyland Robot Rumors

This was not the first time Shanghai Disneyland had been invoked as the setting for a supposed robotic performance. In 2019, a strikingly similar claim circulated on social media, alleging that a video of a graceful female dancer was in fact a robot performing at Shanghai Disneyland. The dancer in question was Qin Xi, a Chinese classical dancer whose performance in flowing traditional costume was so technically refined that viewers, primed by the same constellation of factors discussed above — the inverse uncanny valley, geopolitical narrative, and low-engagement consumption — accepted the robot framing without investigation.

The recurrence of the same narrative structure — beautiful performance, claim of Chinese robot origin, Shanghai Disneyland as location — suggests that this is not a series of independent errors but a *template*, a memetic structure that can be applied to virtually any video of impressive physical performance. The template has several components: the performance must be visually striking enough to arrest scrolling attention; the robot claim provides the novel, emotionally arousing explanation that drives sharing; China provides the plausible technological origin; and Shanghai Disneyland provides a specific, prestigious, real-world venue that lends the claim an air of concrete factuality.

### Why Shanghai Disneyland?

The choice of Shanghai Disneyland as the purported venue is not arbitrary. The park, which opened in 2016, is Disney's sixth global resort and represents a significant intersection of American entertainment technology and Chinese scale. It is a site already associated in the popular imagination with animatronics and technological spectacle — Disney's audio-animatronic figures have been a core element of its theme parks since the 1964 World's Fair. The invocation of Shanghai Disneyland leverages this association, implicitly suggesting that the robots are a next-generation evolution of Disney's animatronic tradition, now powered by Chinese engineering. This framing has a narrative elegance that makes it particularly sticky, even though — or perhaps because — it has no basis in fact.

### The Broader Significance

The Shanghai Disneyland robot dancing claim, and the earlier Qin Xi variant, belong to a broader category of misinformation that might be termed *aspirational misattribution*: false claims that succeed not because they describe something frightening or outrageous, but because they describe something that people *want* to believe is possible. The viewers who shared the dancing robots video were not, for the most part, spreading the claim maliciously. Many were sharing it with genuine amazement, wonder, and even admiration for what they believed Chinese technology had achieved. The misinformation succeeded because it offered an emotionally satisfying narrative — humanity's technological future, embodied in graceful, dancing machines — that was more compelling than the mundane truth of a television dance competition.

This aspirational quality makes such claims particularly resistant to correction. Lewandowsky, Ecker, Seifert, Schwarz, and Cook (2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) have documented the "continued influence effect," whereby misinformation continues to shape people's judgments even after it has been explicitly corrected. A viewer who has experienced the wonder of watching "robots dance" may retain that emotional imprint even after learning that the dancers were human. The correction removes the factual error but cannot fully undo the affective experience.

For fact-checkers, journalists, and digital literacy educators, the Shanghai Disneyland robot claims offer a useful case study precisely because they are not politically toxic or ideologically charged in the way that much misinformation is. They are, in a sense, misinformation in its purest form: false claims that spread because they are wonderful, because they compress the distance between what is and what might be, because they satisfy a deep human appetite for marvels. Understanding why they spread — and why they recur — is essential to understanding the information environment in which we all now live.
