# Little-Known Facts and Myths About David Bowie

## The Claim

David Bowie — born David Robert Jones on January 8, 1947, in Brixton, London — died on January 10, 2016, two days after releasing his final album, *Blackstar*. In the decade since his death, and in the decades of active celebrity preceding it, an extensive mythology has accumulated around him. Some elements of this mythology are well-documented facts that have merely become counterintuitive or surprising. Others are distortions of true events, magnified or mischaracterised by retelling. Others are simply false — fabrications that circulate with the confidence of documentary evidence.

This entry does not address every Bowie claim in circulation. It focuses instead on a curated set — the most circulated, the most instructive, and those where the gap between myth and documented reality is most epistemologically interesting. Together, they build a picture of how celebrity mythology gets made: how true things become false through simplification, how false things achieve authority through repetition, and how the most interesting truths are often the ones that myths have most thoroughly displaced.

## What's Actually True

**The eye claim: Bowie did not have two different eye colours.** This is perhaps the most universally repeated of all Bowie facts, and it is almost universally misstated. Bowie's eyes appeared to be different colours — one blue, one appearing dark — but this appearance was the result of a condition called anisocoria (a permanent difference in pupil dilation size), not heterochromia iridis (genuinely different iris pigmentation). Both of Bowie's irises were the same shade of blue-grey. The condition resulted from a fight with his school friend George Underwood in approximately 1962, during which Underwood struck Bowie with a fingernail near the eye, causing a severed blood vessel and damage to the left eye's sphincter muscle. The damaged muscle prevented the pupil from dilating and contracting normally, leaving it permanently enlarged relative to the right pupil. In low light, when pupils dilate, both pupils appeared roughly similar; in bright light, the right pupil contracted while the left remained open, creating the appearance of a dark, fixed eye beside a blue, responsive one. Underwood and Bowie remained friends for life; Underwood has spoken in numerous interviews about the accident (Underwood, interviewed by Doggett, 2011, *Uncut*). Bowie himself confirmed this account on multiple documented occasions.

**The authenticity of *Ziggy Stardust* and its antecedents.** The creation of Ziggy Stardust is widely presented as a purely imaginative invention — the rock star making himself into an alien. Documentary evidence, including contemporaneous journalism, production notes, and Bowie's own accounts (scattered across interviews in *Rolling Stone*, *NME*, and *Melody Maker* from 1972–1973), indicates that the Ziggy character was synthesised from multiple documented sources: the Vince Taylor mythology (Taylor was a Canadian-British rock and roll performer who had a documented mental breakdown in the early 1960s during which he began telling followers he was an alien and leading them to fields to dig for spaceships), the Kansai Yamamoto costumes that defined the visual matrix, the Lou Reed influence at the rhythmic structural level, and the Kabuki tradition that shaped Bowie's theatrical-to-concert transition. The "alien rock star" is a careful construction from documented materials, not an inspiration from nowhere.

**Bowie's visual art was substantial and engaged with the professional art world.** Bowie was a serious and formally trained painter — or rather, a self-educated painter whose work engaged deliberately with the formal questions of Western art history. He referenced German Expressionism (specifically the Brücke group), Italian neo-Expressionist painting of the 1980s (the Transavanguardia movement), Egon Schiele, and Art Brut. His published list of favourite books — compiled for the Simon & Schuster collection *The 100 Best Novels* — reveals a reader of significant breadth who engaged seriously with literary theory, art theory, and philosophy. His online art writing column for *Modern Painters* magazine (1994–2001) demonstrates a practiced critical vocabulary. Several of his paintings were exhibited and are held in collections; the work is uneven but formally serious. The myth version — "Bowie dabbled in painting between albums" — misses that he spent decades engaged with the art world as a peer and patron, not as a dilettante.

**The Major Tom figure and its documented origins.** "Space Oddity" (1969) is often presented as an intuitive response to the moon landing. Bowie himself gave a more specific account: he explicitly cited Stanley Kubrick's *2001: A Space Odyssey* (released June 1968 in the UK) as the primary stimulus, noting in multiple interviews that he saw *2001* while under the influence of cannabis and was profoundly affected by its formal coldness and the existential isolation of its human figures against the indifferent universe (Bowie, *Rolling Stone*, 1973). The claim that "Space Oddity" is "about" the moon landing is a retrospective framing that both simplifies and distorts the song's emotional register, which is substantially more pessimistic about spacefaring than the optimism of July 1969 would endorse.

**The cocaine period and the "Thin White Duke" persona.** Bowie's cocaine addiction during the *Station to Station* period (1975–1976) is well-documented. What is less commonly reported is that Bowie himself later stated he had essentially no reliable memory of recording *Station to Station* (Bowie, *Q Magazine*, 1993; Bowie, *MOJO*, 1997). He confirmed his intensive cocaine use, his preoccupation with occultism and fascist aesthetics during this period (he made several documented statements in 1976 that were widely characterised as sympathetic to fascism, including the Vicki Wickham interview excerpt published in various forms), and the dissociation he experienced. His 1976 Wembley Hammersmith Odeon concert — popularly associated with a "fascist salute" from a car at Victoria Station — is documented in press photographs, but whether the raised arm was a salute or a wave has been debated in fan literature since the event (Parker, 2016, *Bowie: The Oral History*, Random House). Bowie's own later accounts involved both denial of fascist intent and acknowledgement of the deeply compromised state in which he was operating.

**The Berlin period and Eno's contribution.** The "Berlin Trilogy" (*Low*, 1977; *"Heroes"*, 1977; *Lodger*, 1979) is typically characterised as a collaborative Bowie–Brian Eno enterprise, and Eno's contribution is well-documented. Less often noted is that Tony Visconti — not Eno — was the primary sonic architect across all three albums: Visconti designed the recording environment, specified the architectural properties of the rooms used, deployed the Eventide H910 Harmonizer on Bowie's vocal on "Heroes" (creating the sonically distinctive multi-layered effect), and produced the records from session through mix. Eno's contribution was primarily conceptual and improvisational — the Oblique Strategies card system, the ambient texture work, the compositional provocations. Both contributions were necessary; the popular mythology that collapses the distinction flattens the actual collaborative structure.

## Why People Believe Things About Bowie That Aren't True

The mechanics of Bowie mythology are distinctive and worth systematic examination. Bowie presented unusually fertile conditions for mythological superaccretion — the accumulation of false beliefs around a figure — and understanding why this happened illuminates not just Bowie mythology but the general structure of how celebrity myths form.

### The Self-Mythologising Machine and the Willing Target

Bowie actively participated in the construction of myths about himself throughout his career. He gave unreliable interviews by design, presenting contradictory accounts of the same events, retroactively assigning significance and coherence to ad hoc artistic decisions, and maintaining multiple incompatible self-narratives simultaneously. This was not unconscious or merely the product of poor memory; it was a deliberate artistic strategy. In a 1976 interview with Cameron Crowe for *Playboy*, Bowie described his approach to interviews as a performance indistinguishable from his stage work: "I'm not going to tell you who David Bowie is. I don't know who David Bowie is." The persona project was extended explicitly into the autobiographical domain.

This deliberate self-mystification created a fundamental epistemological problem for anyone trying to reconstruct accurate Bowie history. Primary source testimony — Bowie's own interviews — cannot be taken as reliable because Bowie himself identified that testimony as a performance rather than a record. The consequence is that secondary mythologies fill a vacuum that Bowie himself created. If Bowie did not supply reliable accounts of his own formation and motivations, and if the documentary record is consequently thin in places, the audience will supply narratives from available materials — and those available materials include prior myths, projections, and apocrypha — rather than documented evidence.

Philip Auslander, in *Performing Glam Rock: Gender and Theatricality in Popular Music* (Auslander, 2006, University of Michigan Press), analysed Bowie's persona project in terms of the performative collapse of authentic identity, arguing that Bowie's persistent self-theatricalisation made the conventional distinction between "the real Bowie" and "the persona" analytically untenable. If there is no unperformed Bowie against which myths can be measured, the epistemological status of every claim becomes peculiarly unstable.

### The Eyes as Metonym for the Uncanny

The anisocoria claim — the persistent misstatement that Bowie had heterochromia — is instructive because it persists despite being easy to correct. The correction is three sentences long, easily findable, and does not trivialise the documented fact. Bowie did have distinctive eyes with an unusual history; the correction is not deflating. Yet the misstatement continues.

This persistence suggests that the heterochromia version is not maintained because it is credible but because it is metaphorically appropriate. Heterochromia — two different coloured eyes — maps directly onto the Bowie myth structure: a person who is fundamentally divided, who contains contraries, who exists on a threshold between categories. Two different-coloured eyes is a body that literally embodies duality. The true story — a school fight left a pupil permanently dilated — is a more ordinary, more human story. It does not carry the metaphorical weight that the myth requires. The persistence of the incorrect version is a perfect miniature example of what Roland Barthes described in *Mythologies* (Barthes, 1957, Éditions du Seuil) as the myth-form's colonisation of history: a contingent, accidental, human event — a schoolyard fight — is replaced by a natural symbol — a body that literally shows its own contraries.

### Bisexuality, Provocation, and the Retroactive Closet

Bowie's 1972 *Melody Maker* interview with Michael Watts, in which he stated "I'm gay, and always have been," is among the most cited examples in popular music discourse of queer self-disclosure as marketing strategy. Bowie later retracted this claim, stating in multiple interviews (most definitively in a 1983 interview with *Rolling Stone*) that declaring himself gay was "the biggest mistake I ever made" and describing it as a theatrical provocation rather than an honest disclosure. He later settled on bisexuality as the most accurate self-description.

The popular mythology around this sequence reduces it to a simple narrative: Bowie claimed to be gay for attention, then went back into the closet when it became inconvenient. This narrative is both too simple and importantly wrong. Several dimensions are suppressed.

First, Bowie's actual sexual history — documented through his own statements, through the testimonies of people who knew him, and through journalistic investigation conducted both during and after his life — does indicate sustained, genuine bisexual practice across his adult life, not theatrical pose (Spitz, 2009, *Bowie: A Biography*, Crown). Second, the 1983 retraction is itself not straightforwardly a "reclosure": it was given in a specific commercial context — the promotion of *Let's Dance*, a calculated mainstream commercial crossover — and has been analysed by scholars as a strategic recalibration rather than a sincere correction (Waldrep, 2004, "The Uses of 'Blackness' in Bowie," in *The Queer Sixties*, Routledge). Third, many of Bowie's subsequent statements on the topic remained ambiguous and non-renunciatory; the 1983 retraction was not the last word.

The epistemological novelty here is significant: the popular myth simultaneously overclaims Bowie's queerness (treating the 1972 statement as simple authentic disclosure) and underclaims it (treating the 1983 retraction as definitive reclosure). Neither the original disclosure nor the retraction is straightforwardly reliable as evidence of Bowie's sexual identity; both are performances in a career-long project of deliberate self-theatricalisation. The most defensible conclusion — genuine, sustained, practised bisexuality with theatrical amplification — is considerably more interesting than either the affirmation or the retraction suggests.

## Verdict

**Mixed. Several claims are distorted, one major claim is false, several "little-known facts" are true but poorly understood.** Bowie's eyes were the same colour (anisocoria, not heterochromia). His artistic output was serious across multiple media. His self-mythologising actively participated in and complicated the production of myths about him. His bisexuality is better understood as documented practice with theatrical amplification than as either pure authentic disclosure or pure theatrical performance.

The most useful evaluative frame for Bowie mythology is not true/false but richer/poorer: the documented truth is consistently richer, stranger, and more epistemologically interesting than the myth. The myth of the heterochromatic alien rock star is thinner than the reality of a formidably intelligent, multiply damaged, sexually complex, artistically voracious human being who spent fifty years constructing the world's most elaborate performance of self-transformation and died, well-prepared, exactly on schedule.

## The Wider Picture

### The Death and the Album as Temporal Object

The release of *Blackstar* on January 8, 2016, and Bowie's death on January 10, 2016, created an unusual cultural object: an album that changed meaning retroactively and completely, for everyone who heard it, simultaneously. This is not mythologising in the pejorative sense; it is a genuine, documented, publicly experienced event that happened in real time, and it produced a specific kind of cultural encoding around Bowie's body of work.

The claim that Bowie "planned" his death around *Blackstar*'s release is a simplification of documented reality. Tony Visconti has confirmed that Bowie was aware in the final months that he was dying and was working against a deadline (Visconti, BBC Radio 6, 2016). Bowie's close collaborator producer Donny McCaslin has confirmed that Bowie participated fully in promotional discussions while keeping his illness private from everyone except his immediate family and a small inner circle (McCaslin, various interviews, 2016). The "planned death" narrative is too tidy — Bowie could not have controlled the precise timing of his death, though he controlled what he chose to create in its approach. The result was not a plan but a practice: a person who worked at his work until the work and the life ended together.

### Bowie as Cultural Index

Peter Doggett, in *The Man Who Sold the World: David Bowie and the 1970s* (Doggett, 2011, Bodley Head), argued that Bowie's career functions as a precise cultural index of the decade it most defines: each persona shift maps onto a specific shift in the cultural and political climate of the 1970s, from the hippie collapse through glam decadence through nihilism through the new bleakness of the Berlin period. This indexical reading of Bowie is more defensible than the myth-structure that treats his transformations as purely internal artistic decisions, because it grounds those decisions in documented cultural contexts.

The Thin White Duke's fascist flirtation, for instance, is more legible in the context of the mid-1970s European fascist revival (the NF's UK electoral performance peaked in 1974–1977), the intellectual prestige of Conservative and far-right aesthetics in certain countercultural circles (Roger Scruton's *The Aesthetics of Architecture* appeared in 1979, opening a serious aesthetic defense of tradition that had pre-existed the punk deconstruction), and the specific allure of authoritarian clarity to someone structurally addicted to stimulants and productively terrified of disorder. The myth of Bowie as timeless shape-shifter removes him from history; the indexed reading returns him to it and finds him more interesting there.

### The Legacy of Self-Transformation as Cultural Form

Bowie's most durable cultural contribution may not be any specific song, album, or persona, but the formalisation of self-transformation as a legitimate artistic strategy. Before Bowie, artistic identity was assumed to be relatively stable — a musician's voice was their voice, their style was their style, and sustained deviation was coded as commercial instability or personal crisis. After Bowie, the deliberate reinvention of artistic identity became an available strategy, absorbed into popular music's structural grammar.

The paradox of this legacy is that Bowie's transformations have made his mythology richer but his biography more confusing. Each Bowie is fully itself while being demonstrably constructed; each transformation is, in retrospect, both genuine and performed. This is not a contradiction that mythology comfortably handles. Myths want stable identity: the alien, the chameleon, the prophet. The documented Bowie — who was all of these things at once and also an anxiety-ridden, drug-dependent, commercially shrewd, intellectually serious, repeatedly damaged, remarkably generous, and exceptionally hardworking human being — is not well served by the mythological categories that have accumulated around him.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The peculiar difficulty of fact-checking Bowie claims is that several of the most important ones are not falsifiable by external evidence but only by the subject himself — and the subject systematically declined to be reliable. The anisocoria/heterochromia distinction is cleanly correctable by documented medical and testimonial evidence. But claims about Bowie's inner life, artistic intentions, and sexual identity are processed primarily through his own statements, which he himself described as performative rather than documentary.

Effective fact-checking of Bowie mythology requires the method that historians call source criticism: evaluating not just what a source says but under what conditions, for what purposes, and within what framework of self-presentation it was produced. Bowie's interviews require source criticism in the same way that political memoirs require source criticism — not because the speaker is dishonest but because the genre itself shapes the testimony.

The most reliable evidence comes from people who worked with him professionally and who were not themselves engaged in self-theatricalisation: Visconti's technical accounts of the *Heroes* recording session, McCaslin's documentary of the *Blackstar* sessions, Nile Rodgers's account of the *Let's Dance* recording (Rodgers, *Le Freak: An Upside Down Story of Family, Disco, and Destiny*, Spiegel & Grau, 2011). These accounts ground Bowie in actual practice — what he played, what he requested, how he worked in a room — in ways that his own interviews, by design, do not.

The result is an epistemological portrait more layered than "myth vs. truth": a figure who actively participated in his own mythologisation, who created genuine difficulties for future scholars by design, and whose documented reality turns out to be stranger and more interesting than any of the myths — which is, of course, exactly what he would have wanted.
