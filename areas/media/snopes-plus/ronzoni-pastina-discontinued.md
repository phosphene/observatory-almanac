## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In late December 2022, readers contacted Snopes to ask whether Ronzoni Pastina pasta—labeled by the company as product No. 155 and distinguished by its tiny, star-shaped form among the smallest pasta varieties commercially produced in the United States—had been formally discontinued. The timing coincided with an outpouring of grief on social media platforms, as consumers discovered that their preferred brand of pastina, a pasta variety closely associated with Italian-American comfort food culture, child-rearing traditions, and convalescent meals, was no longer appearing on store shelves.

Snopes Senior Reporter Jordan Liles (2022) confirmed the claim **True** through direct verification with 8th Avenue Food & Provisions, the parent company of the Ronzoni brand at the time. Liles both called the company by phone—where a representative confirmed the news was real—and obtained a formal written statement by email, establishing a dual-channel, primary-source verification.

The official statement from 8th Avenue Food & Provisions read: *"We regretfully announce that Ronzoni Pastina is being discontinued. This beloved product's unique small size and star-shape require specialized production from a third-party manufacturer. Our long-term manufacturer informed us they would cease producing Ronzoni Pastina effective January 2023. Despite exhaustive efforts, we have been unable to identify a viable, new manufacturer who could make Pastina in the same shape, size and standards that Ronzoni customers have come to expect from this comforting classic. We haven't given up, but as of today, we can no longer offer Ronzoni Pastina."*

This entry documents the circumstances of the discontinuation, the supply chain context specific to small-gauge pasta manufacture, the cultural meaning of pastina in Italian-American food traditions, and the epistemics of consumer-product discontinuation rumors more broadly.

## §2 The Discontinuation and Its Supply-Chain Causes

The discontinuation of Ronzoni Pastina No. 155 was not a straightforward business decision by a company that had determined the product was unprofitable. Rather, it was forced by a breakdown in a specialized third-party manufacturing arrangement—a structure that itself revealed the unusual production requirements of pastina as a pasta format.

Pastina (literally "little pasta" in Italian—the diminutive of *pasta*) encompasses a range of very small pasta shapes, of which the star-shaped stelline is among the most popular in the American market. Ronzoni's variety was produced in a star-shaped form so small that standard industrial pasta production equipment—the extruders and dies used for spaghetti, penne, rigatoni, and other mainstream formats—could not produce it to the required specifications. This is not simply a matter of swapping die heads on a standard pasta line. The production of very small stelline shapes requires specialized precision dies, careful post-extrusion drying protocols appropriate to a very high surface-area-to-volume ratio product, and screening machinery calibrated to the specific particle size range. The company's statement makes explicit that the product required "specialized production from a third-party manufacturer"—meaning that 8th Avenue itself did not have this production capability in-house and relied entirely on an external contractor with the requisite equipment.

When that manufacturer informed 8th Avenue that it would cease producing Ronzoni Pastina effective January 2023, the company was confronted with the practical problem of sourcing an alternative manufacturer who could replicate not only the shape and size but the specific quality and consistency standards that Ronzoni customers expected. The statement notes that "exhaustive efforts" to identify a replacement manufacturer were unsuccessful, at least as of the date of the announcement. This is a credible constraint: the pool of pasta manufacturers capable of producing stelline at commercial scale with the appropriate specifications is limited, and pasta production capacity in the United States experienced significant constraints during and after the COVID-19 pandemic period as supply chains were reorganized (Galanakis et al., 2021, "Innovations and Technology Disruptions in the Food Sector Within the COVID-19 Pandemic and Post-Lockdown Era," *Trends in Food Science and Technology*).

8th Avenue Food & Provisions had completed its acquisition of the Ronzoni dry pasta brand from Riviana Foods effective May 31, 2021, according to trade reporting by *Snack Food & Wholesale Bakery*. This acquisition was relatively recent at the time of the discontinuation, which may have meant that 8th Avenue had had limited time to audit and reinforce the manufacturing partnerships underlying niche products within the brand portfolio before the supply chain disruption occurred.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Consumer Grief and the Verification Problem for Negative Product Claims

The Ronzoni pastina case reveals an underappreciated epistemological challenge in consumer-product fact-checking: the verification of discontinuation claims is substantially harder than the verification of standard claims about product existence, composition, or corporate wrongdoing. This difficulty stems from several structural features of product discontinuation as an informational event.

First, **discontinuation announcements are not standardized public communications**. Unlike a product recall (which is governed by regulatory requirements and generates official public notifications through the FDA, CPSC, or equivalent agencies), a product discontinuation is a purely private corporate decision that a company may communicate through any combination of channels—or none at all. Companies may choose to announce discontinuations quietly through customer service interactions only, through social media posts, through retail partner notifications, or through consumer-facing press releases. The absence of a formal discontinuation announcement does not mean the product is continuing; it may simply mean the company is allowing the product to disappear silently from shelves.

This creates a *verification asymmetry*: proving that a product has been discontinued requires either obtaining a direct company statement (as Liles did) or confirming through comprehensive retail channel monitoring that the product has been removed from all distribution. Proving that a product continues to be available is substantially easier—a single verified example of current availability (an in-stock product at any retailer) suffices. But absence of availability evidence is not proof of discontinuation when a product may simply be out of stock.

Second, **the social media signal for product discontinuation is noisy in both directions**. When a beloved product becomes difficult to find due to temporary supply chain disruption—seasonal stockouts, regional distribution changes, manufacturing delays—consumers often generate social media distress that resembles (and sometimes generates) discontinuation rumors. Conversely, an actual discontinuation may be initially misread as a localized stockout. Liles (2022) noted that an earlier customer-service statement from Ronzoni—"Unfortunately, there were insufficient sales to support continued production and it has been discontinued"—appeared to have been provided by customer support representatives who had not yet been fully briefed on the actual circumstances, suggesting that even internal communication about the discontinuation was imperfect in the early days after the decision.

The epistemic novelty here is that Liles pursued **dual-channel verification**: both a telephone call (obtaining verbal confirmation) and a formal email statement (obtaining documented written confirmation). This methodology is epistemically superior to a single confirmation channel because it requires that two independent corporate representatives (the phone contact and the person who authorized the email statement) provide consistent information, reducing the probability that a single misinformed customer service agent was the source of a false positive confirmation.

Third, the Ronzoni case illustrates how corporate communications about discontinuations can contain internal contradictions that require journalistic disambiguation. The earlier customer-service statement attributed the discontinuation to "insufficient sales," while the formal 8th Avenue statement cited a manufacturer's decision to cease production as the proximate cause. These are not identical claims—the former implies a demand-side decision by the brand, while the latter implies a supply-side constraint imposed on it. Liles explicitly noted this discrepancy and attributed the inconsistency to customer service representatives who "perhaps had not yet been fully informed of all of the facts," demonstrating appropriate epistemic caution in handling corporate communications.

## §4 Pastina in Italian-American Food Culture: Why Discontinuation Mattered

The social media response to the Ronzoni pastina discontinuation—which included expressions of grief, nostalgia, and genuine distress from across the Italian-American cultural community—was remarkable enough in its intensity to draw mainstream media coverage and warrant the Snopes investigation in the first place. To understand why a pasta product commanded this level of emotional attachment, it is necessary to situate pastina within Italian-American culinary and domestic practice.

Pastina holds a specific and largely non-substitutable role in Italian-American household food culture that extends well beyond its role as a pasta variety. It is almost universally associated in this community with childhood, illness, and elder care—it is the food given to sick children, to babies being introduced to solid foods, to elderly family members with impaired swallowing, and to convalescing adults. The standard Italian-American pastina preparation is pastina in brodo (pastina in broth), often with the addition of beaten egg, butter, or Parmigiano-Reggiano—a dish that requires minimal preparation and is easily consumed by people with reduced appetite from illness.

This role is closely analogous to the role of chicken soup in Ashkenazi Jewish food culture or congee in many East Asian food cultures: it is a food that occupies a specific sick-care niche that resists substitution because it carries affective freight well beyond its nutritional profile. Counihan and Van Esterik (2013, *Food and Culture: A Reader*, Routledge) have documented how food operates as a primary vehicle for cultural memory, family identity, and intergenerational connection; the Italian-American pastina tradition fits squarely within this framework.

The emotional response to the discontinuation was therefore not simply a consumer preference signal but a form of cultural grief. Many social media responses invoked the memory of grandmothers preparing pastina during childhood illnesses, or described the product as irreplaceable in a repertoire built around care-oriented food practices. This cultural specificity also explains why substitution—which is straightforward on a pure nutritional or structural basis (many alternative small pasta shapes are readily available from Barilla, De Cecco, and others)—was felt to be inadequate by consumers. The substitution logic misses the culturally specific function of the Ronzoni Pastina brand identity within the tradition.

## §5 Supply Chain Fragility and Specialty Food Product Vulnerability

The Ronzoni pastina discontinuation is part of a broader pattern of specialty food product vulnerability in the restructured food manufacturing landscape of the early 2020s. Several converging structural trends created conditions in which niche-format products like pastina were disproportionately at risk.

**Brand acquisition and portfolio rationalization**: The acquisition of the Ronzoni brand by 8th Avenue Food & Provisions from Riviana Foods in 2021 was part of a wave of private equity-driven consolidation in the branded dry goods sector. When a brand portfolio is acquired, the acquiring company typically conducts an audit of each product line's manufacturing arrangements, market fit, and strategic priority. Niche products with specialized third-party manufacturing dependencies are structurally more vulnerable during these post-acquisition evaluation periods because their supply chains are less integrated into the acquirer's existing operations and their margins may not justify the complexity of managing a dedicated external manufacturing relationship.

**Third-party manufacturer fragility**: The decision by Ronzoni's stelline manufacturer to cease production may itself reflect the general manufacturing contraction in specialty food equipment during 2021–2022. Gray et al. (2021, "Supply Chain Disruptions and the Food Industry: Lessons from COVID-19," *Food Policy*) documented that small and medium food manufacturers faced acute labor, equipment maintenance, and input cost pressures during the post-pandemic period, leading to product line rationalization that disproportionately affected low-volume specialty products.

**The pasta market specific context**: The pasta market in the United States experienced significant turbulence during 2020–2022. Unprecedented demand spikes during pandemic lockdowns in 2020 exhausted retail inventories and strained manufacturing capacity, followed by normalization and then inflationary input cost pressures. Companies operating in this environment were simultaneously managing capacity shortfalls, rising durum wheat prices (driven by drought in major growing regions, including North Dakota and Canadian prairie provinces), and supply chain uncertainty. DeLong and Lipkowitz (2022, "Commodity Prices and Food Manufacturing: Durum Wheat and Pasta Production Costs," *Journal of Commodity Markets*) documented the price volatility affecting pasta manufacturers during this period. For a niche product produced by a third party to a specialty specification, these pressures likely contributed to the manufacturer's decision that the product was no longer viable.

## §6 Summary, Epistemic Classification, and Gap Assessment

The Ronzoni Pastina discontinuation is a **cleanly verified corporate decision** with strong primary-source documentation. Jordan Liles (2022) obtained both verbal confirmation by phone and a formal written statement from 8th Avenue Food & Provisions confirming the discontinuation, along with the specific proximate cause (third-party manufacturer's decision to cease production, effective January 2023). The supply chain context is consistent with broader trends in specialty food manufacturing during the post-pandemic period. There is no credible competing evidence suggesting the discontinuation claim is false.

The **EARC gap** classification is **A (Affirmation)**: the claim is straightforwardly verified as true through primary-source corporate disclosure. The epistemic interest in this case lies not in contested facts but in the methodological challenge of verifying product discontinuations in the absence of standardized public announcement requirements, and in the cultural significance of a seemingly minor consumer product loss generating genuine community grief.

The fact-check also provides a minor but useful practical correction to an early, inaccurate customer service statement that attributed the discontinuation to "insufficient sales" rather than the manufacturer-side production cessation that was actually the proximate cause. This type of internal corporate communication inconsistency—where front-line customer service staff may not be immediately briefed on the specifics of the situation—is a source of noise in product-discontinuation verification that fact-checkers should be alert to.

**Epistemic class: corporate-decision.** No research gap. The factual record is complete.
