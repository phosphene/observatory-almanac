## 1. The Claim

The origin story of Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer is one of America's most cherished pieces of Christmas lore — a narrative about a red-nosed reindeer that doubles as a narrative about its own creation. The version that circulates widely online, and that Snopes reproduced in its article, tells the story of Bob May, a depressed and brokenhearted copywriter living in a drafty Chicago apartment with his four-year-old daughter Barbara as his wife Evelyn lay dying of cancer. Unable to afford a Christmas gift, May created a storybook about a misfit reindeer named Rudolph to bring comfort to his grieving daughter. The story continues: the Montgomery Ward department store chain learned of the booklet, bought the rights, distributed millions of copies, and eventually — in an act of extraordinary generosity — returned the copyright to May, making him wealthy. May's brother-in-law Johnny Marks adapted the story into a song, which Gene Autry recorded after Bing Crosby and Dinah Shore turned it down, and "Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer" became the second-best-selling Christmas song of all time.

This narrative, which appears on countless websites, in forwarded emails, and in inspirational publications, is deeply satisfying. It appeals to our love of underdog stories, our belief in corporate redemption, our faith that creativity born of suffering will be rewarded. It has all the emotional beats of a holiday movie: tragedy, perseverance, recognition, generosity, and triumph.

Snopes, in a December 2013 article by David Mikkelson, addressed this claim with a "Mixture" rating. The article identified what was true — that Robert L. May wrote Rudolph, that his wife was dying of cancer, that he tested the story on his daughter, that Montgomery Ward distributed millions of copies, that the copyright was eventually returned, and that the song became a massive hit — and what was false: the chronology and motivation were inverted. Rudolph was not created spontaneously to comfort a dying mother's child. It was commissioned by Montgomery Ward as a promotional booklet. May was assigned the task by his employer, and he tested the story on his daughter during its development, not after independently creating it as a gift.

The Snopes correction is factually important. The popular narrative reverses the order of causation in a way that transforms a commercial assignment into a heroic act of parental love. But the "Mixture" rating, suggesting a roughly equal balance of truth and falsehood, may understate the degree to which the popular narrative gets the essential emotional reality correct. May was dealing with his wife's terminal illness while creating Rudolph. He did test the story on his young daughter. He did struggle financially. The copyright return did transform his circumstances. The popular myth compresses and dramatizes these events, but it does not fabricate them from nothing.

## 2. What's Actually True

The documented history of Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer is, in many ways, more interesting than the sentimentalized version. It is a story about the intersection of commerce, creativity, and personal hardship — a story that reflects the realities of mid-century American corporate culture rather than the fantasies of inspirational internet lore.

Robert L. May was a 34-year-old copywriter at the Chicago-based Montgomery Ward department store chain in 1939. Montgomery Ward had a tradition of buying and distributing coloring books to children who visited Santa Claus in their stores each Christmas season. May's department head, seeing an opportunity to save money, decided that the company should create its own promotional giveaway booklet rather than purchasing pre-printed coloring books from external vendors. May, who had a demonstrated talent for writing children's stories and limericks, was assigned to create the booklet.

May drew on multiple influences. The Ugly Duckling by Hans Christian Andersen provided a template for a story about an outcast who proves his worth. May's own childhood — he was frequently teased for being shy, small, and slight — gave the story emotional authenticity. And the specific conceit of a glowing reindeer nose came from May's own imagination, though it gave his superiors pause: they worried that a red nose would be associated with drinking and drunkards, making the story unsuitable for a Christmas promotion.

May wrote Rudolph's story in verse as a series of rhyming couplets. As he wrote, he tested the story on his four-year-old daughter Barbara. Barbara was thrilled, but May's bosses remained skeptical. To win their approval, May enlisted Denver Gillen, a friend from Montgomery Ward's art department, to sketch some deer at the Lincoln Park Zoo. Gillen's illustrations of a red-nosed reindeer softened the resistance, and the Rudolph story was approved for production.

Montgomery Ward distributed 2.4 million copies of the Rudolph booklet in 1939. Paper shortages during World War II curtailed further printing, but by the end of 1946, a total of six million copies had been distributed. The character was enormously popular.

The crucial fact about the copyright is this: because May created the story on a "work made for hire" basis as a Montgomery Ward employee, the company owned the copyright. May received no royalties. He was deeply in debt from the medical bills stemming from his wife Evelyn's terminal illness, which claimed her life around the time of Rudolph's creation. In January 1947, May persuaded Montgomery Ward's corporate president, Sewell Avery, to transfer the copyright to him. With the rights in hand, May's financial situation improved dramatically. The character was a protected trademark requiring licensing fees for commercial use.

Rudolph's story was reprinted commercially by Maxton Publishers in 1947. In 1949, May's brother-in-law, Johnny Marks, adapted the story into song lyrics and music. Marks had difficulty getting the song recorded; it was turned down by Bing Crosby and Dinah Shore before Gene Autry agreed to record it. "Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer" was released in 1949 and became a phenomenal success, eventually selling more records than any other Christmas song except "White Christmas."

So the bones of the popular story are accurate. May was grieving his wife's death. He tested the story on his daughter. Montgomery Ward distributed millions. The copyright was returned. The song was a massive hit. What the popular story gets wrong is the sequence and motivation: Rudolph was not a spontaneous gift created to comfort a dying mother's daughter. It was a corporate assignment created to save Montgomery Ward money on promotional giveaways.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

The Snopes article is one of the platform's stronger pieces of historical fact-checking. It provides a detailed, well-sourced account of Rudolph's creation, correctly identifying both the accurate and inaccurate elements of the popular narrative. The article establishes the commercial origin of Rudolph, documents May's personal circumstances, traces the development and distribution of the booklet, explains the copyright transfer, and provides the history of the song adaptation. The sourcing is appropriate and the tone is measured.

Where the article falls short is in its overall framing of the claim's significance. The "Mixture" rating and the article's emphasis on what is "False" (the inversion of motivation) create the impression that the popular narrative is substantially wrong. In fact, the popular narrative captures the emotional and biographical truth of Rudolph's creation with reasonable fidelity. May was a grieving husband. He was a devoted father. He created a story about an outcast that resonated with children. His creation made him wealthy after years of struggle. These are the essential elements that make the story meaningful, and they are all true.

The Snopes critique focuses on the order of events and the question of original motivation. But this emphasis reflects a particular philosophy of fact-checking — one that prioritizes chronological precision and causal attribution over emotional or thematic accuracy. Was Rudolph created as a gift for May's daughter? No, strictly speaking. Was Rudolph created by a man whose wife was dying of cancer, and did he test it on his daughter as he wrote it? Yes. The difference between these two formulations matters, but how much does it matter? The Snopes article treats this distinction as central; the popular narrative treats it as incidental.

The article also does not adequately address the genre of the narrative it is correcting. The popular Rudolph origin story has the characteristics of what sociologists call a "foundation myth" — a simplified, dramatized account of a true story that serves cultural purposes beyond factual accuracy. Foundation myths are not lies; they are condensed narratives that preserve what a community finds meaningful about an event. The Rudolph story, in its popular form, preserves the meaning of May's achievement — that love and creativity can emerge from grief and that even a corporate assignment can be animated by personal sincerity. Snopes treats the myth as a factual claim to be adjudicated rather than a cultural artifact to be understood.

Furthermore, the Snopes article does not discuss the broader context of the Rudolph phenomenon — the massive success of the 1964 Rankin/Bass television special, the proliferation of Rudolph merchandise, the character's integration into Santa Claus mythology. Nor does it address the irony that Rudolph, a character created as a money-saving corporate promotion, became one of the most commercially valuable Christmas properties in history. These contextual elements would have enriched the analysis and helped readers understand why the popular myth took the form it did.

The article's tone also merits comment. The description of the popular narrative as "glurgified" — a word meaning "excessively sentimental" — carries a dismissive connotation that undercuts the article's claim to neutral fact-checking. There is a subtle elitism in the implication that ordinary people who share the sentimental version of the story are naive, while Snopes's readers are sophisticated enough to handle the more complex corporate reality.

## 4. Why This Matters

The Rudolph origin story matters beyond the confines of Christmas trivia. It illuminates fundamental questions about how we tell stories about creativity, about the relationship between commerce and art, and about the function of mythology in popular culture.

The persistent appeal of the sentimentalized Rudolph story reflects a deep cultural need. In a society where most creative work is produced within corporate structures — where writers, artists, and designers are employees creating work-for-hire — the Rudolph story offers a fantasy of creative autonomy and corporate redemption. It suggests that genuine creativity can pierce through commercial imperatives, that the system can be moved by individual talent, and that corporate executives can act with unexpected generosity. These are comforting beliefs, and they are not entirely without basis in reality — the copyright transfer from Montgomery Ward to May was indeed an unusual and generous act.

But the real story is more instructive than the fantasy. The real Rudolph was created by a skilled professional doing his job well under difficult personal circumstances. It was refined through feedback from his daughter and his colleagues. It was approved because persuasive illustrations convinced skeptical executives. It succeeded because millions of families found it charming. It made May wealthy because he was fortunate enough to work for a company that ultimately did the right thing. This version of the story is, in some ways, more useful as a model than the fantasy. It suggests that professional craft, perseverance, and a bit of luck can produce something enduring, even within the constraints of corporate employment.

The fact-checking of Rudolph's origins also raises questions about the appropriate scope of fact-checking as an enterprise. Is it important that the popular narrative of Rudolph's creation is chronologically inverted and motivationally simplified? Yes, for some purposes. Is it important enough to warrant correction from a major fact-checking platform? Reasonable people may disagree. The Snopes article treats the Rudolph story as a factual claim because fact-checking is its institutional mandate. But the Rudolph story is not a news story or a political claim; it is a piece of folklore that evolved organically. Subjecting folklore to strict factual scrutiny may be less useful than understanding what the folklore reveals about the culture that created and preserved it.

The deeper issue is about how we understand the relationship between fact and meaning. The popular Rudolph story is factually inaccurate in its details but emotionally and thematically true. It captures something real about Robert May's experience — his grief, his love for his daughter, his creativity in the face of hardship — even as it gets the timeline and motivation wrong. If we insist that all true stories must be factually accurate in every particular, we lose the ability to tell stories that convey emotional truth through dramatization. The Rudolph case suggests that there is room for both factual accuracy and narrative meaning — and that the most effective communication often requires a nuanced understanding of both.

## 5. Research Notes

The Snopes article sources its factual claims primarily through historical accounts of Rudolph's creation. The key sources include published interviews with Robert L. May, historical records of Montgomery Ward, and the account of the song's creation through Johnny Marks and Gene Autry. These sources are credible and well-established in the historical record.

The article does not cite a single published source for its corrections of the popular narrative. The claim that Rudolph was created as a commercial assignment rather than a personal gift appears to rest on the author's own research and knowledge. While the account is consistent with what is known from reliable historical sources, greater transparency about the evidentiary basis would have strengthened the article.

Notably, the article does not engage with the question of how the popular narrative originated. Who wrote the first version of the sentimentalized story? When did it start circulating? How did it spread? Understanding the genealogy of the myth would have helped readers understand why the myth took the form it did and why it persists. Without this context, readers are left with the impression that the myth appeared from nowhere and simply propagated because people are credulous.

The article also does not explore the role of inspirational publishing and email forward culture in spreading the sentimentalized narrative. The Rudolph origin story is the kind of narrative that thrives on inspirational websites, in church newsletters, and in forwarded emails — platforms that prioritize emotional impact over factual precision. A more thorough analysis would have addressed why these platforms are particularly susceptible to this kind of narrative compression.

The absence of Barbara May's perspective is notable. By 2013, when the article was published, Barbara would have been in her late seventies. Did she ever comment on the popular version of the story? Did she feel that it distorted her father's achievement, or did she see it as capturing the essential truth? Her voice is missing from the analysis.

The article's strength is in its narrative clarity and its willingness to correct a popular misconception with a more accurate account. Its weakness is in its failure to engage with the cultural meaning of the myth it is correcting — treating the myth as a mistake to be fixed rather than a phenomenon to be understood.

## 6. Verdict

The Snopes article "The Origins of Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer" is a factually accurate correction of a popular narrative that inverts the chronology and motivation of Rudolph's creation. Rudolph was not a spontaneous gift from a grieving father to his daughter; it was a commercial assignment from Montgomery Ward that May worked on while grieving his wife's death. The article correctly identifies which elements of the popular story are true and which are inaccurate.

The "Mixture" rating is appropriate for the narrow question of whether the popular narrative is entirely true or entirely false. The popular narrative contains a mixture of accurate elements — May's wife's cancer, the testing on his daughter, the copyright return, the song's success — and inaccurate ones — the spontaneous creation, the gift motivation, the chronology.

However, the article's treatment of the popular narrative as primarily a factual claim to be adjudicated rather than a cultural artifact to be understood represents a limitation of the fact-checking approach. The popular Rudolph story functions as a foundation myth — a simplified narrative that preserves emotional and thematic truth at the expense of chronological precision. Snopes's correction is valuable but incomplete. A complete analysis would have explored why the myth persists, what cultural needs it serves, and how it relates to the more complex reality.

The gap category for this entry is "distorted but grounded": the popular origin story of Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer distorts the timeline and motivation of the character's creation, but every significant biographical element in the myth — May's grief, his daughter's involvement, the commercial success, the copyright return — is grounded in fact. The distortion is not invention but compression and reordering. Understanding this distinction is essential for anyone who wants to think seriously about how fact and meaning interact in the stories we tell about creativity, commerce, and the holidays.
