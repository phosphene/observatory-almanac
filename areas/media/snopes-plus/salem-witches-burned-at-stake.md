# Accused Witches Were Burned at the Stake During the Salem Witch Trials

## 1. The Claim

The popular belief holds that during the Salem witch trials of 1692, accused witches in colonial Massachusetts were burned at the stake. This image — women tied to posts, flames rising around them, Puritan magistrates watching with grim satisfaction — is among the most vivid scenes in the American historical imagination. It appears in casual conversation, popular media, political rhetoric, educational materials, and even some ostensibly historical dramatizations. When Americans invoke "Salem" as shorthand for persecution, moral panic, or theocratic excess, the burning stake is almost invariably the visual anchor of the reference.

The claim is false. Not a single person was burned at the stake during the Salem witch trials. The actual methods of execution — hanging and, in one extraordinary case, pressing — were dictated by a specific legal framework that the Massachusetts Bay Colony inherited from English common law. Understanding why the burning myth persists requires tracing the legal, cultural, and cognitive pathways through which a continental European practice became fused in collective memory with a distinctly Anglo-American legal proceeding.

This entry examines the claim through three lenses: the legal-historical framework that determined how Salem's accused were actually executed; the named victims whose individual fates the burning myth erases; and the epistemic mechanisms by which a politically useful historical falsehood resists correction even when the documentary record is unambiguous.

## 2. What's Actually True

### The Legal Framework: English Common Law vs. Continental Canon Law

The method of execution applied at Salem was not a matter of local discretion or Puritan zealotry operating in a legal vacuum. It was determined by a precise statutory inheritance. The Massachusetts Bay Colony operated under English common law, and the operative statute governing witchcraft prosecutions was the **English Witchcraft Act of 1604** (1 Jac. 1, c. 12), enacted in the first year of James I's reign. This statute replaced the earlier Elizabethan Witchcraft Act of 1563 (5 Eliz. 1, c. 16) and significantly expanded the range of offenses punishable by death, but it was absolutely explicit about the method: convicted witches were to be hanged by the neck until dead, as with any other felon under common law (Levack, B.P., 2006, *The Witch-Hunt in Early Modern Europe*, 3rd ed., Pearson Longman).

This distinction is not incidental — it reflects a fundamental jurisdictional boundary in European legal history. **Burning at the stake was the penalty prescribed under ecclesiastical and canon law**, particularly for heresy, and it was the dominant method of execution in witch trials conducted under the inquisitorial legal systems of continental Europe. In the Holy Roman Empire, France, Scotland (which followed a mixed legal tradition with significant continental influences), and other jurisdictions where ecclesiastical courts held sway over matters of spiritual deviance, burning was both the standard punishment and a theological statement: the fire was understood to purify the heretic's soul and eliminate the physical vessel of corruption (Kors, A.C. & Peters, E., 2001, *Witchcraft in Europe, 400–1700: A Documentary History*, 2nd ed., University of Pennsylvania Press).

England's common law tradition drew a sharp line between secular felony and ecclesiastical heresy. Witchcraft, as codified in the 1604 Act, was prosecuted as a secular crime — a felony against the King's peace — not as a theological offense against divine order. The accused were tried in secular courts by juries, not by inquisitorial tribunals. The penalty followed the standard common law punishment for felony: hanging. This legal architecture traveled intact to the Massachusetts Bay Colony. When the Court of Oyer and Terminer was established in Salem in May 1692 by Governor William Phips, it operated under this English common law framework. The judges — Chief Justice William Stoughton, Samuel Sewall, John Hathorne, and others — applied English legal procedure, including the felony penalty of hanging (Rosenthal, B., 1993, *Salem Story: Reading the Witch Trials of 1692*, Cambridge University Press).

The distinction between hanging jurisdictions and burning jurisdictions maps almost perfectly onto the distinction between common law and civil/canon law traditions in early modern Europe. England and its colonies hanged witches. The German states, France, Switzerland, parts of Scandinavia, and Scotland burned them. The thousands of burnings that occurred in Germany alone during the great witch-hunting panics of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries — estimates range from 25,000 to 50,000 executions across continental Europe, many by burning — created the dominant visual template that would later be misapplied to Salem (Levack, 2006; Behringer, W., 2004, *Witches and Witch-Hunts: A Global History*, Polity Press).

### The Named Victims: Nineteen Hanged, One Pressed, Several Dead in Custody

The documentary record of the Salem executions is extensive and specific. The **Salem Witch Trial Documentary Archive and Transcription Project** at the University of Virginia has digitized and transcribed the original court records, depositions, warrants, and execution documents, providing primary-source verification for every execution that occurred during the trials.

The executions proceeded in four waves across the summer and early autumn of 1692, all by hanging at Gallows Hill (also known as Proctor's Ledge, as confirmed by the 2016 archaeological study by Carlson, L.M. and others published in cooperation with the City of Salem):

**First execution — June 10, 1692:**
- **Bridget Bishop** — the first person executed. Bishop had been accused of witchcraft previously in 1680 and was widely regarded with suspicion in Salem Village. Her trial was expedited, and she was hanged just eight days after her conviction (Boyer, P. & Nissenbaum, S., 1974, *Salem Possessed: The Social Origins of Witchcraft*, Harvard University Press).

**Second wave — July 19, 1692:**
- **Sarah Good** — a homeless, impoverished woman whose social marginality made her a ready target. At the gallows, when the Reverend Nicholas Noyes urged her to confess, Good reportedly replied: "I am no more a witch than you are a wizard, and if you take away my life God will give you blood to drink" (Rosenthal, 1993).
- **Elizabeth Howe** — a farmer's wife from Topsfield, accused on the basis of spectral evidence and longstanding neighborhood disputes.
- **Susannah Martin** — of Amesbury, a widow who had been accused of witchcraft decades earlier and who maintained a defiant composure throughout her trial.
- **Rebecca Nurse** — a 71-year-old grandmother from a respected family. Her conviction was particularly controversial; the jury initially returned a verdict of not guilty, but Chief Justice Stoughton instructed them to reconsider, and they reversed their finding. Nurse's case became a turning point in public opinion against the trials (Norton, M.B., 2002, *In the Devil's Snare: The Salem Witchcraft Crisis of 1692*, Alfred A. Knopf).
- **Sarah Wildes** — of Topsfield, whose family had longstanding feuds with the Putnam family of Salem Village, illustrating the social-factional dynamics that Boyer and Nissenbaum (1974) identified as central to the crisis.

**Third wave — August 19, 1692:**
- **George Burroughs** — a former minister of Salem Village. At the gallows, Burroughs recited the Lord's Prayer perfectly, which according to folk belief was impossible for a witch. The crowd wavered, but Cotton Mather, present on horseback, urged the execution forward. Burroughs' case remains one of the most dramatic and morally troubling episodes of the trials (Rosenthal, 1993).
- **Martha Carrier** — described by Cotton Mather as a "rampant hag" and "Queen of Hell." She maintained her innocence to the end.
- **George Jacobs Sr.** — an elderly man whose own granddaughter, Margaret Jacobs, had testified against him under pressure and later recanted in anguish.
- **John Proctor** — a prosperous farmer and tavern keeper who had vocally opposed the trials. Proctor's case became the basis for Arthur Miller's 1953 play *The Crucible*, though Miller significantly altered the biographical details for dramatic purposes.
- **John Willard** — a constable who had initially participated in arresting the accused but then refused to make further arrests, after which he himself was accused.

**Fourth wave — September 22, 1692:**
This was the largest single day of executions, with eight people hanged:
- **Martha Corey** — a church member whose husband Giles had also been accused.
- **Mary Eastey** — Rebecca Nurse's sister. Her petition to the court from prison, pleading not for her own life but for the lives of those who would come after her, is among the most moving documents in American legal history (Salem Witch Trial Documentary Archive, University of Virginia).
- **Ann Pudeator** — a widow and medical practitioner from Salem Town.
- **Alice Parker** — a Salem Town resident accused on the basis of spectral evidence.
- **Mary Parker** — of Andover, no relation to Alice Parker, swept up in the Andover phase of the accusations.
- **Wilmot Redd** — of Marblehead, about whom relatively little is known beyond the court records.
- **Margaret Scott** — an elderly widow from Rowley.
- **Samuel Wardwell** — of Andover, who initially confessed but then recanted, sealing his fate. Those who confessed and maintained their confessions were generally spared; those who recanted were executed.

**The pressing of Giles Corey — September 19, 1692:**
Giles Corey, Martha Corey's husband, refused to enter a plea — neither guilty nor not guilty. Under English common law, a defendant who "stood mute" could be subjected to *peine forte et dure* ("strong and hard punishment"), in which progressively heavier stones were placed upon the chest to compel a plea. Corey endured two days of pressing and, according to tradition, his only words were "more weight." He died without entering a plea. His refusal was likely strategic: under the law of the time, a person who died without being convicted could not have their estate forfeited to the government, thereby preserving his family's inheritance (Rosenthal, 1993; Norton, 2002).

**Deaths in custody:**
Several accused persons died in jail during the trials, including Sarah Osborne, Roger Toothaker, Lydia Dustin, and Ann Foster. Conditions in the colonial jails were dire — cold, overcrowded, disease-ridden — and the accused were required to pay for their own imprisonment, including the cost of their chains (Norton, 2002).

In total: **nineteen hanged, one pressed to death, and at least four to five dead in custody**. Zero burned at the stake.

## 3. Why People Believe This

### Source Conflation and the Continental Template

The most straightforward explanation for the burning myth is **source conflation** — the merging of two historically distinct traditions of witch prosecution into a single composite image. The European witch-hunts of the fifteenth through seventeenth centuries produced an estimated 40,000 to 60,000 executions, with major concentrations in the German-speaking territories, France, Switzerland, and Scotland (Levack, 2006). In these jurisdictions, burning was the normative method. The sheer scale of continental witch-burning — vastly exceeding the 19 executions at Salem — means that the "burning witch" is the statistically dominant image in the broader history of European witch persecution. When Americans think of "witch trials" generically, they access the continental template and map it onto the only witch trial they know by name: Salem.

This conflation is reinforced by the fact that American popular culture rarely engages with the legal specifics of colonial Massachusetts. The distinction between common law and canon law — decisive for determining the method of execution — is not part of general historical literacy. Without that legal framework, there is no cognitive barrier to substituting the more dramatic, more widely-represented image of burning for the historically accurate but visually less distinctive image of hanging.

### The Role of Cinema and Literature

Visual media has been a powerful engine of the burning myth. The 1937 Paramount film *Maid of Salem*, starring Claudette Colbert, depicted dramatic fire-related imagery in its portrayal of the Salem trials, embedding the visual association for a mass audience decades before widespread historical correction was available. Arthur Miller's *The Crucible* (1953), while not literally depicting burning, intensified the fire-and-persecution imagery through its allegorical structure — the play's political purpose was to dramatize McCarthyism, and the language of "burning" and "crucible" (a vessel for melting metals by fire) saturated the cultural reception of Salem with fire imagery. The 1996 film adaptation of *The Crucible*, starring Daniel Day-Lewis and Winona Ryder, further cemented the association through visual aesthetics that emphasized flames, torches, and conflagration even in scenes depicting hanging (Reis, E., 1997, *Damned Women: Sinners and Witches in Puritan New England*, Cornell University Press).

The feedback loop between dramatic representation and historical memory is well-documented. Cultural products do not merely reflect existing beliefs; they actively construct the imagery through which historical events are remembered. Once the burning image is cinematically established, it becomes the default retrieval cue for "Salem" in popular memory, regardless of historical accuracy.

### Schema Theory and Memory Distortion

The cognitive mechanism underlying this specific error is well-explained by **schema theory** as developed in the landmark study by Brewer, W.F. and Treyens, J.C. (1981, "Role of Schemata in Memory for Places," *Cognitive Psychology*, 13(2), 207–230). Brewer and Treyens demonstrated that people's memories of environments are systematically distorted by their pre-existing schemas — mental frameworks of what "should" be present. Participants who waited in a room designed to look like an office later "remembered" objects consistent with an office schema (books, filing cabinets) that were not actually present, while failing to recall schema-inconsistent objects that were.

Applied to historical memory: once the schema for "witch trial" includes burning at the stake — a schema acquired primarily from continental European history and its cultural representations — individuals will "remember" Salem as involving burning, even if they have at some point encountered the correct information. The schema overwrites the specific, corrective detail. Hanging is schema-inconsistent for "witch trial" in the popular imagination, and so it is forgotten or replaced.

### Collective Memory as Mediated Action

The sociologist James V. Wertsch (2002, *Voices of Collective Remembering*, Cambridge University Press) offers a more structural account. Wertsch argues that collective memory is not a passive repository of facts but a form of **mediated action** — it is shaped by the narrative tools (stories, images, tropes) available in a given culture. Americans remember Salem through a particular narrative tool: the story of religious persecution, theocratic violence, and the suffering of innocent women at the hands of fanatical authorities. Within this narrative, burning is the "correct" image not because it is historically accurate but because it is **narratively functional** — fire encodes destruction, purification, religious zeal, and spectacular cruelty in ways that hanging does not.

Wertsch's framework explains why correction is difficult: the burning image is not an isolated factual error but a load-bearing element in a larger narrative structure. To remove it is to weaken the narrative, and the narrative serves important cultural functions — it is the vehicle through which Americans process anxieties about religion in public life, state power, and the persecution of outsiders.

### Oral Tradition and Narrative Compression

The anthropologist Jan Vansina (1985, *Oral Tradition as History*, University of Wisconsin Press) documented how oral traditions systematically compress and regularize historical events over time. Details that are unusual or institution-specific (such as the common-law specificity of hanging for felony witchcraft) are replaced by more generic, culturally salient versions of the same event-type. A "witch execution" in the oral tradition becomes the most prototypical version of a witch execution — and the prototype, drawn from centuries of continental European practice, is burning. Vansina's mechanism operates regardless of literacy; even in literate societies, the transmission of historical events through conversation, classroom summaries, and media snippets follows oral-tradition dynamics of compression and prototypicalization.

### **Epistemic Novelty: The Burning Myth as Identity-Protective Political Cognition**

Standard fact-checking treats the Salem burning myth as a simple factual error — people don't know the method of execution and can be corrected with the right information. But this framing fails to account for the myth's extraordinary resistance to correction. Surveys consistently show that even educated Americans who have been exposed to corrective information revert to the burning image. The deeper mechanism at work is what we might call **identity-protective historical cognition**, drawing on the framework developed by Kahan, D.M. (2013, "Ideology, Motivated Reasoning, and Cognitive Reflection," *Judgment and Decision Making*, 8(4), 407–424) for political beliefs and extended here to historical memory.

The burning myth is not simply an error — it is a **political myth** that serves specific functions in American self-understanding about the relationship between religion and state power. The image of women burning at the stake encodes a particular moral narrative: that theocratic authority, when unchecked, produces spectacular atrocities against the vulnerable. This narrative is foundational to the American secularist tradition, to feminist readings of history, and to progressive critiques of religious influence in public life. The burning image makes Salem a story about the unique horror of religiously-motivated state violence — a horror that the more prosaic method of hanging, shared with common criminals, does not adequately convey.

When the burning myth is corrected, the correction threatens not just a factual belief but the **moral infrastructure** built upon that belief. If Salem's victims were hanged like common felons rather than burned like heretics, the story becomes less distinctively about religious persecution and more about the ordinary failures of a legal system — less about theocracy and more about bad evidence law (specifically, the admission of "spectral evidence"). The correction deflates the mythic narrative and, with it, the political lessons that narrative is made to carry.

This is precisely the mechanism Nyhan, B. and Reifler, J. (2010, "When Corrections Fail: The Persistence of Political Misperceptions," *Political Behavior*, 32(2), 303–330) identified in their research on the **backfire effect**: corrective information that threatens a person's political identity or ideological commitments can actually strengthen the original misbelief. Applied to the Salem case, we should expect that people for whom the Salem narrative carries significant political meaning — about secularism, feminism, or the dangers of religious authority — would be the most resistant to correction, not because they are least informed, but because they have the most to lose narratively from accepting the correction.

The burning myth thus occupies a peculiar epistemic position: it is a false historical claim that functions as a true political claim. The political truth it encodes — that theocratic power can destroy innocent lives, that women have been disproportionately targeted by systems of spiritual policing — is not undermined by the factual correction. But the emotional and rhetorical force of the political claim *is* diminished by the correction, because burning is a more effective symbol of religious violence than hanging. The myth persists not because people lack access to the correct information but because the correct information is, in a sense, politically less useful than the error.

This analysis suggests that effective correction of the burning myth requires not just stating the correct method of execution but **providing an alternative narrative vehicle** for the legitimate political concerns the myth encodes. The actual history of Salem — with its specific victims, its legal technicalities, its social factionalism — is, in fact, a more damning indictment of institutional failure than the mythologized version. But it requires more cognitive effort to process, and it lacks the single, iconic image that the burning myth provides.

## 4. Verdict

**False.** No accused witch was burned at the stake during the Salem witch trials of 1692. Nineteen people were executed by hanging at Gallows Hill, one man (Giles Corey) was pressed to death under stones for refusing to enter a plea, and several accused persons died in jail. The method of execution was determined by the English Witchcraft Act of 1604 and English common law, which prescribed hanging for felony witchcraft — not burning. The confusion arises from conflation with continental European witch trials conducted under ecclesiastical and canon law, where burning at the stake was the standard punishment for heresy and witchcraft. This conflation has been reinforced by cinematic and literary representations, schema-driven memory distortion, and the political utility of the burning image in American collective memory.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### Salem in the Landscape of Witch Persecution

The Salem trials, while central to American historical consciousness, were a relatively minor episode in the broader history of European and colonial witch persecution. The 19 executions at Salem (plus Giles Corey's pressing) represent a fraction of the tens of thousands of executions that occurred across Europe between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries. The major witch-hunting regions — the German states of Bamberg, Würzburg, Trier, and Cologne; the Pays de Vaud in Switzerland; the Scottish Lowlands; Lorraine and the Basque country — each produced execution tolals that dwarfed Salem's (Behringer, 2004; Levack, 2006).

What makes Salem historically significant is not its scale but its context: it was the largest witch trial in British North America, it occurred in a colonial society that would later become the United States, and it produced a remarkably complete documentary record. The Boyer and Nissenbaum (1974) thesis — that the Salem accusations mapped onto pre-existing social and economic factionalism between Salem Village (agrarian, declining) and Salem Town (mercantile, rising) — transformed the study of witch trials from a narrative of superstition and fanaticism into a sociological analysis of community conflict. Their work, building on the primary sources preserved in the Salem Witch Trial Documentary Archive at the University of Virginia, remains the foundational scholarly interpretation after more than fifty years.

### The Legal-Historical Significance

The distinction between hanging and burning jurisdictions is not merely a matter of trivia — it reveals the deep structural difference between the English common law tradition and the continental inquisitorial tradition. In the common law system, witchcraft was a secular felony: the accused had the right to a jury trial, the right to confront witnesses (however imperfectly exercised), and the right to counsel (rarely provided in practice). The inquisitorial system, by contrast, placed the accused before a clerical tribunal operating under different rules of evidence, where confession — often extracted through torture — was the gold standard of proof, and the punishment of burning served theological as well as punitive purposes (Kors & Peters, 2001).

The Salem trials operated under common law procedure, but with a catastrophic innovation: the Court of Oyer and Terminer admitted **spectral evidence** — testimony that the accused's spirit or specter had appeared to the witness in dreams or visions. This evidentiary standard, which essentially made accusation equivalent to proof, was the procedural engine of the crisis. When Increase Mather published *Cases of Conscience Concerning Evil Spirits* in October 1692, arguing that spectral evidence was insufficient for conviction because the Devil could assume the shape of an innocent person, the intellectual foundation of the trials collapsed. Governor Phips dissolved the Court of Oyer and Terminer, and the replacement Superior Court of Judicature, which excluded spectral evidence, acquitted nearly all remaining defendants (Norton, 2002).

The lesson of Salem, properly understood, is not primarily about the horrors of burning (which did not happen) but about the **fragility of evidentiary standards** — about what happens when a legal system admits a category of evidence that cannot be tested, contested, or falsified. This is a more intellectually demanding lesson than the mythologized version, but it is also more directly applicable to contemporary debates about the reliability of testimony, the standards of proof in legal proceedings, and the dangers of moral panic.

### The Victims Restored

The burning myth does a particular disservice to the actual victims of the Salem trials by replacing their specific, documented fates with a generic image. Rebecca Nurse was not engulfed in flames — she was hanged at age 71 after a jury was pressured into reversing its acquittal. Giles Corey was not burned — he was crushed under stones over two days in an act of state-sanctioned torture designed to compel speech. George Burroughs was not consumed by fire — he recited the Lord's Prayer perfectly at the gallows, shaking the faith of the crowd in his guilt, before being hanged anyway at Cotton Mather's insistence. Mary Eastey was not incinerated — she wrote, from her jail cell, a petition of extraordinary dignity asking the court to examine its methods so that "no more Innocent Blood be shed" (Salem Witch Trial Documentary Archive, University of Virginia).

These individual stories are more powerful, more morally complex, and more historically instructive than the generic burning image. The myth replaces twenty distinct human tragedies with a single, undifferentiated spectacle. Correcting the myth is, among other things, an act of restoration — returning to the victims their actual deaths and their actual words.

### The Rehabilitation and Its Incompleteness

The Massachusetts colonial legislature began acknowledging errors relatively quickly by historical standards. In 1711, a bill restored the good names and provided financial restitution to the families of some of the accused. But the rehabilitation was incomplete and selective. It was not until 1957 that the Commonwealth of Massachusetts formally apologized for the trials. And it was not until 2001 — more than three hundred years after the events — that the last five victims who had not been individually named in the 1711 legislation were officially exonerated by an act of the Massachusetts legislature (Norton, 2002). The long arc of rehabilitation itself tells a story about how institutions process their own complicity in injustice, and how the incomplete correction of historical wrongs creates the conditions for mythologization.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Salem burning myth is rated **False** by Snopes, which notes that the accused at Salem were hanged, not burned, and that the confusion stems from conflation with European witch trials. The Snopes assessment is factually accurate and clearly presented, directing readers to the historical record.

Standard fact-checking approaches to this claim tend to follow a predictable structure: state the popular belief, provide the correct historical facts (hanging, not burning), cite the legal and historical basis for the distinction, and note the European origin of the burning image. This is effective as far as it goes, and it provides the essential corrective information.

However, the analysis in this entry suggests that the standard approach is **necessary but insufficient** for several reasons:

**First**, the burning myth is not merely a factual error but a schema-embedded narrative element (Brewer & Treyens, 1981). Correcting a schema-embedded belief requires not just presenting the correct information but actively restructuring the schema — helping people build a new mental model of "witch trial in colonial America" that has hanging, not burning, as its default image. This is a pedagogical challenge, not just an informational one.

**Second**, the myth's persistence is driven partly by identity-protective cognition (Kahan, 2013; Nyhan & Reifler, 2010). When correction threatens a valued narrative, the correction itself may be resisted. Effective correction needs to acknowledge the legitimate moral concerns encoded in the burning image — the reality of religious persecution, the disproportionate targeting of women, the horror of state-sanctioned killing of the innocent — while redirecting those concerns toward the actual historical facts, which are no less horrifying for being different from the myth.

**Third**, the correction needs to provide what Wertsch (2002) would call an alternative **mediational tool** — a new narrative, image, or framework that can carry the same cultural meaning as the burning myth while being historically accurate. The image of Giles Corey whispering "more weight" as stones crushed his chest is, arguably, more powerful than any burning scene. The image of Rebecca Nurse, acquitted by a jury and then convicted after judicial pressure, is a more precise indictment of institutional failure than the generic spectacle of flames. The petition of Mary Eastey, written in her own hand from jail, is a more moving document of innocence and dignity than any cinematic burning.

**Fourth**, fact-checkers could do more to explain the **legal-historical framework** that determined the method of execution. The distinction between common law and canon law is not mere historical trivia — it illuminates the structural difference between two entire legal civilizations and their respective approaches to deviance, punishment, and the boundary between church and state. Making this distinction vivid and comprehensible helps readers understand not just *what* happened at Salem but *why* — and, crucially, why it happened differently from what they expected.

The broader lesson for fact-checking methodology is that myths which serve political or identity-protective functions require a different correction strategy than simple factual errors. A claim like "Salem witches were burned" resembles, in its epistemic structure, political misperceptions more than it resembles ordinary ignorance. The correction must engage not only with the facts but with the **narrative economy** in which the myth circulates — understanding why the false version is preferred, what cultural work it performs, and what alternative narratives can carry the same moral weight while remaining true.

The Truth Vault rates this claim **FALSE** with high confidence, based on an unambiguous documentary record, clear statutory law, and scholarly consensus across more than a century of historical research.
