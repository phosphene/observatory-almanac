# "Salem 'witches' were burned at the stake"

The Salem witch trials stand as one of colonial America's darkest chapters, a period when accusations of witchcraft swept through Massachusetts Bay Colony like wildfire. Yet despite their prominent place in American historical memory, these events are frequently misrepresented in popular culture and casual discourse. Perhaps no misconception is more persistent than the belief that accused witches in Salem were burned at the stake.

This claim appears regularly in casual historical references, Halloween decorations, and even some educational materials. The image of flames consuming the accused has become so deeply embedded in our collective understanding of Salem that correcting it often meets with genuine surprise. Yet the historical record is unambiguous: not a single person was burned at the stake during the Salem witch trials of 1692-1693.


## 1. The Claim

**The Claim:** During the Salem witch trials of 1692-1693, accused witches were executed by being burned at the stake.

This assertion appears in various forms across popular culture, social media discussions, and sometimes even in educational contexts. It manifests as direct statements ("They burned witches at Salem"), visual representations in media, and casual assumptions underlying broader discussions of the trials. The claim often appears alongside accurate elements of the Salem story, making it particularly insidious—surrounded by factual information about the accusations, trials, and community hysteria, the burning detail slips by unchallenged.

The persistence of this misconception speaks to deeper patterns in how historical memory forms and spreads. Salem has become a cultural touchstone for religious extremism, mass hysteria, and injustice, but the specific details of what actually happened have been obscured by centuries of literary and artistic interpretation.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Historical Record:** The documented facts about Salem executions are stark and precise. Between June and September 1692, nineteen people were hanged on what became known as Gallows Hill (now Proctor's Ledge). The victims were:

- June 10, 1692: Bridget Bishop
- July 19, 1692: Rebecca Nurse, Sarah Good, Susannah Martin, Elizabeth Howe, and Sarah Wildes 
- August 19, 1692: John Willard, John Proctor, George Jacobs Sr., George Burroughs, and Martha Carrier
- September 22, 1692: Martha Corey, Mary Easty, Alice Parker, Mary Parker, Ann Pudeator, Wilmot Redd, Margaret Scott, and Samuel Wardwell

Additionally, Giles Corey was pressed to death with stones on September 19, 1692, after refusing to enter a plea. Several others died in jail while awaiting trial, including Sarah Osborne, Roger Toothaker, Lyndia Dustin, and Ann Foster.

**Legal Framework:** The Salem trials operated under English common law as applied in Massachusetts Bay Colony. Under this system, witchcraft was a felony punishable by hanging, not burning. The colonial legal code, derived from English precedent, prescribed hanging as the standard method of execution for most capital crimes, including witchcraft.

This stands in stark contrast to continental European practice, where burning alive (often preceded by strangling) was the typical punishment for witchcraft, heresy, and other crimes considered particularly heinous. The difference reflects distinct legal traditions: English common law versus Roman law influences in continental Europe.

**Contemporary Documentation:** Court records, eyewitness accounts, and official documents from the period consistently describe hangings. Thomas Brattle's letter of October 8, 1692, provides a contemporary critique of the trials and specifically mentions hangings. The Reverend Cotton Mather's accounts also reference hangings, not burnings.

No contemporary source suggests burning was ever considered as an execution method during the Salem trials. The absence of such evidence is given the extensive documentation of the proceedings—if burnings had occurred, they would certainly have been recorded.


Snopes' verdict is historically sound and well-supported by primary source evidence. However, the epistemological implications extend beyond simple fact-correction.

**Methodological Reliability:** The distinction between hanging and burning in Salem is not a matter of interpretation or incomplete records—it's documented historical fact supported by multiple independent sources. Court records, contemporary accounts, and physical evidence all confirm hanging as the execution method used.

This represents the kind of historical claim that should be immune to revisionism or alternative interpretations. The evidence is clear, contemporary, and convergent from multiple sources.

**Cultural Memory vs. Historical Fact:** The persistence of the burning myth illustrates how cultural memory can diverge dramatically from historical fact, especially when that divergence serves narrative or symbolic purposes. The burning image carries emotional and symbolic weight that hanging somehow lacks—it seems more dramatic, more medieval, more appropriate to the "witch trial" story as culturally understood.

This divergence matters because it shapes how we understand not just Salem specifically, but religious persecution and mass hysteria more broadly. The burning myth makes Salem seem more foreign, more removed from contemporary American legal and cultural norms, when the reality is more unsettling: Salem happened within recognizable legal frameworks using standard judicial procedures.

**Legal and Cultural Significance:** Understanding the actual execution methods used at Salem provides insight into the legal culture of colonial New England and its relationship to both English precedent and continental European practice. The hanging detail illuminates the extent to which Massachusetts Bay Colony remained tied to English legal traditions even as it developed its own particular forms of Puritan governance.

The distinction also matters for understanding the specific nature of the Salem crisis. The trials represented an extreme application of existing legal frameworks, not an abandonment of legal process in favor of mob violence. This makes the events both more systematic and, in some ways, more troubling than the burning myth suggests.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The misconception about burning appears to have multiple overlapping sources, creating a complex web of cultural confusion that has proven remarkably durable.

**Literary Influence:** Arthur Miller's "The Crucible" (1953), while not explicitly depicting burnings, contributed to a dramatic reimagining of Salem that emphasized fire imagery and inflammatory rhetoric. The play's powerful metaphors and emotional intensity helped cement Salem in popular consciousness as a story of consuming passion and destructive fervor—imagery that readily translates to actual flames in popular memory.

More directly influential were earlier literary works, including Washington Irving's writings and various 19th-century novels that freely mixed European witch trial elements with Salem details. These works treated the historical Salem as part of a broader "witch burning" narrative that conflated different times, places, and legal systems.

**Visual Culture:** Film, television, and artistic representations consistently show witch executions as burnings, regardless of historical context. From early Hollywood productions to modern horror films, the visual language of witch persecution centers on stakes and flames. This imagery is so pervasive that it has become the default cultural reference point for witch executions generally.

Halloween decorations, museum displays, and educational materials frequently perpetuate this visual confusion. Even when creators know the Salem-specific facts, they often default to burning imagery because it has become the universal symbol of witch persecution.

**European Context Bleeding:** The broader history of European witch trials, where burning was indeed common, creates a backdrop that influences how Americans understand Salem. Most people have some awareness that "witches were burned" historically, and they apply this general knowledge to the specific Salem case without recognizing the legal and cultural differences.

This reflects a common pattern in historical memory where specific events become absorbed into broader cultural narratives, losing their particular details in the process. Salem becomes not just the Salem witch trials, but representative of "witch trials" generally.

**Educational Compression:** Textbooks and educational materials, striving for brevity, often present Salem within broader discussions of religious persecution or witch hunting that include European examples. Students may encounter descriptions of burning in one context and hangings in another, with the distinction blurring over time.

The pedagogical challenge is real: teaching Salem requires explaining both its uniqueness and its place in broader patterns of persecution, but the more context provided, the more opportunities for conflation arise.


## 4. Verdict

**RESOLVED:** The historical record definitively establishes that no one was burned at the stake during the Salem witch trials of 1692-1693. All 19 executions were carried out by hanging, with one additional death by pressing. This is not a matter of interpretation but of documented historical fact.

**Gap Category Confirmed:** Distorted-but-grounded. The confusion stems from conflating European witch trial practices with colonial American procedures. The underlying knowledge that "witches were burned" historically is accurate for many contexts, but incorrect for Salem specifically.

**Epistemological Status:** This represents a clear case where Snopes' fact-checking aligns perfectly with scholarly consensus and primary source evidence. The correction is straightforward and should be universally adopted.

**Cultural Significance:** The persistence of this misconception illuminates how historical memory forms and spreads, particularly how dramatic imagery can override factual accuracy. Understanding the actual Salem execution methods provides more accurate insight into colonial legal culture and the specific nature of the 1692-1693 crisis.

**Recommendation:** Educational materials should explicitly address this misconception while explaining the differences between European and colonial American witch trial practices. The correction provides an opportunity to teach both accurate Salem history and broader lessons about legal systems, cultural transmission, and historical methodology.

The Salem witch trials were horrific enough without embellishment. The reality—systematic legal proceedings leading to the execution of twenty people and the imprisonment of many more based on spectral evidence—requires no dramatic enhancement. Understanding what actually happened, rather than what we imagine happened, provides clearer insight into both the specific historical moment and the broader human capacity for systemic injustice conducted through ostensibly legitimate institutions.

In this case, Snopes' fact-checking serves not just to correct a misconception, but to restore historical specificity to events that have been absorbed into broader cultural narratives. The truth about Salem—that it operated through recognizable legal frameworks using standard procedures to produce unjust outcomes—may be more unsettling than the burning myth, but it is also more instructive for understanding how such injustices occur and might be prevented.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The Salem burning misconception exists within a broader landscape of historical distortions about witch trials, colonial America, and religious persecution.

**Comparative Context:** Across Europe, burning was indeed the standard execution method for convicted witches. In the Holy Roman Empire, France, Scotland, and other jurisdictions influenced by Roman law traditions, burning alive was prescribed for witchcraft, often with the "mercy" of prior strangulation. The Spanish Inquisition, various papal inquisitions, and secular courts all employed burning for heresy and witchcraft.

England itself had a more complex history. While burning was used for heresy and for women convicted of petty treason (including husband murder), simple witchcraft was typically punished by hanging. However, aggravated cases or repeat offenders might face burning. The English Witchcraft Acts of 1542, 1563, and 1604 generally prescribed hanging, though they allowed for more severe punishments in extreme cases.

The key distinction is that by 1692, Massachusetts was operating under a simplified version of English law that had largely standardized hanging for felonies. The complexity and discretionary elements present in English practice had been reduced to more straightforward colonial applications.

**American Witch Trial Context:** Salem was neither the first nor the last witch prosecution in colonial America, but it was by far the largest and most systematic. Earlier cases, such as those in Connecticut (including the Hartford witch panic of 1662-1663), also used hanging as the execution method. Later cases, continuing sporadically into the 18th century, maintained the same approach.

This consistency across time and place in colonial America reflects the systematic adoption of English legal frameworks rather than ad hoc community responses. American witch prosecutions were conducted within established legal structures, not as lynch mob actions or community vigilante responses.

**Historiographical Evolution:** The scholarly understanding of Salem has evolved significantly since the 19th century. Early accounts, influenced by Romantic literary sensibilities and anti-Puritan sentiment, emphasized the irrationality and extremism of the proceedings. These accounts often imported elements from European witch trial narratives, including burning imagery.

Modern scholarship, beginning with works like Paul Boyer and Stephen Nissenbaum's "Salem Possessed" (1974), has focused more on understanding Salem within its specific social, economic, and political context. This approach has produced more accurate accounts of what actually happened, while also providing deeper insights into why it happened.

The persistence of the burning myth in popular culture represents a lag between scholarly and popular understanding that characterizes many historical topics. Academic corrections can take decades to filter into general cultural knowledge, especially when they contradict emotionally powerful misconceptions.

**Legal History Implications:** The Salem execution methods illuminate broader patterns in colonial American legal development. The consistency with which English precedents were followed, even under extreme circumstances, demonstrates the extent to which colonial legal culture remained derivative of English practice.

This has implications for understanding the development of American legal traditions more broadly. Even during periods of crisis and apparent breakdown, colonial legal systems maintained their essential structural characteristics. Salem represents an extreme stress test of colonial legal culture, not its abandonment.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates this claim as definitively **FALSE**. Their fact-check, available at snopes.com/fact-check/salem-witch-trials-burned-stake/, provides clear documentation that no one was burned at the stake during the Salem witch trials. The site emphasizes that while burning was indeed the preferred method of witch execution in many parts of Europe, the English colonial legal system followed different precedents.

Snopes correctly identifies the source of confusion: the conflation of European witch trial practices with those in colonial New England. Their assessment aligns with the scholarly consensus among historians of early America and specialists in the Salem trials specifically.

However, Snopes' treatment, while accurate, is relatively brief and doesn't fully explore the historiographical dimensions of how this misconception became so widespread. The fact-check focuses primarily on correcting the record rather than examining why the burning narrative took hold so tenaciously in American cultural memory.
