## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In September 2021, multiple news outlets reported that Samuel Adams — the American craft beer brand owned by Boston Beer Company — was releasing a new edition of its "Utopias" product, a beer with an alcohol by volume (ABV) content of 28%, at a suggested retail price of $240 per bottle. The reports also claimed that the product would be illegal in 15 specific U.S. states due to their laws governing alcohol content thresholds.

Snopes Senior Assignments Editor Jessica Lee (2021) assigned this claim the rating **True**. The Samuel Adams Utopias 2021 edition was confirmed through a September 16, 2021 PR Newswire press release from Boston Beer Company, which confirmed the 28% ABV figure, the October 11, 2021 release date, and the $240 suggested retail price. The company's own website FAQ and product page listed 15 specific states — Alabama, Arkansas, Georgia, Idaho, Missouri, Mississippi, Montana, North Carolina, New Hampshire, Oklahoma, Oregon, South Carolina, Utah, Vermont, and West Virginia — in which the product would not be sold due to their regulatory restrictions on high-alcohol beverages.

This entry examines the Utopias brewing process that achieves a 28% ABV figure in a beer product, the legal frameworks in the 15 prohibitory states, the history of the Utopias biennial release series, and the broader epistemics of alcohol regulation as a patchwork system that creates genuine cross-state legal heterogeneity for commercially available products.

## §2 Samuel Adams Utopias: Product Technology and the 2021 Edition

The Samuel Adams Utopias series represents an extreme end of the craft brewing spectrum — a product that approaches and in some editions overlaps in ABV with low-end distilled spirits, achieved entirely through brewery fermentation and barrel aging rather than distillation. Standard beer is typically between 4% and 7% ABV, with craft ales extending to roughly 8–12% in the strongest conventional expressions. The 28% ABV achieved by Utopias 2021 is approximately 5.6 times the ABV of a standard 5% beer and more than double the ABV of a standard 12% wine.

The process by which a fermented beer product achieves 28% ABV without distillation involves several convergent techniques. First, the base fermentation uses extreme high-gravity brewing with specialty yeast strains capable of tolerating high-alcohol environments. Standard brewing yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) typically becomes inhibited at alcohol concentrations approaching 12–15%; achieving higher ABV requires either using highly alcohol-tolerant yeast strains (sometimes employed in so-called "champagne yeast" finishing techniques) or blending multiple high-gravity batches. Bamforth (2003), writing in *Journal of the Institute of Brewing* (IoB/Elsevier), documented that maximum fermentation-achievable ABV in brewing lies approximately in the 20–24% range without artificial concentration techniques.

Second, "freeze concentration" — the cryoconcentration technique sometimes applied in extreme beers — involves partially freezing the fermented product and removing water-ice, concentrating the remaining liquid's alcohol content. This technique is legally considered to blur the line between brewing and distillation in some jurisdictions, and its use in Utopias has not been formally disclosed by the company. Third, extensive barrel aging in a variety of previous spirits casks — Boston Beer Company has stated that various Utopias editions have been aged in bourbon, Cognac, port, Curaçao triple sec, and Madeira wine casks, which contribute flavor complexity and may contribute residual alcohol through absorption and transfer — further develops the product's character.

The 2021 edition was specifically finished on 2,000 pounds of Michigan Balaton cherries, contributing fruit sweetness and tartness. It is not carbonated — as noted by Fortune (2021) in coverage cited by Lee (2021) — a consequence of the alcohol level destroying CO₂ retention at bottling. The recommended serving size is one ounce, aligning Utopias with spirits rather than conventional beer consumption norms. The flavor profile has been described by multiple reviewers as resembling a fine liqueur — sweet, complex, with port and Cognac-like character — rather than a conventional beer.

At $240 per 25.4-ounce bottle, the product's price per ounce ($9.45) substantially exceeds most premium spirits and places it firmly in the collector and connoisseur category. Boston Beer Company produces approximately 13,000 bottles of Utopias every other year, beginning in 2002, making each edition genuinely scarce.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The American Alcohol Regulation Patchwork and Its Commercial Consequences

The core epistemic novelty in this case is the specific regulatory architecture that makes a product commercially available in 35 states and prohibited in 15 based solely on its alcohol content, creating a patchwork of legal availability that is opaque to most American consumers and that follows no obvious geographic or political pattern.

The 15 states cited in the Samuel Adams company FAQ — Alabama, Arkansas, Georgia, Idaho, Missouri, Mississippi, Montana, North Carolina, New Hampshire, Oklahoma, Oregon, South Carolina, Utah, Vermont, and West Virginia — represent a cross-ideological sample: the list includes deep-red conservative states alongside Vermont, a progressive northeastern state, and Oregon, a generally permissive western state. This non-alignment with conventional political geography reflects the specific history of each state's alcohol regulation framework rather than contemporary political ideology.

American alcohol regulation is characterized by the Twenty-First Amendment's explicit preservation of state authority over alcohol distribution following the repeal of Prohibition in 1933. Section 2 of the Twenty-First Amendment specifically states that "the transportation or importation into any State, Territory, or possession of the United States for delivery or use therein of intoxicating liquors, in violation of the laws thereof, is hereby prohibited." This provision — uniquely for constitutional commerce purposes — explicitly authorizes states to regulate alcohol in ways that would otherwise violate the Commerce Clause.

The resulting regulatory landscape is a legacy of Depression-era compromise rather than coherent policy design. Each state's specific alcohol ABV ceiling reflects its own post-Repeal legislative history, lobbying by incumbent alcohol industries, religious and temperance movement influence, and periodic reform efforts. Mosher and Jernigan (2001), writing in the *Journal of Public Health Policy* (Palgrave Macmillan), documented that post-Prohibition alcohol regulation was deliberately fragmented to prevent the re-emergence of the national saloon and brewing trust structures that Prohibitionists had targeted; the fragmented regulatory result, however, also creates commercial anomalies of the Utopias type that the framers of post-Prohibition policy could not have anticipated.

The specific ABV thresholds that trigger prohibition in each of the 15 states vary. Some states restrict any beverage labeled "beer" from exceeding a specific ABV (commonly 6%, 8%, or 12%, depending on the state); others apply higher content thresholds but classify high-ABV fermented beverages as "malt liquor" or "strong beer" subject to separate licensing and distribution requirements. Utopias at 28% ABV exceeds both the standard beer thresholds and, in some states, the threshold for any non-distilled fermented beverage regardless of classification. The state-by-state legal mechanics are not fully developed in the Snopes verification article.

The epistemic novelty for media literacy purposes is that the "illegal in 15 states" framing, while accurate, may be misread as implying that the beer is prohibited in those states because it is inherently dangerous or because the laws target extreme products specifically. In reality, most of the relevant state laws are general ABV-ceiling regulations not written with products like Utopias in mind; the Utopias product simply happens to exceed thresholds that were set for other purposes in various regulatory contexts across different decades.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Lee's (2021) investigation accurately confirmed the core claims — 28% ABV, 15 states where prohibited, $240 price, October 11 release date — through direct citation of the company's press release, website FAQ, and product page, supplemented by Fortune's contemporaneous reporting. Several additional analytical threads were not developed:

**The specific legal basis for prohibition in each of the 15 states**: The article cites the states from Samuel Adams' own FAQ listing but does not independently verify the specific statute in each state that would prohibit the product or explain how Utopias' classification (as "beer") interacts with state classification frameworks.

**Whether freeze concentration is used in Utopias**: The technique of cryoconcentration, if used, would complicate the product's legal status in ways beyond simple ABV thresholds, because it is classified as a distillation-equivalent technique in some regulatory frameworks. The company does not publicly disclose its full production techniques.

**The history of the Utopias series and ABV evolution**: The article notes that the biennial release runs back to 2002 but does not trace the ABV evolution across editions. Earlier editions ranged from approximately 24% to 27% ABV; the 2021 edition at 28% represented the highest ABV in the series' history. Whether earlier editions were also prohibited in the same 15 states is not addressed.

**Consumer safety framing**: With a recommended serving size of one ounce and a 28% ABV, Utopias consumed carelessly as a conventional beer (poured in multi-ounce servings) would deliver alcohol content equivalent to multiple full standard drinks. The article does not discuss the public health framing of extreme-high-ABV products or the consumer safety considerations raised by their labeling as "beer."

**The $240 price and collector market**: The article cites the $240 suggested retail price but does not address the significant secondary market for Utopias, where bottles regularly trade above suggested retail on collector platforms.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Craft Brewing's High-ABV Arms Race and the Limits of Beer as a Category

The Samuel Adams Utopias series exists at the leading edge of what brewing industry observers have described — sometimes admiringly, sometimes critically — as the high-ABV arms race in extreme craft brewing. Beginning in the early 2000s, multiple craft breweries began producing products with progressively higher ABV figures, using techniques including high-gravity brewing, freeze concentration, and barrel aging to push the fermentation-achievable alcohol ceiling.

Scottish brewery BrewDog and the Massachusetts-based Dogfish Head Brewery have historically competed with Boston Beer Company's Utopias series in the extreme-ABV category. BrewDog's Tactical Nuclear Penguin (2009) reached 32% ABV through ice distillation; its Sink the Bismarck! (2010) reached 41%. However, these products were explicitly subjected to a different regulatory framework (spirits regulation) in several jurisdictions precisely because freeze concentration was used in their production. Utopias' claim to be a "beer" — despite its ABV and non-carbonated spirits-like character — is itself contested in brewing community discourse.

Oliver (2012), in *The Oxford Companion to Beer* (Oxford University Press), documented the definitional debate around "extreme beers" and noted that the boundary between beer and spirits is not purely technical (both can be produced through fermentation; distillation is typically the distinguishing process) but also legal and regulatory, with jurisdictions defining "beer" by varied combinations of ingredient requirements, Process requirements, and ABV ceilings. A product that achieves 28% ABV purely through fermentation with no distillation step occupies a genuinely ambiguous zone: it meets the standard process definition of "beer" but exceeds the regulatory ABV definition in 15 states.

The commercial logic of the Utopias series for Boston Beer Company is also worth examining. At $240 retail for approximately 25.4 ounces of product, the revenue per bottle is extraordinary compared to any conventional beer product. With 13,000 bottles per biennial edition, the total gross revenue per edition (at suggested retail) is approximately $3.12 million — a modest figure in absolute terms for a company with hundreds of millions in annual revenue, but significant as a brand-building and prestige-signaling exercise. The Utopias series positions Samuel Adams as an artisanal innovator at the extreme end of the brewing craft, differentiating the brand from both mass-market beer and the broad middle of the craft beer market.

The 15-state prohibition also functions as marketing content: "illegal in 15 states" is an attention-capturing headline that generated substantial media coverage of the 2021 release, contributing to brand awareness and scarcity signaling at effectively zero additional marketing cost. The product's legal unavailability in nearly a third of the country creates a restricted-access prestige dimension that likely enhances rather than diminishes desirability among the collector and connoisseur audience for whom the product is primarily intended.

Cialdini (2009), in *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* (HarperBusiness), documented that scarcity — specifically the perception that a desired object is limited in availability — systematically increases its perceived value and desirability in ways not explained by its intrinsic characteristics. Legal unavailability in specific states is a distinctive form of scarcity that combines geographic restriction with legal prohibition, creating a particularly potent scarcity signal. Whether Boston Beer Company deliberately selected product characteristics that would guarantee multi-state prohibition to exploit this effect, or whether the prohibition is a genuine commercial constraint, is not publicly documented but is worth considering as a strategic context.

## §6 Verdict, EARC Classification, and Knowledge Gaps

**Verdict: TRUE**

Samuel Adams' 2021 Utopias product is real, was released on October 11, 2021, carries a 28% ABV, and is prohibited for sale in 15 states — Alabama, Arkansas, Georgia, Idaho, Missouri, Mississippi, Montana, North Carolina, New Hampshire, Oklahoma, Oregon, South Carolina, Utah, Vermont, and West Virginia — due to their state laws governing maximum alcohol content in fermented beverages. The product is not carbonated, carries a suggested retail price of $240 per 25.4-ounce bottle, is produced in an edition of approximately 13,000 bottles every two years, and has been part of the Samuel Adams product lineup since 2002.

**EARC Classification: E (Epistemic — regulatory complexity and product boundary classification)**

The analytical gap here is epistemic: the claim requires understanding not only that the product exists and is prohibited in specific states (a simple verification task) but why the regulatory framework produces a patchwork of state-level prohibitions that follows no consistent political or geographic logic, how that framework emerged from post-Prohibition constitutional compromise, and how a fermented beverage achieves 28% ABV without distillation. The "illegal in 15 states" framing is accurate but risks misattribution — implying targeted prohibition of a hazardous product rather than incidental collision with general ABV-ceiling regulations not specifically designed for extreme craft beer.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**
- Specific state statutes and regulatory classifications that prohibit Utopias in each of the 15 states
- Whether cryoconcentration or other non-standard concentration techniques are used in Utopias production
- ABV performance across the full 2002–2021 edition series
- Whether any of the 15 prohibitory states have subsequently updated their laws in ways that would affect Utopias availability
- Secondary market valuation data for Utopias editions
