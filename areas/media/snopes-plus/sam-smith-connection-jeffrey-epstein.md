# Sam Smith Panics as 'Connection' to Jeffrey Epstein's Island Is Leaked?


## 1. The Claim

On September 17, 2023, the YouTube channel Drama Bay published a video with the headline: "Sam Smith PANICS As [Their] Connection With Epstein Island LEAKED!" The video, which accumulated over 5,400 views on YouTube and was cross-posted to TikTok where three posts collectively garnered more than 350,000 views, purported to reveal that musician Sam Smith—who came out as nonbinary in 2019 and uses they/them pronouns—had a hidden connection to the late convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein and his network of sexual exploitation.

The Drama Bay channel operates in the celebrity gossip genre, bearing a prominent disclaimer: "Content might be gossip, rumors, exaggerated or indirectly besides the truth. Viewer advised to do own research before forming their opinion. Content might be opinionated." This disclaimer, which Snopes would later cite as a key factor in its assessment, explicitly signals that the channel does not warrant its content as accurate.

Snopes rated the claim "Unfounded" in a fact check published September 25, 2023, authored by Izz Scott LaMagdeleine. The rating category "Unfounded" is defined by Snopes as applying to "a claim that we have investigated but found no demonstrable evidence to support it. Such claims typically originate as hearsay, speculation, or groundless rumor." Snopes' investigation found that the Drama Bay video offered no concrete evidence of any connection between Smith and Epstein, beyond vague references and speculative association.

The claim represents a recurring pattern in the post-Epstein information environment: the creation of viral content that capitalizes on public fascination with Epstein's network while offering no verified information. The Drama Bay video followed established templates for this genre—an alarming headline, a promise of revelatory information, and content that delivers neither evidence nor documentation. Snopes' identification of the claim as unfounded is correct in its specific finding. But the broader phenomenon of Epstein-related rumor-mongering, the platform dynamics that incentivize this content, and the genuine public interest in understanding the full scope of Epstein's network all deserve more thorough examination than a single fact check can provide.


## 2. What's Actually True

Several factual elements in the Snopes assessment are accurate and require no correction.

The Drama Bay YouTube channel's disclaimer constitutes a preemptive acknowledgment that its content may not be factual. Channels that post content in the "gossip, rumors, exaggerated" space operate in a legal and platform-policy gray area: they avoid liability by disclaiming accuracy while benefiting from the engagement that sensational claims generate. Snopes correctly identifies this disclaimer as significant—a content creator that tells its audience not to trust its content has effectively conceded that the content lacks evidentiary foundation.

The Drama Bay video about Sam Smith did not provide any verifiable evidence of a connection between Smith and Epstein. No flight logs, no photographs of Smith with Epstein, no communication records, no witness testimony—nothing that would satisfy even minimal standards of journalistic evidence. The video's claims were vague and associational, relying on the kind of "connect-the-dots" logic that characterizes much Epstein-related rumor content: if someone attended parties, knew certain people, or visited certain locations, a "connection" is implied.

Cross-platform circulation of the video followed the pattern typical of viral misinformation. The TikTok posts that Snopes identified had garnered over 350,000 combined views, demonstrating the demand for Epstein-related content regardless of its evidentiary quality. The public's hunger for information about Epstein's network—fueled by legitimate investigative journalism that has documented extensive connections among powerful figures—creates a market that rumor-mongers exploit with content that carries no guarantee of accuracy.

Snopes' choice of "Unfounded" rather than "False" reflects a nuanced assessment. The "Unfounded" rating is reserved for claims where the investigating body "found no demonstrable evidence to support" them. This is subtly different from "False," which would require demonstrating that the claim is not just unsupported but positively incorrect. The choice of "Unfounded" suggests that Snopes could not rule out the possibility that some undocumented connection might exist, but found no evidence to support the specific claims made in the video. This is a defensible epistemological position, though it raises questions about the burden of proof in celebrity gossip fact-checking.

However, there are dimensions Snopes does not address. The Epstein case has a well-documented history of legitimate investigative reporting being initially dismissed as rumor. Julie K. Brown's reporting for the Miami Herald, which broke open the Epstein case in 2018, was initially met with skepticism. The 2007 non-prosecution agreement that allowed Epstein to plead guilty to state charges while avoiding federal prosecution was known to some journalists years before Brown's reporting but went unpublished due to unverified sources and legal concerns. This history creates a legitimate epistemic challenge: how can the public distinguish between groundless rumors and investigations that, like Brown's, require years of painstaking verification before publication?

Snopes also does not examine the platform economics that drive Drama Bay's content strategy. YouTube's recommendation algorithm demonstrably rewards sensational content with higher engagement metrics, and channels that produce Epstein-related rumors benefit from the platform's incentive structure. The fact that Drama Bay's disclaimer exists suggests awareness of this dynamic and an attempt to insulate the channel from liability while continuing to profit from the engagement its content generates. This is not merely an individual bad actor problem—it is a structural problem of platform design that fact-checking cannot address on its own.

The misgendering of Smith in Drama Bay's headline—using "his" rather than "their"—adds another dimension. While Snopes notes this in passing, the deliberate misgendering of a public figure as part of negative content is a recognized tactic in online harassment. The conflation of Smith's nonbinary identity with involvement in Epstein's network suggests a form of guilt-by-association that exploits anti-LGBTQ prejudice: the implication that non-traditional gender identity correlates with moral corruption. This dimension of the claim deserves more attention than Snopes gives it.


## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes' identification of the Drama Bay video as unfounded is correct on the evidence. The channel's own disclaimer effectively admits its content may not be factual. The video offered no evidence that would satisfy even minimal journalistic standards. The cross-platform amplification followed the established pattern of rumor-driven viral content. These elements of the assessment are sound and defensible.

Where Snopes falls short is in several significant dimensions:

The reliance on the YouTube channel's disclaimer as a primary indicator of unreliability, while factually correct, is epistemologically limited. A content creator's self-description should not be the primary evidence that their content is unreliable—there are many reasons content creators may disclaim accuracy, including legal caution and platform policy compliance. The disclaimer is evidence of the channel's business model, not of the truth or falsity of any specific claim. Snopes' focus on the disclaimer creates an implicit framework where a channel without a disclaimer would be treated as more credible, which is not necessarily justified.

The "Unfounded" rating, while defensible as a technical matter, may create confusion about the claim's evidentiary status. Readers unfamiliar with Snopes' rating taxonomy might interpret "Unfounded" as equivalent to "baseless" rather than as "investigated but insufficient evidence either way." In the context of Epstein-related claims, where legitimate investigative reporting is still emerging years after Epstein's death, the distinction between "no evidence" and "evidence of falsity" is more than semantic—it reflects genuine uncertainty about the extent of Epstein's network. A claim can be unfounded today and supported tomorrow as new evidence emerges from ongoing investigations, litigation, and journalistic work.

Snopes does not address the broader ecosystem of Epstein-related content on YouTube and TikTok. The Drama Bay video is one instance of a much larger pattern. A search for Epstein-related content on YouTube in 2023 would return thousands of videos making unsupported claims about various public figures. The platform's algorithm demonstrably promotes this content because it drives engagement. Snopes treats the Drama Bay video as an isolated instance rather than as symptomatic of a systemic information problem that platform design and content moderation practices actively generate.

The fact check also fails to acknowledge the genuine public interest value of Epstein-related investigations. Not all Epstein rumors are equal: some are groundless speculation designed for engagement farming, while others represent early, unverified attempts to understand a network that legitimate journalists are still working to document. Snopes' "Unfounded" rating applies a single standard to all Epstein claims regardless of their relationship to ongoing investigative work. This flattening of distinction may inadvertently delegitimize the entire project of investigating Epstein's network—a project that has produced genuine public service journalism alongside the inevitable noise of rumor and speculation.

Finally, Snopes does not engage with the question of why the Drama Bay video's audience existed. Over 350,000 views across TikTok suggest genuine public appetite for Epstein-related information. This appetite is understandable given the scale of Epstein's crimes, the documented breadth of his social network, and the perception—supported by evidence—that many of his associates have not been held accountable. Fact-checking that dismisses this appetite as simply credulity toward rumors misses the legitimate information deficit that creates the market for rumor content. When the public cannot obtain verified information about matters of clear public interest, they will consume whatever information is available, regardless of its quality. The solution to this problem is not more dismissal but more reporting—a task at which Snopes, as a platform focused on evaluating existing claims rather than producing original investigations, is structurally limited.


## 4. Why This Matters

The Sam Smith rumor illustrates several structural problems in the contemporary information environment that extend well beyond any single celebrity gossip claim.

The first concerns the relationship between legitimate investigative journalism and the ecosystem of rumor and speculation that surrounds high-profile criminal cases. The Epstein case is perhaps the clearest recent example of this dynamic. Julie K. Brown's 2018 Miami Herald series, "Perversion of Justice," documented how wealthy and well-connected defendants receive preferential treatment in the criminal justice system. Her reporting relied on years of source cultivation, document review, and legal challenges—work that cannot be replicated by content farms operating on YouTube engagement metrics. But the same public demand for information about Epstein's network that made Brown's reporting so consequential also fuels the rumor ecosystem. When Snopes dismisses a Drama Bay video as unfounded, it is performing an important function—but it is also implicitly distinguishing its own work from the work of investigative reporters whose methods and timelines are very different from Snopes' rapid-response model.

The second concerns platform economics and content moderation. YouTube and TikTok earn revenue from the engagement that sensational content generates. A video titled "Sam Smith PANICS As [Their] Connection With Epstein Island LEAKED!" will generate more views, shares, and watch time than a video titled "No Evidence Connects Sam Smith to Jeffrey Epstein's Island." The platform's recommendation algorithms amplify the former while starving the latter of distribution. Snopes' fact check, however accurate, will reach a tiny fraction of the audience that consumed the original rumor. This is not a failure of fact-checking—it is a structural feature of platform-mediated information ecosystems that no individual fact check can overcome.

The third concerns the relationship between mis/disinformation and prejudice. The misgendering of Smith in the Drama Bay headline is not incidental to the claim but integral to it. The video's framing exploits anti-LGBTQ prejudice by suggesting that non-traditional gender identity is associated with moral corruption or criminality. This pattern—associating marginalized groups with high-profile crimes involving powerful figures—has historical precedents and contemporary resonance. The same dynamic appears in antisemitic Epstein conspiracy theories, in claims about immigrant involvement in trafficking networks, and in racialized narratives about crime more broadly. Fact-checking that treats these dimensions as secondary to the factual accuracy of the surface claim misses an important part of the harm that such content causes.

The fourth concerns the epistemology of negative claims in celebrity gossip contexts. The claim that Sam Smith has a "connection" to Epstein's island is what philosophers call a negative claim in the sense that it is inherently difficult to disprove. How would one definitively prove that someone does not have a connection to a sex trafficker? The absence of evidence is not evidence of absence—particularly when the subject in question died before full documentation of his network was possible. This epistemological asymmetry between positive claims (which can be supported with evidence) and negative claims (which cannot be definitively proven) creates a structural advantage for rumor-mongers. Fact-checkers can say "we found no evidence," but they cannot say "this cannot possibly be true." The Drama Bay channel knows this, which is why its business model works despite—or perhaps because of—its "Unfounded" rating. The rating itself becomes part of the content's mystique: the establishment is covering something up, the system is rigged against truth-tellers, the fact-checkers are part of the conspiracy.

This dynamic is not unique to Epstein rumors. It appears in claims about government corruption, corporate malfeasance, and every domain where institutional trust is low and the demand for accountability is high. Fact-checkers who treat each instance as an isolated claim to be evaluated on its evidentiary merits will inevitably miss the structural pattern that makes such claims persistent and resistant to correction.


## 5. Research Notes

The Snopes article by Izz Scott LaMagdeleine, published September 25, 2023, documents the Drama Bay video (YouTube ID YaF4083ApPc), the channel's disclaimer, and cross-platform circulation on TikTok and X (formerly Twitter). The article cites archived versions of the X posts and links to the TikTok posts, providing a clear paper trail for verification. The Billie Eilish connection mentioned in the Snopes article—the claim that a flight log showing Smith mentioned Eilish's name—is an interesting detail that Snopes does not develop: it suggests that the Drama Bay video may have conflated two separate figures, further undermining its credibility.

The broader context of Epstein-related misinformation includes several other Snopes fact checks addressing similar claims. Snopes has previously fact-checked claims about Epstein's "client list," the circumstances of his death, and purported connections between Epstein and various public figures. These fact checks collectively document a persistent pattern of rumor-mongering around the Epstein case that has evolved over time but shows no signs of diminishing.

The regulatory context of YouTube's content moderation policies is relevant but unexplored in the Snopes article. YouTube's policies prohibit content that "harasses, threatens, or bullies individuals" and content that "promotes harmful conspiracy theories," including those related to Jeffrey Epstein. The Drama Bay video—which insinuates a connection between Smith and a convicted sex trafficker while offering no evidence—arguably falls within these prohibited categories. That it remained on the platform despite the policies suggests either that YouTube's enforcement is inconsistent, that the video did not trigger automated detection, or that it was not reported. Snopes does not address this policy dimension, though it would be relevant to understanding the platform's role in circulating the claim.

The Sam Smith rumor is one of many celebrity-focused Epstein rumors that circulated in 2023. Similar claims have appeared about Stephen Hawking, Donald Trump, Bill Clinton, Prince Andrew, Elon Musk, and numerous other public figures. The quality of evidence varies enormously across these cases: some are supported by flight logs, deposition testimony, or witness accounts; others rest on nothing more than association or speculation. Snopes' "Unfounded" rating for the Smith claim is appropriate given the available evidence, but the article would benefit from acknowledgment that not all Epstein-related claims are equally unsupported, and that distinguishing between them requires more than a single rating category.


## 6. Verdict

**Classification**: **Distorted but Grounded — Unsupported**

The claim that Sam Smith has a documented "connection" to Jeffrey Epstein's island is unsupported by any verifiable evidence. The Drama Bay video that propagated the claim operates under an explicit disclaimer that its content may not be factual. Snopes' "Unfounded" rating is appropriate given the available evidence.

**What Snopes gets right:**
- The Drama Bay video offers no verifiable evidence of a connection between Smith and Epstein
- The channel's own disclaimer indicates that its content may not be accurate
- The video has been cross-posted to TikTok and X, generating significant engagement without evidentiary basis
- The misgendering of Smith in the headline is correctly noted
- The rating appropriately reflects the evidentiary vacuum

**What Snopes misses or underplays:**
- The platform economics and algorithmic incentives that drive Epstein-related rumor content
- The structural pattern of which the Drama Bay video is one instance, not an isolated aberration
- The distinction between groundless rumors and emerging investigative reporting in the Epstein case
- The exploitation of anti-LGBTQ prejudice through the conflation of nonbinary identity with moral corruption
- The genuine public information deficit that creates demand for Epstein-related content
- The epistemological asymmetry between positive claims (can be supported with evidence) and negative claims (cannot be definitively disproven)

The Sam Smith rumor is ultimately more revealing about the structure of the post-Epstein information ecosystem than about any claim about Smith or Epstein. The public appetite for information about Epstein's network is driven by legitimate concerns about justice, accountability, and the relationship between wealth and criminality. That this appetite is exploited by content creators who offer speculation without evidence is a genuine problem. But dismissing the appetite itself as mere credulity toward rumors ignores the underlying information deficit. Fact-checkers, platforms, and journalists all have roles to play in addressing this deficit, and no single fact check can substitute for the kind of sustained investigative reporting that is most effective at displacing rumor with reliable information.
