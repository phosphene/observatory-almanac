# Why Did Thousands of Sand Dollars Wash Ashore in Oregon?


## 1. The Claim

In mid-August 2021, photographs and video footage of thousands of sand dollars clustered along the high-tide line of Seaside Beach on Oregon's northern coast began circulating widely on social media, after Oregon's Seaside Aquarium shared images on Facebook on August 15. The images showed dense accumulations of the distinctive disc-shaped marine invertebrates — some still bearing their characteristic purple and greenish cilia, indicating they were alive, others already bleached white in the summer sun. The viral spread was rapid, with coverage appearing in local outlets such as *The Oregonian* and Oregon Public Broadcasting, and international publications including *The Daily Mail* and Yahoo News.

The implicit claim embedded in viral sharing of these images was one of alarm: that a mass die-off of marine wildlife had occurred on the American Pacific coast, that something was clearly wrong with the ocean, and that the event might be connected to broader environmental crises — climate change, ocean pollution, or human industrial activity. Some posts framed the event as mysterious and unprecedented. Others connected it explicitly to large-scale environmental damage. A smaller portion of commentary asked the genuinely scientific question: what actually causes an event like this?

The question, once stripped of its more sensationalized framings, is an entirely legitimate and scientifically interesting one. Sand dollars are not creatures that typically walk off their sandy seafloor habitats onto beaches; their normal mode of existence is sessile and subtidal, tucked into soft sandy sediment in nearshore or deeper waters. A mass stranding event, particularly one involving adults rather than juveniles, signals that some disruption forced these animals out of their beds and into conditions they could not survive. The event thus sits at the intersection of invertebrate ecology, oceanography, and the complex challenge of interpreting animal die-offs in a rapidly changing marine environment.

The Snopes article on this event, written by Madison Dapcevich and published on August 19, 2021, focused on providing scientific context for what could have caused the stranding, consulting directly with an expert in invertebrate zoology. The article is classified as explanatory rather than fact-checking a false claim — it functions as a science explainer for a genuine, non-fabricated event.

---

## 2. The Verdict

The Oregon sand dollar mass stranding event of August 2021 was real. The photographs and video footage circulating on social media were authentic, sourced from the Seaside Aquarium, a reputable local institution. The scale of the event — thousands of individuals washed ashore — was documented by aquarium staff and confirmed by eyewitness reports.

No single definitive cause was ever publicly identified, which is itself an important and scientifically honest finding. Christina Piotrowski, collections manager of invertebrate zoology at the California Academy of Sciences, consulted with Snopes on the event and concluded that "without information on current offshore water conditions, it is difficult to determine exactly what contributed to the mass die-off." This epistemic humility about cause is appropriate: marine mass stranding events routinely resist monocausal explanations, and the interpretive challenge is compounded when they occur in open-water settings rather than confined bays or estuaries where water quality data is more readily available.

The event should not be interpreted as straightforwardly unprecedented, though its scale and the apparent maturity of the animals involved made it noteworthy. Several comparable events have occurred along other North American coastlines in the modern record, and the factors that could have caused the Oregon event — harmful algal blooms, anoxic bottom conditions, extreme storm surge, current disruption — are not exotic mechanisms but known drivers of marine invertebrate mortality. What distinguished the Oregon event was its scale, its location on the U.S. West Coast (unusually for this species), and the visibility generated by the aquarium's social media documentation.

## 3. The Science: Sandy Beds, Echinoderm Ecology, and Mass Mortality Events

The species involved in the Oregon stranding was *Dendraster excentricus*, the Pacific sand dollar — an echinoderm found along the western coast of North America from Alaska to Baja California, Mexico. Understanding why such events occur requires familiarity with the biology and ecology of this species and the broader context of mass mortality events in marine invertebrates.

*D. excentricus* is a flattened cousin of sea urchins (order Clypeasteroida), distinguished by its almost completely disc-shaped calcium carbonate test — the hard external skeleton — which is covered in millions of hair-like projections called cilia. These cilia serve dual purposes: locomotion across the sandy seafloor and filtration of food particles from the water column. When alive, the cilia are dense and give the animal a velvety, often purple or greenish appearance. After death, the cilia are shed rapidly and the bleached white "sand dollar" of beach combing emerges. The animal is "sand dollar" vernacular in North America; in the United Kingdom the same organisms are called "sea biscuits" or "snapper biscuits."

*D. excentricus* lives primarily in shallow to moderately deep subtidal zones, typically at depths of 3 to 30 metres, in sediments with high sand content. They often form extraordinarily dense beds — populations of hundreds or thousands of individuals per square metre have been recorded — which creates ecological efficiency in terms of filter feeding but also makes the entire population simultaneously vulnerable to any perturbation of local conditions. Piotrowski described this explicitly: "D. excentricus beds are inherently susceptible to both localized land-based activity and large-scale oceanic conditions."

**Epistemic novelty:** A key finding in the scientific literature that is frequently missed in popular accounts of sand dollar strandings is that young sand dollars of *D. excentricus* exhibit a remarkable behavioural adaptation to avoid displacement: they actively ingest sand particles, with a documented preference for iron-heavy grains, specifically as ballast to stabilise themselves in the sediment against strong currents (Telford et al., 1985, *Journal of Experimental Marine Biology and Ecology*). This means the animals are not simply passive inhabitants of their habitat but active managers of their own weight and position. An event that overcomes this ballasting behaviour — displacing adult individuals from their beds en masse — therefore implies forces unusually strong or rapid relative to what the animals can compensate for behaviourally.

This matters epistically because it shifts the framing of mass strandings away from mysterious catastrophe and toward a more tractable ecophysiological question: what conditions exceed the behavioural and physiological tolerances of a species that is, under normal circumstances, well-adapted to variable sandy seabed environments?

Several candidate mechanisms were identified by Piotrowski as potentially sufficient:

**Harmful Algal Blooms (HABs):** Excessive nutrient loading — particularly from agricultural runoff entering coastal river systems, as would enter the Oregon coast via the Columbia River — can trigger explosive growth of phytoplankton, some species of which produce toxins or can create anoxic conditions as they decompose. The Oregon coast was already implicated in HAB activity in 2021; published spatial analyses of the "warm blob" phenomenon in the northeastern Pacific documented how the marine heatwave of 2013–2015 set the stage for an exceptional HAB event that reached from southern Oregon to California, with a later paper by McCabe et al. (2016, *Geophysical Research Letters*) and follow-up synthesis work by Anderson et al. (2021, *Frontiers in Climate*) specifically linking warm-blob conditions to the most toxic and geographically extensive harmful algal event on the U.S. West Coast in the historical record, including mass mortality events across multiple species in 2011.

**Anoxic seafloor conditions:** When large algal blooms collapse, the bacterial decomposition of organic matter consumes dissolved oxygen, creating "dead zones" on the seafloor where oxygen levels drop below what benthic invertebrates require for survival. Sand dollars, living directly in the sediment at the seafloor interface, are particularly exposed to such anoxic episodes. Hypoxia and anoxia have been increasingly documented off the Oregon coast as a consequence of both natural upwelling dynamics and anthropogenic eutrophication; Chan et al. (2008, *Science*) documented recurring shelf hypoxia events in the northern California Current System, including waters adjacent to Oregon, with bottom-water oxygen levels sufficient to cause invertebrate die-offs across significant seafloor areas.

**Ocean acidification:** Lower pH values in Pacific Ocean surface and subsurface waters reduce the availability of carbonate ions that organisms like sand dollars require to build and maintain their calcium carbonate tests. Research on *D. excentricus* larvae specifically examined by O'Donnell et al. (2010, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*) found that larvae raised under elevated pCO₂ (simulating projected ocean acidification conditions) showed significantly reduced calcification and survival rates. Piotrowski appropriately noted that ocean acidification was "unlikely to be related to the 2021 die-off," since acidification affects calcification and larval development rather than producing acute adult mortality events, but its interaction with other stressors remains an area of active research.

**Physical disturbance:** High wave action, unusually strong bottom currents, or storm surge preceding the stranding event could have physically dislodged sand dollars from their beds and swept them shoreward. The timing of the event — mid-August, during summer calm typically along the Oregon coast — somewhat weakens this explanation, but unusual upwelling events or subsurface current anomalies can generate bottom disturbance independent of surface wave conditions.

## 4. What the Evidence Shows

Several features of the Oregon event constrain interpretation. First, the stranding appeared to be concentrated along the afternoon high-tide line, as the Seaside Aquarium documented, with live animals still present when the tide receded — suggesting the animals were being carried toward shore by tidal and/or current action rather than arriving already dead and decomposed. This implies an acute transport mechanism rather than a slow die-off in situ.

Second, and importantly, the animals involved appeared to be adults — sexually mature individuals. Piotrowski specifically flagged this as significant: "What's perplexing about the Oregon die-off is that many of the sand dollars appear to be sexually mature or adult individuals, which suggests that perhaps the event was caused by something out of the ordinary cycle." This distinguishes the Oregon event from the analogous 2016 stranding of juvenile sand dollars in southwestern Florida, where a particularly large spawning year produced a correspondingly large cohort of recently settled juveniles with high natural mortality — a more readily explicable phenomenon.

Third, the same coastline is situated within a broader region experiencing documented environmental change. The Washington state shellfish closures in effect at the time due to marine biotoxins from harmful phytoplankton, noted by Snopes, indicate that harmful algal activity was already actively affecting the region. The spatiotemporal proximity of HAB-related shellfish closures to the sand dollar stranding event does not establish causation but is consistent with a hypothesis involving algal toxins or anoxic conditions as a contributing factor.

The scientific literature on *Dendraster excentricus* population dynamics is informative here: the species has a relatively long lifespan for a sand dollar — approximately ten years, based on growth ring analyses in the pioneering work by Ebert & Dexter (1975, *Marine Biology*) — and regular adult die-offs at unusual scales may impair population recovery across reproductive cycles. This means events like the Oregon stranding are not merely visually striking but potentially ecologically consequential.

## 5. Gaps and Uncertainties

The most significant gap is the absence of a definitive causal determination for the Oregon event. Piotrowski was explicit: "I'm afraid that without further study of local sea conditions we won't be able to know what happened." No follow-up published investigation of water chemistry, toxicological analysis of stranded animals, or remote sensing survey of benthic conditions at the time of the stranding was made publicly available in the scientific literature in the period following the event. This is not unusual — acute, localised marine invertebrate die-off events rarely attract the resources for full post-event environmental forensics — but it means the event remains an unresolved observation rather than an explained phenomenon.

The broader baseline monitoring situation also represents a gap: while NOAA's Pacific Coast environmental monitoring programs capture broad hydrographic patterns, very fine-scale data on the specific conditions of nearshore subtidal sand dollar beds off Seaside, Oregon on the dates in question remain unavailable in open-access records. Such monitoring gaps are common for benthic invertebrates, which receive less scientific attention than commercially important species despite their important ecological roles.

There is also uncertainty about the frequency of comparable historical events. Mass strandings are likely underreported except when they occur in proximity to populated areas, scientific monitoring stations, or visually striking enough to be photographed and shared. The Seaside Aquarium's active social media presence transformed a local marine event into a global news story; countless comparable events in more remote locations may have gone unrecorded.

---

## 6. Why This Matters

The Oregon sand dollar stranding illuminates a broader class of epistemic challenge in environmental journalism and science communication: acute, visually striking marine events generate intense short-term media interest but are rarely followed by the kind of systematic investigation that would transform them into scientific knowledge. The viral moment is well-documented; the underlying mechanism is not. This asymmetry between media attention and scientific follow-through is structurally common in environmental reporting.

Sand dollars serve as more than striking beach objects. As filter feeders and grazers of zooplankton, diatoms, and organic detritus in the water column, *D. excentricus* populations occupy a meaningful functional position in nearshore Pacific ecosystems. Piotrowski articulated this concisely: they "not only process organic matter along the seafloor, but also act as primary predators of zooplankton, and grazers on algae and diatoms." A significant reduction in adult population density, whether from acute die-offs or chronic background mortality driven by ocean warming and acidification, could have measurable downstream effects on the trophic structure of the communities they inhabit.

The event also illustrates the limitation of the "mystery" frame that media coverage often deploys for unexplained natural events. The Oregon stranding was not mysterious in the sense that no relevant explanatory framework existed; ecologists understand the types of conditions that displace and kill benthic invertebrates. It was uncertain in a more specific and scientifically interesting sense: multiple plausible mechanisms were known, but the data to discriminate between them were unavailable. That distinction — between genuine scientific mystery and data-limited uncertainty within an established explanatory framework — is rarely made clearly in popular coverage and is worth preserving.
