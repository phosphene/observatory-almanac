## The Claim

On April 7, 2025, the American biotechnology company Colossal Biosciences announced that it had successfully "de-extincted" the dire wolf (*Aenocyon dirus*), a large canid species that vanished from North America more than ten thousand years ago. The announcement was covered by *Time* magazine — which ran the story as a cover feature — the *Washington Post*, the Associated Press, Smithsonian Magazine, and dozens of other news outlets. Three pups named Romulus, Remus, and Khaleesi were presented as the world's first returned specimens of an extinct species.

The response from the broader scientific community was immediate and largely hostile to the "de-extinction" framing. Palaeontologists, geneticists, and evolutionary biologists took to social media, institutional press, and scientific publications to dispute the claim that these animals were dire wolves, calling them instead "genetically modified gray wolves," "optimistically 1/100,000th dire wolf," and "animals tweaked to resemble extinct features." One paleontologist stated flatly: "This is not a dire wolf. They cannot do de-extinction. They can just tweak living animals to have primitive features."

The claims in circulation thus had two layers. The first, from Colossal: dire wolves have been de-extincted; living members of the species exist. The second, from sceptical scientists: these are not dire wolves; the "de-extinction" framing is scientifically misleading. Snopes asked whether both the science and the framing were accurate, concluding that the science was real and impressive while the "de-extinction" label was contested.

This entry examines the scientific substance, the terminological controversy, and the broader implications for how biotechnology companies communicate with the public.

## What's Actually True

**The scientific work is genuine and significant.** Colossal Biosciences extracted ancient DNA from two dire wolf fossils, one of which was more than 72,000 years old. Recovering usable genetic sequence from material of this age is a substantial technical achievement; ancient DNA degrades rapidly through oxidative and hydrolytic damage, and extraction protocols for Pleistocene-era specimens require extensive contamination-control procedures and sophisticated computational reconstruction methods. The dire wolf genome assembled by Colossal was reportedly more complete than the sequence used in the influential 2021 *Nature* study (Perri et al., 2021) that first established the dire wolf's genetic distinctiveness from the grey wolf lineage.

From that reconstructed genome, Colossal identified 14 genes responsible for features distinguishing dire wolves from their closest living relatives — primarily traits related to coat colour (producing the white fur visible in the pups' photographs), body size, and musculature. The company then used CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing technology to introduce modified versions of these 14 genes into blood vessel cells harvested from living gray wolves. The modified cellular DNA was then used to create embryos, which were implanted into surrogate dog mothers and carried to term. The three pups born from this process are — by any measure — living animals, in apparent good health at the time of announcement, and they carry genetic modifications derived from ancient dire wolf sequence data.

**The lineage question is genuinely contested.** The choice to use gray wolf (*Canis lupus*) genomes as the base for the editing process rests on a claim about dire wolf phylogeny that is itself disputed. Perri et al. (2021), published in *Nature*, compared dire wolf DNA from fossil specimens against seven living canid species and concluded that dire wolves had a "distant evolutionary relationship" with all of them, diverging from the lineage leading to modern canids approximately 5.7 million years ago. Notably, that study suggested the dire wolf was no more closely related to the gray wolf than to the golden jackal.

Colossal's own pre-print study — unpublished at peer review at the time of the announcement — uses a larger and reportedly more complete dire wolf genome and reaches a different conclusion: that while the initial lineage split occurred around the same time (~5.7 million years ago), subsequent gene flow between dire wolf ancestors and the ancestors of gray wolves and coyotes means that, when all genome-wide ancestry is summed, gray wolves are somewhat more closely related to dire wolves than jackals are. Colossal Chief Science Officer Beth Shapiro described this as the consequence of "extensive interbreeding" between lineages "deep in evolutionary time."

This distinction matters because it determines the legitimacy of using the gray wolf genome as the reconstruction target. Colossal's methodological choice is defensible under the interpretation generated by their own genomic analysis. It remains contested science, however, because the pre-print had not passed peer review at the time of announcement, and independent researchers had not yet been able to evaluate the methodology and data.

**The "de-extinction" definition is bespoke.** The most significant issue with Colossal's announcement is not the science itself but the terminological framework used to communicate it. Colossal's website explicitly acknowledges that it uses "de-extinction" differently from the conventional definition, which the company cites from Wikipedia as "the process of generating an organism that either resembles or is an extinct species." Colossal proposed what it called "functional de-extinction": an organism that both resembles and is *genetically similar* to an extinct species through resurrection of core genes, engineering of natural resistances, and enhancement of adaptability.

Under this definition, an animal is de-extinct if it looks like the original and shares some genetic material with it. Strict genetic identity — the reconstruction of an organism with the complete, original genome of an extinct species — is explicitly excluded from the requirement. This is an important revision: the 14 genes modified in the pups represent roughly 0.003% of the approximately 20,000 protein-coding genes in the mammalian genome. A gray wolf and a dire wolf were already substantially more similar to each other than to most mammals; the modifications introduced by Colossal represent a small increment of additional similarity in a specific directionally curated set of traits.

The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) noted in a 2016 report (IUCN SSC, 2016) that "the term 'de-extinction' is misleading in its implication that extinct species, species for which no viable members remain, can be resurrected in their genetic, behavioural and physiological entirety." Colossal's definition effectively acknowledges this critique from the IUCN while reframing it as a feature rather than a limitation — arguing that functional similarity, rather than complete genetic restoration, is the meaningful metric for conservation purposes.

## Why the Terminology Controversy Matters

The dispute over "de-extinction" is not merely a semantic quibble among scientists occupying different terminological territories. It tracks onto substantive disagreements about what biotechnology can and cannot do, how its capabilities should be communicated, and what responsibilities commercial entities bear when marketing to public audiences.

**The species problem.** What counts as a "dire wolf" is not a question with a single answer derivable from the genomes. Species concepts in biology are plural and contested: the biological species concept (reproductive isolation), the phylogenetic species concept (monophyletic lineage from a common ancestor), the ecological species concept (occupancy of a distinct ecological niche), and others generate overlapping but non-identical species designations for many groups (Mayden, 1997, *Molecular Phylogenetics and Evolution*). Colossal's pups are grey wolves with modified coat colour, musculature, and body size genes. Whether that makes them "dire wolves" depends on which species concept you apply and how you weight phenotypic versus genotypic similarity.

The pups look considerably whiter and reportedly larger than typical grey wolves (though at the time of announcement they were still young pups and adult size comparison was unavailable). By a phenotypic criterion focused on certain visible features, they do partially resemble what we know about dire wolf appearance from fossil evidence and palaeontological reconstruction. By a genotypic criterion, they remain overwhelmingly gray wolf, with 14 edited genes among tens of thousands.

**The ecological question.** Colossal's stated aspiration is not merely to create animals that resemble extinct species but to restore ecological roles — to reintroduce "functional replacements" into ecosystems. This ecological framing — the idea that the pups might eventually "return the dire wolf to its rightful place in the ecosystem" — generates additional scientific scepticism. Dire wolves occupied a specific ecological position as large apex predators in Pleistocene North America, co-evolving with prey species (giant ground sloths, ancient horses, mastodons) that are themselves extinct. The ecosystem they help regulate no longer exists. What "returning the dire wolf to the ecosystem" would mean in 21st-century North America, where neither the prey base nor the competitive guild the dire wolf inhabited remains intact, is not addressed adequately in Colossal's communications.

**The commercial context.** Colossal Biosciences is a private company with investors. It has raised substantial capital on the basis of promises about de-extinction, and the April 2025 announcement was timed to coincide with media coverage at a scale that included *Time* magazine covers. The company's commercial interests are aligned with maximising the perceived significance of its announcements — not with producing maximally conservative characterisations of its scientific achievements. This does not mean the science is fraudulent. It does mean that the choice of "de-extinction" over "genetic modification guided by ancient DNA" reflects brand management as much as scientific communication. Understanding the announcement requires reading it through both lenses simultaneously.

Researchers unaffiliated with Colossal were notably not available for comment to Snopes at time of publication (Izzo, 2025), which itself reflects the dynamics of embargoed announcements: because the story was timed and coordinated with a media rollout, independent scientific voices were initially limited to rapid-response commentary on social media rather than considered expert assessment through formal press channels.

## Epistemic Novelty: The Paleogenomics Revolution and Its Limits

The Colossal announcement sits at the intersection of two genuinely revolutionary scientific developments that have occurred in the past decade: ancient DNA analysis and CRISPR genome editing. Understanding the announcement correctly requires understanding what each field has made possible and where each currently runs into hard limits.

**Ancient DNA paleogenomics** has transformed evolutionary biology and archaeology since the mid-2000s, with methodological advances enabling recovery of useful genetic sequence from increasingly degraded fossil material. The Nobel-winning work of Svante Pääbo and colleagues at the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology demonstrated that Neanderthal and Denisovan genomes could be recovered from specimens tens of thousands of years old (Pääbo, 2014, *Neanderthal Man*, Basic Books; Green et al., 2010, *Science*). Subsequent work by Eske Willerslev's group and others pushed the temporal limit further, recovering sedimentary ancient DNA from deposits more than 100,000 years old (Willerslev et al., 2003, *Nature*). The dire wolf fossils used by Colossal were well within the range of documented ancient DNA recovery.

However, recovering sequence fragments is not the same as having a complete, fully resolved genome. Ancient DNA consists of short, damaged fragments that must be computationally assembled relative to a reference genome — typically a close living relative. The reference genome used shapes which features of the ancient genome can be identified and how confidently they can be assigned. For a lineage as divergent as the dire wolf, where the reference genome is a grey wolf with divergence time potentially exceeding 5 million years, there are significant limits to resolution. Regions of the genome that differ substantially between the two species are precisely the hardest to assemble accurately from ancient fragments, because there is no good reference to align them against. This means the most uniquely "dire wolf" parts of the genome may be exactly the parts most poorly represented in Colossal's reconstruction.

**CRISPR gene editing** is a genuinely transformative technology that has enabled precise, targeted modification of genomic sequence at specific locations. Its applications in agriculture, medicine, and basic research have been extensive since its adaptation for genome editing by Doudna and Charpentier (2012, *Science*). CRISPR allows scientists to introduce specific sequence changes at defined genomic coordinates with a precision and efficiency previously impossible.

However, CRISPR-based gene editing of 14 genes cannot introduce the full complexity of traits that made dire wolves distinct from grey wolves. Phenotypic traits — including coat colour, size, musculature, behaviour, physiology — are polygenic: they result from the combined effects of many genes operating in developmental context, interacting with each other and with environmental inputs. Modifying 14 genes changes 14 specific molecular processes; it does not reconstruct the full developmental programme that produced the dire wolf's distinctive phenotype. The pups' white coats and modified bodies are the outputs of those 14 targeted interventions, not of the reconstruction of the dire wolf's complete developmental genetics.

This is the source of the "1/100,000th dire wolf" critique: the fraction refers not to the percentage of genes modified but to the approximate proportion of genomic difference between grey wolves and dire wolves that those 14 genes address. It is a rhetorically sharp way of making the scientific point that the modifications, while technically precise and genuinely guided by ancient DNA evidence, are a small perturbation on a baseline that remains overwhelmingly grey wolf.

## Broader Context: What De-extinction Discourse Does

The Colossal announcement generated public excitement that substantially exceeded what objective assessment of the science would warrant, and understanding why this gap exists tells us something important about how de-extinction as a concept operates in contemporary culture.

De-extinction carries a narrative weight that transcends the specific biotechnology involved. It frames biotechnology as a reversal of biological loss, an undoing of extinction, a recovery of something that should not have been lost. This narrative resonates deeply with contemporary environmental consciousness, which is acutely aware of accelerating biodiversity loss and carries a significant grief dimension. The suggestion that extinction might be undoable is emotionally powerful because extinction is experienced as permanent, irreversible tragedy. "De-extinction" promises a technology of reversal for the most irreversible of biological processes.

This narrative power is precisely what makes the terminology choice consequential. If Colossal had announced "genetically modified grey wolves with dire wolf aesthetic features," the announcement would have been scientifically accurate and still genuinely impressive — the paleogenomic work alone is remarkable — but it would not have generated *Time* covers or the level of public excitement that accompanied the announcement. The "de-extinction" label transformed a biotechnology achievement into a category of ontological reversal. That transformation is marketing, not science.

The conservation biology community has warned about this dynamic for years. Researchers including Minteer, Collins, and others (Minteer et al., 2014, *Science*) have argued that de-extinction discourse risks redirecting conservation attention and funding toward spectacular resurrection projects and away from the protection of currently living threatened species that are less glamorous but more achievable to conserve. The concern is not that the technology is without value — Colossal's red wolf conservation work is cited even by its critics as genuinely useful — but that the framing of de-extinction as a solution to biodiversity loss may generate moral hazard by suggesting that extinction is less permanent than it is.

---

## Verdict

**The science is real; the label is contested.** Colossal Biosciences accomplished something genuinely impressive in April 2025: the extraction of workable ancient DNA from a >72,000-year-old dire wolf fossil, the identification of genomic features distinguishing dire wolves from grey wolves, and the use of CRISPR technology to produce living animals incorporating those features. The three pups are alive and apparently healthy.

Whether those animals are "dire wolves" or "de-extinct" depends on which species concept is applied and which definition of "de-extinction" is used. Under Colossal's own bespoke definition — functional de-extinction as genetic similarity plus phenotypic resemblance — the company's claim is internally consistent. Under the definitions used by most paleontologists, geneticists, and conservation biologists, the pups are genetically modified grey wolves with phenotypic modifications guided by dire wolf DNA. The distinction matters because it affects what the public understands about what the technology can and cannot do, about what extinction means, and about what it would mean to genuinely reverse it.

Snopes accurately characterised the situation as "part cool science, part flashy marketing." Both parts are real.
