# Scientists understood the physics of climate change in the 1800s, thanks to Eunice Foote


## 1. The Claim

The claim circulating in science communication, climate journalism, and social media holds that an American scientist named Eunice Newton Foote discovered the greenhouse effect in 1856 — three years before John Tyndall, the Irish physicist who is conventionally credited with the discovery. The fuller version of the claim asserts that scientists understood the basic physics of human-caused climate change as early as the mid-nineteenth century, and that Foote's contribution was erased, ignored, or suppressed, largely because she was a woman working in a period when women were structurally excluded from professional scientific institutions.

The claim typically circulates in two contexts. The first is climate communication: advocates wish to establish that the physical basis for anthropogenic climate change is not a recent or contested theory but one with a long and deep scientific pedigree. The second is history-of-science discourse: feminist historians of science and science communicators have worked to restore credit to women scientists whose contributions were systematically overlooked by the institutions of their day. Foote's case has become paradigmatic in both conversations. Her story frequently appears in educational materials, museum exhibits, science journalism, and popular books as evidence that women did serious science long before they were admitted to the formal scientific community, and that climate science has roots stretching back more than 160 years.

The implicit or explicit message is: this is not ideologically motivated modern science — it was established in the 1850s by an amateur experimenter working in upstate New York, and the physics has not changed.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Eunice Foote's scientific work.** Eunice Newton Foote (1819–1888) was an American scientist, inventor, and women's rights activist. She was among the signatories of the Seneca Falls Declaration of Sentiments in 1848, and she held several patents for inventions in vulcanised rubber and elastic polymers during the 1860s. In 1856 she submitted a paper titled "Circumstances affecting the heat of the sun's rays" to the American Association for the Advancement of Science. The paper was presented at the AAAS annual meeting in Albany, New York — though not by Foote herself. According to historical accounts examined by Sorenson (2011, *Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society*), the paper was read aloud by Joseph Henry, the Smithsonian's founding secretary, who also introduced it with a comment acknowledging that it was unusual for scientific work to be presented by a man on behalf of a woman.

The paper was subsequently published in the *American Journal of Science and Arts* in 1856, and it describes a series of experiments in which Foote placed different gases — carbonic acid (CO₂), air of varying moisture levels, and hydrogen — into glass cylinders, exposed them to direct sunlight, and measured their temperature responses. Her results showed that moist air heated more than dry air, and that carbonic acid (CO₂) heated more than both — reaching a temperature of 52°C compared to 35°C for common air in direct sunlight. Critically, she also observed that the CO₂-rich cylinder cooled more slowly than the others when removed from direct sunlight.

From these observations, Foote wrote: "An atmosphere of that gas [carbonic acid] would give to our earth a much higher temperature; and if as some suppose, at one period of its history the air had mixed with it a larger proportion than at present, an increased temperature from its own action as well as from increased weight must have necessarily resulted." This is unambiguously a prediction of greenhouse warming from elevated atmospheric CO₂ — stated in a published scientific paper in 1856.

**John Tyndall's work.** John Tyndall began systematic experiments on the radiative properties of gases in 1859, publishing his foundational results in the *Philosophical Magazine* in 1861 (Tyndall, 1861, *Philosophical Magazine*). Tyndall's experimental apparatus was considerably more sophisticated than Foote's. He used an infrared radiation source — a copper vessel filled with hot water — and measured how different gases absorbed or transmitted heat radiation using a galvanometer connected to a thermopile. His method allowed him to isolate the specific absorption of radiated (thermal) infrared energy by gases, distinct from their capacity to absorb incoming solar radiation. He demonstrated that CO₂, water vapour, methane, and ozone were strong absorbers of terrestrial infrared radiation, while nitrogen and oxygen (the dominant components of air) were nearly transparent to it. This is the mechanism at the core of the greenhouse effect as we now understand it.

There is no documentary evidence that Tyndall knew about Foote's paper before conducting his experiments. The archival record reviewed by Jackson (2019, *Notes and Records: The Royal Society Journal of the History of Science*) found no citation of Foote in Tyndall's published or archived correspondence. The absence of citation may reflect ignorance of her work, the relatively limited circulation of the *American Journal of Science and Arts* in British scientific networks, or the conventions of the period around citation of amateur or non-institutional science. It does not resolve to deliberate suppression, though it does reflect the structural reality that women's scientific contributions in the nineteenth century were less likely to be noticed, cited, and built upon.

**What Foote discovered and what she did not.** Foote's experiment demonstrated that CO₂ absorbs solar heat more efficiently than air and retains it longer. Her conclusion about atmospheric warming from elevated CO₂ was correct and preceded Tyndall's published work by three years. In that specific sense, the claim that she discovered the greenhouse effect before Tyndall is substantially accurate.

However, there are meaningful distinctions between what Foote's experiments established and what Tyndall's established. Foote's glass cylinders measured the heating response of gases to sunlight — a process that includes both direct solar absorption and some degree of trapping of re-emitted radiation within the glass enclosure. Tyndall's apparatus specifically isolated the absorption of thermal infrared radiation emitted by warm bodies, which is the physically precise mechanism responsible for the atmospheric greenhouse effect: the Earth's surface absorbs solar radiation, warms, and re-emits as long-wave infrared, which is then absorbed by greenhouse gases rather than escaped to space. Foote's experiment was not designed to isolate this mechanism and did not demonstrate it. Her conclusions were correct; the physical mechanism she identified was not entirely precise.

This distinction is not a reason to diminish Foote's achievement; it is a reason to understand it accurately. Foote made an empirically grounded prediction from real observations. Tyndall provided the physical mechanism that explained and extended that prediction. Both contributions are genuine and both deserve recognition.


## 3. Why People Believe This (And What It Illuminates)

**The epistemically novel claim.** Most coverage of this story frames Foote's rediscovery as a straightforward correction of a historical oversight — a case of a deserving woman being denied credit by a patriarchal scientific establishment. That framing is not wrong, but it passes over a more interesting epistemic question: *why did Foote's discovery fail to accumulate citations and research in the way that Tyndall's did?*

The answer is not primarily about gender suppression, though gender was certainly a structural factor in the period's scientific organisation. It is more precisely about what climate historian Roland Jackson (2019, *Notes and Records*) describes as the problem of measurement resolution. Foote's experimental method — glass cylinders exposed to sunlight — was not capable of producing the kind of quantitatively precise results that could be built on by subsequent researchers. Her temperature readings were relative and instrument-dependent; her mechanism was ambiguous between solar absorption, convection differences, and infrared trapping; and her experimental design did not isolate the variable that matters most for atmospheric physics, which is the interaction between gases and long-wave thermal radiation. Tyndall's galvanometric method produced results that were reproducible, quantitatively precise, and clearly mechanistically interpretable, which is why they generated a research programme and Foote's did not.

This illuminates a general principle in the history of science: it is not enough to be correct. A discovery that becomes generative — that enables subsequent researchers to extend, refine, and build on the result — requires a combination of correctness, precision, and communicability that Foote's work had only in part. Tyndall's work had all three. This is a story about the sociology of scientific knowledge as much as about gender injustice, though both are genuinely present.

**The climate communication function.** The revival of Foote's story in the 2010s was substantially driven by climate communicators who wanted to counter the framing of anthropogenic climate change as a recent, contested, or politically motivated theory. Demonstrating that the basic physical principle — CO₂ trapping heat — was established by empirical experiment in 1856 and corroborated by Tyndall in 1861 is strategically useful in this debate. It places climate science in the category of long-established 19th-century physics, comparable in status to thermodynamics or electromagnetism, rather than in the category of recent modelling exercises that might plausibly be revised. This is not an inaccurate characterisation — the greenhouse mechanism is genuinely well-established — but the deployment of Foote's story in this argument sometimes emphasises the cultural drama of her rediscovery at the expense of accurate scientific genealogy.

**The rediscovery narrative.** Foote's paper was essentially invisible in the scientific literature for 155 years. Raymond Sorenson, a retired oil geologist, located it while researching the history of climate science and published his findings in the *Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society* in 2011 (Sorenson, 2011). Katharine Hayhoe and other science communicators then amplified the story significantly in the mid-2010s, and it entered the mainstream of popular science writing. The rediscovery narrative has a powerful emotional resonance — the vindication of a figure erased by institutional sexism — that makes it particularly shareable and memorable. But the emotional force of the rediscovery can cause communicators to overstate the claim, presenting Foote as having discovered everything Tyndall discovered rather than as a genuine but limited predecessor.


## 4. Verdict

**True with important nuance.** Eunice Newton Foote published a genuine scientific paper in 1856, based on real laboratory observations, that correctly identified CO₂ as a gas that absorbs more heat than common air, and correctly predicted that an atmosphere with elevated CO₂ would produce a warmer Earth. She deserves credit as an early discoverer of the greenhouse warming principle. The claim is substantially true.

The nuances that matter are: (a) Foote's experiment measured solar heating of enclosed gas, not the infrared absorption that is the precise mechanism of the greenhouse effect; (b) Tyndall's 1859–1861 work established the mechanism with greater rigour, precision, and mechanistic clarity; (c) there is no evidence Tyndall knew about Foote's work; (d) the failure of Foote's work to generate a research programme reflects both the structural exclusion of women from scientific institutions in the period and the limited technical precision of her experimental method. The claim is not false; it is best understood as accurate with important context about what type of discovery she made and how it relates to Tyndall's contribution.


## 5. The Wider Picture

Foote's story sits within a flourishing field of work recovering women's contributions to the history of science. Rachel Ignotofsky's popular book *Women in Science* (2016) included Foote; Roland Jackson's biography of Tyndall (2018, *John Tyndall: Darkness and Light*) addressed the question of priority carefully; and multiple museum installations and educational curricula have included Foote since approximately 2016. The historiographic project here is legitimate and important. Women were structurally excluded from scientific societies, publication networks, and university appointments throughout the nineteenth and well into the twentieth century. The consequence is that women who did serious scientific work were more likely to be forgotten, cited without credit, or omitted from canonical histories than men doing equivalent work.

The Foote case also touches on the broader question of when scientific understanding of climate change became available to policymakers and, by extension, whether there is a meaningful sense in which the petroleum industry and governments had reason to act on climate knowledge far earlier than the twentieth century. Climate historian Naomi Oreskes (2004, *Science*) and colleagues have documented the development of consensus understanding of anthropogenic warming through the twentieth century; the Foote/Tyndall baseline establishes that the foundational physics was available in principle from the 1850s, though the connection between that physics and projections for the Earth's actual atmospheric composition under industrial emissions required additional scientific infrastructure — atmospheric CO₂ measurement, the development of climate modelling, and systematic temperature record analysis — that was not in place until the twentieth century.

The claim that "scientists understood the physics of climate change in the 1800s" is thus accurate, but the word "understood" needs unpacking. The greenhouse mechanism was identified and experimentally demonstrated. Application of that mechanism to predict future climate trajectories from industrial emissions required a much larger scientific programme, which unfolded over the following century.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Eunice Foote claim is unusual among fact-check subjects because it is a historically true claim whose significance can be both overstated and understated, and which appears primarily in contexts where it supports a broader argumentative purpose rather than as an isolated factual assertion. Major fact-checking platforms including Snopes have not formally addressed it as of this writing, partly because the core claim — that she published a paper demonstrating CO₂'s heat-trapping properties in 1856 — is straightforwardly verifiable from the historical record and is not seriously disputed.

The more interesting fact-check work involves examining exactly what Foote discovered, how it relates to Tyndall's work, and whether the framing of her achievement in any given communication is proportionate to the evidence. This requires the kind of scientific and historical contextual knowledge that headline true/false ratings do not easily accommodate.

For media literacy purposes, this case illustrates the importance of distinguishing between a claim being essentially true and the significance assigned to it being accurate. "Foote discovered the greenhouse effect" is approximately true; "Foote's discovery proves climate science has been settled for 170 years in exactly the same form as today" is a stronger claim that requires more careful scrutiny. The historical evidence supports the former claim well and the latter only in qualified form. Good fact-checking in this domain should aim to convey the distinction, even when the headline verdict is true.
