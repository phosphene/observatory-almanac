## §1 — Claim and Verdict

The claim that circulated widely on social media throughout late 2021 was deceptively simple: the house in which Wes Craven's 1996 slasher film *Scream* was filmed could be booked on Airbnb for Halloween night. The claim was partially accurate at the time it spread, but carries an important time-locked rider that most social media posts stripped away. Snopes rated it **Outdated**, a designation the platform reserves for claims that were once true but have since ceased to apply — the offer existed for a brief promotional window in 2021, but no equivalent promotion ran in 2022 or in subsequent years as of the writing of this entry (Palma, Bethania. "Is It Really Possible To Book the 'Scream' House on Halloween?" *Snopes.com*, 2 Oct. 2021, updated 3 Oct. 2022).

The verdict deserves unpacking precisely because "Outdated" occupies uneasy epistemic ground. It is neither "True" nor "False" in the binary sense typical of most fact-checks. Rather, it points to the lifecycle problem inherent in claims about limited-time commercial promotions. Once the promotional window closes, the same words — "you can book the Scream house on Airbnb" — shift from accurate to inaccurate without any speaker intending deception. The claim did not mutate; the world did. This lifecycle characteristic is relevant to how the misinformation spread in 2022 and beyond: older, accurate posts were reshared into a context that had changed, and the original temporal marker ("in 2021") was lost in the re-circulation.

## §2 — Primary Evidence and Source Audit

### The Physical Property

The house used for exterior filming in *Scream* (1996) is a private residence located in the unincorporated community of **Tomales, Marin County, California**, approximately 70 miles north of San Francisco. It serves as the fictional "Stu Macher house" in the film — the site of the climactic third-act scene in which Sidney Prescott confronts the Ghostface killers. The town of Tomales is quiet, coastal, and deeply incongruous with its horror-movie fame, a contrast that has long attracted location scouts seeking uncanny domesticity.

### The 2021 Airbnb Promotion

In September 2021, Airbnb listed the property under the host identity of Dewey Riley — a meta-fictional conceit in which the character (rather than the actual property owner) is cast as the host. The listing was designed to advertise two distinct commercial interests simultaneously:

1. The **25th anniversary** of the original *Scream* (1996).
2. The forthcoming **2022 sequel**, later released as *Scream* (2022), directed by Matt Bettinelli-Olpin and Tyler Gillett and produced by Paramount Pictures and Spyglass Media Group.

Three individual one-night stays were made available: **October 27, October 29, and October 31, 2021**. The price per stay was set at **$5** — a nominal sum chosen for symbolic resonance with a horror franchise rather than any meaningful commercial exchange value. Bookings opened on **October 12, 2021**, at 1:00 p.m. EDT.

According to the listing, guests would receive:
- A virtual check-in greeting from David Arquette, reprising his role as Dewey Riley.
- 1990s-era snack offerings including Jiffy Pop popcorn and Reddi-wip.
- A dedicated phone line described as enabling guests to "reach Ghostface directly."
- Memorabilia including DVDs and Woodsboro High paraphernalia from the fictional setting of the film.

The availability was strikingly limited — three nights total. Despite the listing's assurance that "it isn't a contest," the practical effect of offering three nights at $5 to a global Airbnb user base meant effective scarcity indistinguishable from a lottery. Bricken, Rob. "You Can Theoretically Book the Scream House for a Halloween AirBnb." *Gizmodo*, 29 Sept. 2021.

### The 2022 Update

No equivalent promotion was run in conjunction with the 2022 film's theatrical release. When Palma revisited the fact-check in October 2022, she updated the rating from the original assessment to **Outdated**, noting the absence of any new listing. This is the only documented Airbnb promotion tied to a *Scream* property, so subsequent viral recirculations of the 2021 posting constituted the transmission of outdated information.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: Horror-Franchise Hospitality as Parasocial Commerce

The *Scream* house Airbnb promotion illustrates a largely unstudied commercial phenomenon that might be termed **fandom-activated heritage rentals** — cases in which a private residential property temporarily enters the experiential economy not through the conventional mechanisms of vacation rental (luxury, location, amenity) but through its indexical relationship to a culturally resonant piece of fiction.

This is epistemically novel for several intersecting reasons.

**First, value here is purely semiotic.** The property itself offers nothing a conventional Marin County rental would not. There are no on-site film artifacts, no original set dressings, no immersive theatrical infrastructure. The value is the *address* — the fact that camera lenses once aimed at these walls and recorded images that entered the collective visual memory of millions of viewers. Semiotic value of this kind is theorized in Barthes's distinction between *denotation* and *connotation* (Barthes, Roland. *Mythologies*. Seuil, 1957), but the economics of renting a sign rather than a thing have not been rigorously studied at the intersection of media studies and hospitality literature.

**Second, the promotion exploits parasocial architecture.** The fictional framing — Dewey Riley as host, David Arquette as virtual greeter — imports the logic of parasocial relationships (Horton, Donald, and Richard Wohl. "Mass Communication and Para-Social Interaction." *Psychiatry*, vol. 19, no. 3, 1956, pp. 215–229) into a commercial transaction. The guest is not merely renting a room; they are, within the promotional fiction, entering a relationship with a character they have watched onscreen. This collapses the distance between spectator and diegetic world in a way that theme parks approximate but rarely fully close, because theme parks are transparently artificial. The Tomales house, being a real house, carries a different phenomenological register — the actual locations where things (fictionally) happened.

**Third, it illuminates how horror fandom differs from other genre fandom.** Horror tourism as a field — the study of visitors to "dark" sites including crime scenes, disaster locations, and filming locations for horror content — has been explored in the literature on "dark tourism" (Stone, Philip R. "A Dark Tourism Spectrum." *Tourism: An International Interdisciplinary Journal*, vol. 54, no. 2, 2006, pp. 145–160). What is underexplored is the voluntary affective desire to *sleep in* the dark site: to replicate the vulnerability conditions that define the horror genre's appeal. The $5 Airbnb represents the logical terminus of dark-tourism desire — not merely to visit a horror site, but to overnight in it, alone or with a companion, with darkness and Halloween ambiance deliberately activated.

**The epistemic gap** this entry assigns is therefore: the lack of empirical study exploring whether experiential horror hospitality (overnight stays at horror filming locations) produces measurably different psychological effects — including fear arousal, nostalgia activation, or sense of parasocial completion — compared to conventional dark tourism (daytime visits). This is a tractable research question for which no peer-reviewed literature yet exists in either the hospitality management or media psychology literature as of 2026.

## §4 — Contextual Frame: *Scream* (1996) as Meta-Cultural Object

The choice of the *Scream* franchise for this kind of promotion is not arbitrary. Director Wes Craven and writer Kevin Williamson crafted *Scream* as a film explicitly about the cultural scripting of horror — characters within the film cite the rules of slasher movies, creating a recursive relationship between the film's audience and the audience within the film. This meta-textual structure (analyzed extensively in Clover, Carol J. *Men, Women, and Chain Saws: Gender in the Modern Horror Film*. Princeton University Press, 1992) makes *Scream* unusually suited to promotional exercises that blur the line between fiction and reality. The promotion's conceit — a fictional character hosting a real rental — fits seamlessly with the franchise's established aesthetic of genre self-awareness.

Furthermore, the 1996 film arrived at a moment when the slasher genre as a commercially viable form was considered moribund. *Scream* revived it, and its success was inseparable from its referentiality: the film assumed a culturally literate audience that would recognize the horror tropes being deployed and subverted. The same audience segment — educated, genre-fluent, nostalgically attached to 1990s popular culture — constitutes the most plausible Airbnb booking demographic for a promotion like this. The $5 price point was not a revenue strategy; it was an engagement strategy targeting exactly the cohort who would share, screenshot, and discuss the listing on social media regardless of whether they secured a booking.

## §5 — Viral Mechanics and the "Outdated" Verdict

Understanding why the Snopes verdict of *Outdated* matters requires understanding how the claim spread after its shelf life expired.

Social media posts, once published, exist in a kind of archival permanence accessible via search, screenshot, and re-share. The original viral wave in late September and early October 2021 was time-appropriate. However, the same posts — or screenshots of the same Airbnb listing — circulated again in September and October 2022, when fans began discussing the theatrical sequel (released January 14, 2022) and the approach of another Halloween. New users encountered the 2021 posts without temporal context markers and understood them as describing a currently available offer.

This pattern recapitulates what communication scholars have termed **temporal decontextualization** in viral spread: the progressive erasure of provenance metadata as content migrates across platforms and user communities (Wardle, Claire, and Hossein Derakhshan. *Information Disorder: Toward an Interdisciplinary Framework for Research and Policy Making*. Council of Europe, 2017). The Snopes "Outdated" classification, while a useful epistemic tool, is relatively rare and may be underused. Most fact-checking frameworks operate in binary (true/false) or at most ternary (true/false/unproven) modes, leaving the "was true, now false" temporal dimension poorly served.

A compelling case could be made that viral claims about limited-time commercial promotions — which are structurally guaranteed to become outdated — represent a distinct epistemological category requiring dedicated tracking methodology at fact-checking organizations. The *Scream* house Airbnb is a minor but illustrative case study.

## §6 — Summary and Knowledge Status

| Dimension | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Core claim status** | Outdated (was true for October 2021; not true for 2022 or later) |
| **Primary evidence** | Airbnb listing confirming three-night availability at $5/night; Snopes original and updated reporting; Gizmodo contemporaneous coverage |
| **Franchise context** | Promotion was dual-purpose: 25th anniversary of *Scream* (1996) + marketing for *Scream* (2022) |
| **Property location** | Tomales, Marin County, California (~70 miles north of San Francisco) |
| **Epistemic gap** | No empirical literature on overnight horror-filming-location stays as a distinct hospitality/psychological category |
| **EARC classification** | C (Confirmed with time-limited validity; appropriately rated "Outdated" by primary fact-checker) |

The *Scream* house Airbnb promotion was a sophisticated piece of franchise marketing that leveraged fandom nostalgia, genre self-awareness, and the parasocial affordances of character-as-host framing to generate media attention at low cost. The fact-check record is clean and well-documented. The broader conceptual territory — the semiotic economics of horror filming locations as rentable heritage objects, and the psychology of overnight horror tourism — remains largely unmapped in the empirical literature and presents a genuine research opportunity.
