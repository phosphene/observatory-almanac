# The Sea Captain That (Probably) Invented Doughnuts


## 1. The Claim

The origin of the doughnut hole — that defining structural feature that distinguishes a ring doughnut from a ball of fried dough — is one of American culinary mythology's more richly contested questions. The subject of multiple competing folk narratives, at least one congressional monument attempt, and a lasting cultural fascination with the improbability of a nautical origin for a beloved baked good, the claim examined here is: Did a sea captain named Hanson Crockett Gregory invent the doughnut hole, and if so, when, and why?

The story has been circulating in American popular culture since at least 1916, when the then-85-year-old Captain Gregory himself gave an interview to *The Washington Post* claiming authorship of the hole. Since then, the story has been included in food history compilations, regional Maine cultural history, museum exhibits, and countless popular accounts of American culinary innovation. But multiple competing versions exist — the Storm Version, the Penny-Pinching Version, and Gregory's own Indigestion Version — and the existence of competing narratives raises legitimate questions about which, if any, represents the evidence-backed account of what actually happened.

Snopes's coverage of this story, published in June 2021 by reporter Dan Evon under the headline "It was a dark and stormy night in 1847...," classified it in the historical curiosity and entertainment category rather than the conventional true/false verdict structure. The answer is that Gregory almost certainly did something noteworthy to the form of the doughnut around 1847, but the specific mechanism and motivation behind the change remain contested even by Gregory's own accounts. Hence Snopes's hedged title: the captain who "probably" invented doughnuts.


## 2. The Pre-Hole Prehistory: Olykoeks and Dutch Settlers

Before engaging the Gregory question, it is necessary to understand what doughnuts looked like before the hole, because the innovation Gregory is credited with does not involve the creation of fried dough from scratch — it involves the specific but momentous modification of adding a central hole to something that already existed.

The predecessor to the American doughnut is the Dutch *olykoek* — literally "oily cake" — a ball of fried dough that Dutch settlers brought to North America in the 1600s. The term and the dish were part of the cultural and culinary heritage of the Dutch colonial settlement in what is now New York State (then New Amsterdam), and the olykoek shops of new-world Dutch communities represent the doughnut's documented American genealogy. A woman named Anna Joralemon reportedly operated a successful olykoek shop in New York in 1673, representing one of the earliest documented instances of commercial fried dough sales in the American colonies.

The olykoek, being a ball of fried dough without a hole, had a structural problem: the outer edges of the ball cooked faster than the center. In particularly thick or dense olykoeks, the exterior might reach a fully cooked golden-brown state while the interior remained uncooked or partially doughy — a problem that cooks addressed through various strategies. One common solution, documented in food history sources including a 1969 compilation of historical recipes included in the Snopes account, involved stuffing the dough ball with fruits and nuts. By displacing the center of the pastry with a dense filling, the cook changed the thermal dynamics of the frying process in a way that promoted more even cooking throughout. Elizabeth Gregory — the mother of Captain Hanson Crockett Gregory and a "famous local seafront cook" according to the 1969 recipe compilation — is credited with developing a doughnut-precursor recipe that featured nutmeg, cinnamon (both carried as cargo on her son's shipping runs), lemon rind, and hazelnuts or walnuts at the center. The nuts served not only as flavoring but as the structural displacement mechanism that prevented soggy middles.

This pre-hole innovation context is important for understanding what Gregory's contribution actually was. He did not invent fried dough. He did not invent spiced fried dough. He did not even invent the concept of modifying the center of a fried dough ball to solve the cooking problem. He modified the specific *solution* — from "fill the center with nuts" to "remove the center entirely" — and in doing so created the structural form of the doughnut that has persisted for more than 175 years.


## 3. The Three Versions and Gregory's Own Account

Three competing origin stories have circulated for the Gregory doughnut hole innovation, and they diverge on the most fundamental narrative question: why did he do it?

**Version 1: The Panicked Storm Captain.** According to this version — Snopes's self-acknowledged "favorite" for its sheer dramatic implausibility — Gregory was eating one of his mother's doughnuts on his ship on a dark and stormy night in 1847 when a sudden storm struck with no warning. Finding himself urgently needing both hands to steer, Gregory impaled the doughnut on one of the handles of the ship's steering wheel, thereby creating a hole in the center through emergency improvisation. Having survived the storm with the expedient of hands-free doughnut storage on a wheel handle, Gregory supposedly recognized the practical convenience of the hole and began incorporating it as a standard feature. A 1980 illustration by John Huehnergarth, published in *Boy's Life* magazine, depicts this moment with a thoroughly straight-faced commitment to the nautical implausibility of the scenario.

Snopes notes, reasonably, that it is "skeptical, to say the least, of this version of the story." The mechanism by which impromptu ship-wheel impalement becomes a standing culinary innovation recommendation to one's mother requires accepting a somewhat heroic chain of causation. The storm version belongs to the genre of origin myth that values theatrical memorability over plausibility.

**Version 2: The Penny-Pinching Captain.** This version, documented in the same 1969 historical recipe compilation included in the Snopes account, holds that Gregory's primary motivation was cost reduction. Elizabeth Gregory had been filling the centers of her doughnuts with hazelnuts or walnuts — expensive ingredients that added material cost to every pastry. Gregory, described by the compilation as "a wise captain who knew the value of a penny," allegedly went to a tinsmith, commissioned a circular cutter with a hole in the center, brought it to his mother, and instructed her to use it to produce doughnuts without the nut filling. The resulting center-absent doughnuts not only saved the cost of the nuts but, per the framing of the account, produced a pastry that cooked more evenly and retained the praise of Gregory's crew — who, conveniently, did not notice the nuts were gone or didn't care.

The penny-pinching version is economically plausible in a way the storm version is not, but it contradicts Gregory's own account and attributes to him a somewhat uncharitable motive — savings-driven indifference to the pastry's original quality — that clashes with the more affirmative self-portrait Gregory offered in his 1916 interview.

**Version 3: The Indigestion-Driven Invention (Gregory's Own Account).** In 1916, *The Washington Post* interviewed the then-85-year-old Captain Gregory directly. Gregory's version was that the doughnuts his mother made were too oily and sat badly on digestion — they caused gastrointestinal discomfort, particularly the undercooked centers, and Gregory resolved around 1847 to solve the problem by removing the center entirely. Without the dense unreached interior mass, he reasoned, the doughnut would fry evenly throughout, become less oily overall, and trouble his digestion less. Gregory's interview transcript, as preserved in the Snopes account, begins: "It was back, oh, I don't know just what year..." — a characteristically unpretentious opener for a culinary origin claim that Gregory clearly considered important enough to report accurately, even if precise year memory escaped him at age 85. Gregory stated that his discovery was, in his own comparison, on par with Columbus's discovery of America — a claim that, while grandiose, illustrates how sincerely he regarded his innovation as historically significant.


## 4. Epistemic Analysis: The Legend-Evidence Interface in Food History

The doughnut hole case is an exemplary study in what might be called the legend-evidence interface problem in culinary origin history: the space where folk narrative, self-testimony, and material documentation intersect imperfectly but productively.

Food history as a scholarly discipline has grappled extensively with the methodological challenges of tracing the origins of specific culinary practices. The problem is structurally difficult: most culinary innovation happens without documentation, adopted iteratively by anonymous practitioners rather than attributed to named inventors, and the record that does exist is typically retrospective, shaped by cultural interests in assigning credit and narrative coherence. The result is that many food origin stories are either entirely undocumented (leaving only plausible speculation) or over-documented (attracting multiple competing claimants, each supported by partial evidence). The doughnut hole falls into the second category: there is genuine evidence for Gregory's role, but it is the self-testimony evidence of an old man describing events from roughly seventy years earlier, corroborated by family tradition and the 1969 historical recipe compilation but not by any contemporary 1847 documentation (Freedman, Paul, ed. 2007. *Food: The History of Taste*; Theophano, Janet. 2002. *Eat My Words: Reading Women's Lives Through the Cookbooks They Wrote*).

The three-version structure of the Gregory legend is itself epistemically instructive. The storm version (dramatic, memorable, impractical) behaves like an origin myth generated by cultural transmission: it privileges narrative tension over plausibility and is the kind of story that persists because it is maximally satisfying as a story. The penny-pinching version (pragmatic, economically coherent, unflattering) resembles a demythologizing counter-narrative: it offers a deflationary account that grounds the invention in base economic motivation rather than either heroism or intellectual insight. Gregory's own version (indigestion-driven, quietly self-aggrandizing in the Columbus comparison but modestly uncertain in its dating) is the most psychologically plausible of the three — it describes a modest but genuine problem-solving insight motivated by immediate physical discomfort, credited to a specific individual who carried the memory forward and claimed it proudly.

**Epistemic novelty**: The three-version structure of the doughnut hole origin story presents an unusually clean case for examining how redundant origin narratives function epistemically. In most cases where multiple incompatible stories exist for the same historical event, historians must choose between them on the basis of evidence quality and circumstantial plausibility. Here, Gregory's self-testimony is available and provides a choice point: the storm version and penny-pinching version are both third-party retroactive narratives, while the indigestion version is primary testimony from the person most plausibly positioned to know. Given basic standards of historical evidence evaluation, primary testimony — even from an elderly informant with imperfect year-memory — carries considerably more evidential weight than recollections transmitted through folk narrative chains. The epistemically correct conclusion is not "we cannot know" but rather "Gregory's own account is the best available evidence and should be weighted accordingly, while acknowledging that it is unverifiable through independent documentation."

This represents a general principle in oral origin history: self-testimony, even imperfect, typically outweighs equally imperfect third-party legend when evaluating competing accounts of a specific person's action. The standard of proof in this context is "preponderance of evidence" rather than "beyond reasonable doubt," and the preponderance here favors the indigestion account.

There is a secondary epistemic issue worth noting: the question of whether Gregory *invented* the doughnut hole or merely *popularized* it. Distinct from the specific Gregory story, a parallel track of food history documents other putative claimants to the doughnut hole innovation, including Dutch-descended bakers who may have made the modification independently, and the possibility that the hole configuration was known in multiple pastry traditions before Gregory's claimed 1847 date. Food historians acknowledge this possibility without being able to resolve it: the absence of documentation for earlier hole-doughnuts does not prove they didn't exist, but Gregory's claim predates all available documented counter-evidence (Quinzio, Jeri. 2009. *Of Sugar and Snow: A History of Ice Cream Making*; Coe, Sophie D., and Michael D. Coe. 1996. *The True History of Chocolate*).


## 5. Cultural Legacy and Memorialization

Captain Hanson Crockett Gregory's claim to the doughnut hole has been treated seriously enough by American regional culture to generate institutional memorialization. In Rockport, Maine — the town most closely associated with Gregory's biography — there is documented community engagement with his legacy as a figure of local culinary history. The town is near Camden, Maine, a historic seaport community in Midcoast Maine where maritime history intersects with folk culinary tradition in ways that occasionally produce lasting recognition for figures like Gregory.

The broader cultural significance of the doughnut hole as American culinary form is considerable. The ring doughnut's structural innovation — the hole — proved to be enormously consequential from a commercial standpoint: it standardized portion size, improved cooking evenness, reduced ingredient costs (no center filling needed), created a distinctive and instantly recognizable visual form, and, perhaps most importantly, enabled the development of the doughnut-frying machinery that would support the mass commercial doughnut industry beginning in the early twentieth century. Ring-format doughnuts can be fried on belt-driven conveyor systems with consistent timing and temperature control in ways that ball doughnuts cannot, and the Krispy Kreme and Dunkin' industrial production models that dominate the contemporary doughnut industry both rely on ring-format mechanical frying that traces back to the structural form Gregory (probably) originated.

The doughnut hole as a food product in its own right — the small ball of dough punched from the center of the ring — is an additional minor innovation that emerged from the ring-format doughnut. Krispy Kreme's "Original Glazed Doughnut Holes" (branded as "Holes") and Dunkin's "Munchkins" are direct commercial descendants of the material that Elizabeth Gregory's original nutty filled doughnuts contained at their centers: a piece of dough that was once the structural problem the hole was designed to solve, repurposed as a separate product once the hole had made its removal normative. The full chain of culinary logic runs from the olykoek's uneven cooking problem, through Elizabeth Gregory's nut-filling solution, through Hanson Gregory's hole innovation, to the Munchkin — a five-generation product genealogy that maps onto roughly 350 years of American culinary history.


## 6. Verdict

**Rating: PROBABLY TRUE**

The available evidence supports the conclusion that Captain Hanson Crockett Gregory of Maine was responsible for introducing the hole to the center of the American doughnut, likely around 1847. Gregory's own testimony to *The Washington Post* in 1916 — that he was motivated by a desire to ameliorate the indigestion caused by his mother Elizabeth Gregory's center-heavy fried cakes — is the best available primary evidence for this claim and, on the standard of historical preponderance, outweighs the competing storm and penny-pinching versions, both of which are third-party retroactive narratives without supporting primary documentation.

The caveat embedded in Snopes's "probably" framing is real and justified: Gregory's 1916 testimony describes events approximately 69 years prior, dates the relevant year only approximately, and cannot be independently confirmed through contemporaneous documentation. The possibility that comparable ring-format fried dough existed before Gregory's innovation cannot be excluded. What can be said with confidence is that Gregory claimed the invention, that his claim predates all documented counter-testimony, that his account's internal logic is consistent with documented culinary history, and that his innovation — if genuine — had consequences for the development of the American doughnut industry that are profound and traceable.

The doughnut as a ring with a hole is traceable through Gregory's self-testimony and subsequent food history scholarship to a specific person, a specific motivation (indigestion), and a specific approximate date (around 1847) on the Maine coast. That is more evidence than most culinary origin claims can claim.
