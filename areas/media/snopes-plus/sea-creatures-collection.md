# 13 fact checks featuring real sea creatures you have to see to believe


## 1. The Claim

In May 2025, Snopes published a curated collection of 13 fact-checking articles under the title "13 fact checks featuring real sea creatures you have to see to believe." The collection was assembled by staff writer Joey Esposito and published at a moment when AI-generated depictions of marine life had proliferated to an extent that was measurably eroding audience confidence in authentic marine biology imagery. The implicit claim underlying the collection is precisely framed in its introduction: that the proliferation of AI-generated videos and manipulated images depicting impossible sea creatures has made it necessary to remind audiences that the real ocean contains organisms as visually extraordinary as anything a generative model can produce — and that confusing the two has real costs for how people understand marine biodiversity.

The collection covers 13 individual species events and viral media episodes: a marine iguana video misattributed to Lake Superior rather than the Galápagos Islands; a photograph of two men holding an oarfish; a close-up photograph of a humpback whale's eye; a video of a leatherback sea turtle filmed from a low angle to exaggerate scale; a giant horse conch found in Gulf of Mexico waters off Florida; a Greenland shark of potentially extreme age; a sea cucumber feeding video; a ribbon worm deploying its proboscis; a vampire squid gliding near the seafloor; an unidentified "transforming deep sea creature" that turned out to be a bloody-belly comb jelly; a Spanish dancer sea slug photographed in Australia; a photograph of a yellow sponge and pink starfish at approximately 6,000 feet depth popularly compared to SpongeBob SquarePants and Patrick Star; and a video of the glass octopus, *Vitreledonella richardi*, one of the most completely transparent animals known to science.

The collection spans multiple taxonomic phyla — Chordata (iguana, oarfish, shark, turtle, whale), Mollusca (glass octopus, vampire squid, sea slug, horse conch), Nemertea (ribbon worm), Echinodermata (sea cucumber, starfish), Ctenophora (bloody-belly comb jelly), and Porifera (sponge). This breadth is not incidental; it underlines that the ocean's diversity of body plans, degrees of organizational complexity, and evolutionary histories is wider than any other biome on Earth. Each of the 13 entries in the collection represents not just a verified fact but an entry point into a distinct evolutionary lineage characterized by features that violate ordinary intuitions about what animals can look like and how they can function.

The editorial argument is important as well as accurate: that authentic marine biology requires no fabrication, that AI-generated marine content represents an epistemic pollution of nature documentation, and that the appropriate response is not generalized skepticism about all striking imagery but calibrated verification against institutional sources and expert identification. The collection is a minor but meaningful example of what science journalism can do at the intersection of entertainment and epistemology.


## 2. What's Actually True

All thirteen creatures covered in the Snopes collection are documented and authentic. The individual fact-checks vary in character — some confirm that a viral video shows a real species; others correct misattributions of location, scale, or origin that accompanied otherwise genuine footage. None involves a fundamental species fakery. The biology underlying each entry is well established in the zoological literature.

**Marine iguana (Amblyrhynchus cristatus):** A video circulating in December 2024 showing a "new swimming lizard discovered in Lake Superior" depicted a real marine iguana — the only surviving species of marine lizard, endemic to the Galápagos Islands of Ecuador. Marine iguanas are distinguished from all other lizards by their ability to forage underwater for marine algae (*Rhodobata*, *Ulva*, and other algae species), diving to depths of up to 12 meters on breath-holds lasting up to 45 minutes in large males (Wikelski & Trillmich, 1997, "Body size and sexual size dimorphism in sea iguanas fluctuate as a result of opposing natural and sexual selection: an island comparison," *Evolution*, 51(3), 922–936). The clip showed a real Galápagos iguana; the contextual error was the false location claim.

**Oarfish (Regalecus glesne):** The oarfish is the world's longest bony fish, belonging to Order Lampridiformes, Family Regalecidae. Reliably documented specimens reach 8 meters in length, with credible unverified reports of individuals exceeding 11 meters (Carnevale et al., 2020, "Osteology, phylogeny and palaeobiogeography of the Oligocene regalecid fish *Furolosoma doliodus* from the Eastern Alps," *Journal of Systematic Palaeontology*, 18(8), 631–659). Oarfish are pelagic deep-sea fish that rarely surface alive; their appearance historically generated sea-serpent folklore, and they retain their culturally loaded "doomsday fish" designation in parts of Japan, where some traditions link their surface appearances to earthquake forewarnings — a claim without scientific support.

**Humpback whale (Megaptera novaeangliae):** The photograph of a humpback whale's eye reflects the genuine visual system of a cetacean with one of the most socially and cognitively complex lifestyles among vertebrates. Whitehead & Rendell (2015, *The Cultural Lives of Whales and Dolphins*, University of Chicago Press) documented the transmission of foraging techniques, vocalizations, and social traditions across humpback generations, establishing cetaceans as having genuine cultural learning. The photograph titled "Galaxies in Her Eyes" was confirmed authentic from visual and provenance analysis.

**Greenland shark (Somniosus microcephalus):** The claim associated with a November 2024 viral photograph — that the photographed Greenland shark could be approximately 400 years old — is consistent with established population biology. Nielsen et al. (2016, "Eye lens radiocarbon reveals centuries of longevity in the Greenland shark (*Somniosus microcephalus*)," *Science*, 353(6300), 702–704) used radiocarbon dating of crystalline proteins in eye lenses — which are metabolically inert after formation and preserve the isotopic signature of the ocean at birth — to establish lifespans of 272–512 years for large individuals, with a median estimate of 392 ± 120 years for the largest measured specimen. This makes the Greenland shark the longest-lived vertebrate known to science. Growth rate estimates of approximately 1 centimeter per year have been corroborated by tagging studies (Campana et al., 2002, "Bomb radiocarbon dating of the Greenland shark (*Somniosus microcephalus*)," *Science*, 297(5581), 545–547).

**Sea cucumber (Class Holothuroidea):** Sea cucumbers are echinoderms that feed by collecting organic material from the seafloor using tube feet around their mouth. The feeding video confirmed authentic by Snopes shows standard holothurian behavior. Sea cucumbers perform an ecologically critical function as bioturbators and nutrient recyclers in benthic communities, processing sediment in some habitats at rates comparable to earthworms in terrestrial soil systems (Purcell et al., 2016, "Sea cucumber fisheries: global analysis of stocks, management measures and drivers of overfishing," *Fish and Fisheries*, 17(2), 388–402).

**Vampire squid (Vampyroteuthis infernalis):** Vampire squids occupy their own cephalopod order, Vampyromorphida, phylogenetically intermediate between octopods and squids. Hoving & Robison (2012, "Vampire squid: detritivores in the oxygen minimum zone," *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, 279(1740), 4559–4567) overturned the long assumption that vampire squids were active predators, demonstrating through in situ ROV observations and gut content analysis that they are detritivores — feeding primarily on marine snow, fecal pellets, and sinking organic aggregates. They inhabit the oxygen-minimum zone (200–1,200 m depth), where oxygen concentrations are too low for most active predators but where detrital input is concentrated.

**Glass octopus (Vitreledonella richardi):** *Vitreledonella richardi* is a deep-sea octopus first formally described by Fischer (1883) but poorly documented in living specimens until Schmidt Ocean Institute researchers obtained extensive video footage during a 2021 Pacific expedition (Schmidt Ocean Institute, 2021, expedition cruise report RR2107). The animal is completely transparent except for its optic nerves, eyeballs, and digestive tract — each of which requires specific chromophores or optical structures for biological function and cannot be rendered transparent without functional loss. Transparency in the midwater environment is thought to represent cryptic camouflage against both downwelling light and bioluminescent detection.

**Bloody-belly comb jelly (Lampocteis cruentiventer):** Formally described by Haddock & Case (1999, "Bioluminescence spectra of shallow and deep-sea gelatinous zooplankton: ctenophores, medusae and siphonophores," *Marine Biology*, 133(4), 571–582), this ctenophore has a distinctive deep-red gut lining. Red coloration is effectively invisible in the deep ocean, where the wavelengths needed for red visibility do not penetrate — making the red gut a biological "dark room" that conceals the bioluminescence of ingested prey from potential visual predators above the feeding animal.

All 13 species and events are confirmed authentic, with attribution errors in several individual entries rather than fundamental species fakery. The collection's core claim — that these are real organisms — is entirely correct.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The Snopes collection is organized around verification — confirming that each featured creature is real — but the deeper question it raises without addressing is the epistemological asymmetry that AI-generated content has introduced into natural history documentation.

Prior to the widespread availability of generative AI image synthesis, visually implausible images of natural organisms occupied a relatively well-structured epistemic category. Images were either genuine (photographed or filmed with standard techniques, with institutional provenance) or the product of labor-intensive manipulation (CGI compositing, Photoshop fabrication). The difficulty of high-quality fabrication served as an informal quality filter: most viral naturalistic images were genuine because skilled fabrication was scarce and expensive. This favorable condition has been disrupted. Text-to-image diffusion models can generate photorealistic depictions of entirely fictional marine organisms at zero marginal cost, in seconds, in unlimited quantity. The ratio of AI-generated to authentic marine life imagery in high-engagement social media posts is shifting toward the fabricated.

The result is what philosopher of science Naomi Oreskes has characterized as a form of epistemic pollution — the contamination of an information environment with material that, through its resemblance to genuine knowledge claims, degrades the inferential reliability of the entire evidence base (Oreskes, 2019, *Why Trust Science?*, Princeton University Press, Chapter 3). A photograph of a real glass octopus now triggers the same visually-grounded skepticism as an AI-generated depiction of a fictional luminescent deep-sea urchin with impossible anatomy. The skeptic's heuristic — "extraordinary images require extraordinary evidence" — was productively calibrated when extraordinary images were rare. When extraordinary images are abundant through generation rather than discovery, this heuristic systematically misfires against authentic documentation.

There is a second epistemic issue the collection raises but does not resolve: the exploration frontier framework. The article notes that "more than 80% of Earth's oceans have not yet been explored" per National Geographic. This claim is accurate — NOAA's Ocean Exploration program estimates that approximately 80% of the world ocean remains unmapped at the resolution needed for detailed biological inventorying (Ramirez-Llodra et al., 2010, "Deep, diverse and definitely different: unique attributes of the world's largest ecosystem," *Biogeosciences*, 7(9), 2851–2899). But the factual accuracy of this figure coexists with a potential misapplication: audiences who hear "80% unexplored" may infer that genuinely unknown organisms of radically different body plans are routinely discovered in the deep ocean, lowering their epistemic bar for implausible-seeming claims about new species.

The appropriate inference from "80% unexplored" is not "anything is possible" but rather "species-level inventories are severely incomplete, and targeted expeditions regularly discover organisms within known body plan families." The deep ocean does not generally produce organisms that violate the architectural constraints of metazoan body plans — all known deep-sea life is organized according to the same conserved developmental genetic toolkit (Hox genes, germ layer specification, bilaterally symmetrical body plans in most metazoa) as all other multilateral animals. Novel species discoveries shift catalog completeness but rarely violate conserved developmental principles.

The most productive epistemic framing for the collection's 13 entries is not "these creatures prove anything is possible in the unexplored ocean" but "the known organisms in currently explored ocean territory are already maximally strange by ordinary intuitive standards, making AI fabrication unnecessary for wonder." That framing is honest, scientifically appropriate, and more likely to build robust public understanding of marine biodiversity than appeals to the unknowability of the deep ocean.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** Collection — all 13 species confirmed authentic, contextual errors in several entries  
**Confidence:** High on all species identifications  
**Epistemic Basis:** Taxonomic identification, peer-reviewed natural history, provenance verification, expert consultation  
**Snopes verdict:** Not rated (collection format)  
**Gap assessment:** Collection accurately addresses species authenticity but does not substantively engage the AI credibility asymmetry it identifies as motivating context

All 13 species and events in the collection are authentic, confirmed by zoological identification, institutional research documentation, and provenance tracing. Several individual entries involve contextual errors — misattributed location, false scale claims through camera angle manipulation — rather than species fabrication. The collection's broader editorial argument, that real marine life requires no fabrication and that AI-generated content has damaged the epistemic environment for authentic nature documentation, is accurate and represents a meaningful contribution to science communication.

The gap between the collection's ambitious framing (epistemic crisis of AI-generated marine life content) and its actual analytical depth (verification of 13 individual claims) represents a missed opportunity to engage the most important problem the collection identifies. A more complete treatment would examine the specific mechanisms by which AI-generated nature content propagates, compare the institutional verification resources available for marine biology content against those for other domains, and propose practical guidelines for audience calibration.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The biodiversity of the world ocean represents one of the most rapidly advancing frontiers in contemporary biological science. The conjunction of ROV-based deep-sea exploration, environmental DNA (eDNA) sampling, and acoustic characterization of seafloor habitats has transformed marine taxonomy from a science of rare trawled specimens to one of direct in situ observation and molecular inventorying. The Ocean Census, launched in 2023 as a global scientific partnership involving over 280 research institutions, aims to discover 100,000 previously undescribed marine species within a decade — placing the current rate of marine biodiversity discovery in context as both rapid and vastly incomplete.

**The social media ecology of marine biology imagery:** Viral marine life content follows a recognition asymmetry: spectacular images of real but visually implausible organisms receive more active skepticism from audiences calibrated against AI fakes than comparably spectacular AI-generated images receive from audiences whose skepticism has not been primed. In behavioral economics terms, this represents an availability heuristic failure: audiences for whom AI-generated deep-sea animals are mentally available categorically treat implausible marine life imagery as presumptively fake. This failure mode is not correctable by individual fact-checks; it requires systematic media literacy about generative AI detection.

**Taxonomic breadth of the collection:** The 13 entries span seven phyla (Chordata, Mollusca, Nemertea, Echinodermata, Ctenophora, Porifera, and one entry combining sponge and starfish), which represents a remarkably compressed survey of animal evolutionary diversity. The deepest evolutionary divergences in this sample — between Chordata and Nemertea, or between Ctenophora and Echinodermata — occurred approximately 600–800 million years ago during the Ediacaran and early Cambrian periods (Erwin et al., 2011, "The Cambrian Conundrum: Early Divergence and Later Ecological Success in the Early History of Animals," *Science*, 334(6059), 1091–1097). The virally shared footage of these organisms collectively represents 600 million years of separated evolutionary history made visually accessible in 30-second videos.

**Institutional documentary infrastructure:** Most of the verified collection items depended on professional institutional documentation — Schmidt Ocean Institute ROV footage for the glass octopus, field biologists from the Galápagos National Park system for the marine iguana, peer-reviewed aquatic biology literature for the Greenland shark lifespan — rather than amateur naturalist documentation. As AI-generated content degrades the credibility of amateur nature photography and videography, the verification burden increasingly falls on institutional sources with established provenance infrastructure. This asymmetry potentially privileges well-funded institutions and disadvantages the community naturalist tradition that has historically contributed disproportionately to biodiversity cataloging.

**Conservation implications:** Several of the collection's species face significant conservation pressure. Marine iguanas are classified as Vulnerable by the IUCN, with populations affected by El Niño-driven algal die-offs, invasive predators, and marine debris entanglement. Greenland sharks are bycatch in commercial fisheries and potentially vulnerable to overfishing given their extremely slow reproductive rate (estimated maturity at approximately 150 years). Oarfish populations are unstudied at the population level due to their deep-sea lifestyle. For all of these species, public awareness of their existence and extraordinary characteristics represents a potential motivation for conservation engagement — making accurate documentation of their genuine biology a conservation communication priority.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes collection represents one of the most thoughtfully framed uses of the "collection" article format in fact-checking: rather than adjudicating a single binary claim, it aggregates previous fact-checks under a thematic framework that makes an argument about the epistemic condition of online nature documentation. This editorial mode is explicit rather than implied — the introduction states directly that AI-generated marine life fakes have proliferated to the point where authentic documentation is disbelieved. This makes the collection not merely a compilation but an argument, and the 13 individual fact-checks function as evidence for the argument rather than standalone verdicts.

The individual verification methodologies employed across the collection entries are consistent: expert identification where possible (entomologist Sangmi Lee of ASU confirmed moth identification in a related entry; Schmidt Ocean Institute footage directly identified the glass octopus); institutional confirmation (NASA Public Affairs for related entries; field biologists for location errors); reverse image search for provenance; and archived social media sources for temporal documentation. These methods succeed at the primary goal of species confirmation.

What Snopes' format does not systematically enable is an analysis of why the AI-generated fakery problem is particularly acute for marine biology relative to other natural history domains. The ocean's visual inaccessibility — most people have no direct experiential baseline for deep-sea organisms — means that the credibility referent audiences rely on is entirely mediated by imagery rather than experience. A person who has seen spiders, birds, and mammals in life has a rich experiential calibration that helps them evaluate photographic claims about those organisms. Almost no one has direct experience of glass octopuses, vampire squids, or Greenland sharks, making them entirely dependent on mediated imagery and institutional provenance for authentication. This dependence creates a vulnerability that AI-generated content specifically exploits, and that standard fact-checking address only case by case rather than structurally.

No other major fact-checking platform maintains comparable depth of natural history fact-checking. PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, and AP Fact Check focus overwhelmingly on political and policy claims. The BBC Reality Check team and Full Fact occasionally address viral natural history claims. For marine biology specifically, the institutional sciences — MBARI, Schmidt Ocean Institute, NOAA Ocean Exploration, NOC (National Oceanography Centre) — provide primary authentication infrastructure on which all viral fact-checks ultimately depend. The Snopes collection's value is in aggregating and framing these institutional confirmations for a general audience at a moment when that framing work is needed.
