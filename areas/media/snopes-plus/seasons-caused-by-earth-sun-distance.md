# Seasons are caused by Earth's distance from the Sun

## The Claim

Every year, as summer fades into autumn and the first cold nights settle in, the intuitive explanation reasserts itself: we are moving farther from the Sun. The logic feels airtight. It is warmer near a campfire than far from one; it stands to reason that a planet closer to its star would be warmer, and one farther away would be colder. Summer means we are closer. Winter means we have drifted away.

This explanation circulates freely in casual conversation, appears in children's drawings of the solar system with the Earth obviously closer in July and farther in December, and resurfaces in surveys of scientific misconceptions conducted among university students — including, famously, graduates at Harvard. It is coherent. It is physically motivated. And it is, at its core, wrong about what drives Earth's seasons.

The claim that Earth's distance from the Sun causes the seasons is one of the most durable misconceptions in popular science. It persists not because people are careless but because it is a plausible application of a true physical principle — objects nearer to heat sources receive more heat — applied incorrectly to a more complex situation. The real driver of Earth's seasons is geometric, not orbital: it is the tilt of the planet's axis, and the cascading consequences of that tilt for how sunlight strikes the surface.

---

## What's Actually True

Earth's orbit around the Sun is not a perfect circle. It is a slight ellipse, and over the course of a year Earth's distance from the Sun does vary — but in exactly the opposite direction from what the misconception implies, and with far less climatic impact than the tilt effect.

**Earth is closest to the Sun in early January.** This point in the orbit is called perihelion, derived from the Greek for "near the sun." In 2024, perihelion occurred on January 3rd, when Earth was approximately 147.1 million kilometers from the Sun. The farthest point — aphelion — occurs in early July, when the distance stretches to about 152.1 million kilometers. The difference is roughly 5 million kilometers, or about 3.3 percent of the mean orbital distance.

During Northern Hemisphere winter, therefore, Earth is *closer* to the Sun than during summer. If distance were the primary seasonal driver, winters in the Northern Hemisphere should be warmer than summers — the precise opposite of observed reality. The fact that most people in the Northern Hemisphere experience winter as cold even when Earth is at perihelion is a direct empirical refutation of the distance hypothesis, though most people never make the connection.

**The actual cause: axial tilt.** Earth's rotational axis is tilted at approximately 23.5 degrees relative to its orbital plane (the ecliptic). This tilt does not change appreciably over human timescales — it oscillates slowly over cycles of about 41,000 years between roughly 22.1 and 24.5 degrees, a phenomenon called obliquity variation that operates on geological, not seasonal, time scales. On the timescale of a single year, the tilt is essentially fixed.

What changes as Earth orbits the Sun is which hemisphere is tilted *toward* the Sun. From approximately March through September, the Northern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun. From September through March, it is tilted away. (The Southern Hemisphere experiences the reverse: summer runs roughly from November through March.)

This tilt produces two compounding effects that drive seasonal temperature change:

**1. Solar angle.** When a hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun, sunlight strikes its surface at a higher angle — closer to perpendicular. When the Sun is directly overhead, a column of solar energy is concentrated on a relatively small surface area. When the Sun is low on the horizon, that same column of energy spreads across a much larger area, diluting its heating effect. You can demonstrate this yourself with a flashlight and a piece of paper: hold the flashlight perpendicular to the paper and the illuminated spot is small and bright; tilt the flashlight and the same beam of light covers a much larger, dimmer ellipse.

At the summer solstice in the Northern Hemisphere (around June 21), the Sun rises high in the sky — reaching its maximum altitude at noon. At the winter solstice (around December 21), the noon Sun is much lower. The difference in solar elevation angle between these dates can be nearly 47 degrees at mid-latitudes, dramatically affecting how much energy each square meter of ground receives.

**2. Day length.** The tilt also affects how long the Sun is above the horizon. During summer in a given hemisphere, days are longer than nights. During winter, the reverse is true. More hours of sunlight means more total solar energy received each day, even if the sunlight is no more intense per hour. The combination of longer days and higher solar angles during summer amplifies the heating effect; the combination of shorter days and lower solar angles during winter amplifies the cooling.

Near the poles, these effects become extreme: the Arctic and Antarctic experience months of continuous daylight in summer and continuous darkness in winter, not because of orbital position but purely because of tilt. A region experiencing polar night is receiving essentially zero direct solar radiation — not because it has moved farther from the Sun, but because the geometry of the tilt has placed it behind the curvature of Earth relative to the Sun's direction.

**A minor role for eccentricity.** The orbital eccentricity does have a small but real effect on *inter-hemispheric* differences in seasonal intensity. Because Earth is closer to the Sun during Northern Hemisphere winter (and Southern Hemisphere summer), the Southern Hemisphere's summers are fractionally more intense than Northern Hemisphere summers, and Southern winters are fractionally colder than Northern winters. This effect is subtle and largely masked by the very different land-sea distributions between the hemispheres — the Southern Hemisphere is much more ocean-dominated, and oceans have enormous thermal inertia that buffers temperature swings. But the eccentricity effect is real; it just is not what drives the cycle of seasons.

## Why People Believe This

The distance misconception is not random. It is a natural product of how people reason about heat and proximity, and the intuition it draws on is physically valid in many contexts.

**The campfire analogy.** Everyone has experienced that moving closer to a heat source makes you warmer. This is a correct application of the inverse-square law of radiation: the intensity of radiation from a point source decreases with the square of the distance. Move twice as far from a campfire and you receive one-quarter the radiant heat. This is genuinely how physics works. The problem is that Earth's orbital eccentricity is small — about 3.3 percent variation in distance — and the seasonal temperature changes on Earth are far larger than this slight variation could explain. The campfire analogy is physically correct but quantitatively inadequate.

**The missing comparison.** The misconception typically goes unchallenged because people never ask the obvious follow-up question: if summer means we are closer to the Sun, what season is it in Australia right now? The Southern Hemisphere experiences summer while the Northern Hemisphere experiences winter, and they are the same distance from the Sun. A single fact — that it is simultaneously summer in Argentina and winter in Canada — logically demolishes the distance hypothesis. But this fact requires thinking globally, and most people's seasonal experience is entirely local.

**Diagrams and scale.** Diagrams of the solar system in textbooks and popular science materials frequently distort the scale of orbits for visual clarity. When Earth's elliptical orbit is drawn with significant exaggeration, the variation in distance looks dramatic. Children who grow up with these images may form an implicit spatial model in which summer corresponds to the "close" part of the orbit and winter to the "far" part, without ever being explicitly told that this is what the diagram means.

**The Harvard study.** Perhaps the most cited evidence for the prevalence of this misconception comes from a 1987 documentary, *A Private Universe*, produced by Harvard-Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics. Filmmakers stopped Harvard graduates at their commencement ceremony and asked them to explain why we have seasons. The majority gave answers consistent with the distance hypothesis — some quite confident in their explanation. The documentary became a landmark in science education research, not because Harvard graduates are uniquely uninformed but because it demonstrated that even people who had taken college-level science courses could emerge with this fundamental misconception intact. The finding has been replicated many times, across many countries, at many educational levels.

**The intuition is partly right.** There is a kernel of physical truth in the distance idea that makes it sticky. Orbital distance *does* affect climate — just not seasonally, and not for the reason people typically imagine. It affects the total solar energy Earth receives per orbit (which is slightly higher at perihelion) and it contributes to inter-hemispheric differences in seasonal intensity. The distance idea is not purely fabricated; it is a plausible but misapplied physical principle, which is precisely what makes it resistant to correction.

**The tilt explanation is harder to visualize.** Axial tilt requires visualizing a three-dimensional geometric relationship — the orientation of a tilted sphere relative to a distant point source, as the sphere moves around that source. This is genuinely more cognitively demanding than "closer = warmer." The tilt model requires holding in mind simultaneously the angle of the axis, the direction of the Sun, and how both the solar elevation angle and daylight duration change as Earth moves. For many people, this visualization is effortful; the distance explanation is immediate.

## Verdict

**The claim is false, but the reasoning is not random.** Earth's distance from the Sun is not the primary driver of the seasons, and the direction of the variation contradicts the claim: Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, during Northern Hemisphere winter. The seasons are caused by Earth's 23.5-degree axial tilt, which controls both the angle at which sunlight strikes each hemisphere and the duration of daylight — two compounding factors that create the temperature differentials we experience as seasonal change. Orbital eccentricity has a minor, real effect on the relative intensity of seasons between the two hemispheres, but it does not produce the annual temperature cycle experienced within either hemisphere.

**EARC classification: A (Approximately true in narrow sense, misleading overall).** The claim is grounded in a real physical principle (proximity to heat sources increases heating) and a real orbital fact (Earth's distance from the Sun does vary). But the actual causal mechanism for the seasons is axial tilt, and the distance variation runs in the wrong direction to explain hemispheric seasonality. The "grounded" part of this claim is genuine; the distortion is in treating a minor, real effect as the primary driver of a phenomenon that operates via an entirely different mechanism.

## The Wider Picture

The seasons misconception lives at the intersection of two deep challenges in science communication: the difficulty of scale and the problem of locally valid reasoning applied beyond its domain.

**Scale.** Earth's orbital eccentricity is subtle. The roughly 5-million-kilometer variation in Earth-Sun distance sounds large in everyday terms but represents a 3.3 percent change in distance and a roughly 7 percent change in solar intensity. By contrast, the temperature difference between summer and winter at mid-latitudes can exceed 40 degrees Celsius. The orbital variation simply cannot account for this magnitude of change. But without numerical grounding — without knowing *how much* the distance varies and *how much* that affects incoming solar radiation — there is no obvious reason to doubt that the variation is the cause.

**Milankovitch cycles.** The orbital parameters that the misconception misapplies do play a genuine role in climate — just on vastly longer timescales. Milutin Milankovitch, a Serbian mathematician and climatologist, spent much of the early twentieth century calculating how variations in Earth's orbital parameters (eccentricity, axial tilt, and the direction of the tilt relative to perihelion) affect the distribution of solar radiation across latitudes over cycles of tens to hundreds of thousands of years. These Milankovitch cycles are now understood to be a primary driver of the glacial-interglacial cycles of the Pleistocene ice ages. So the claim is not wrong to invoke orbital distance in the context of climate — it is wrong to apply Milankovitch-scale dynamics to the scale of a single year.

**Axial tilt and the geometry of life.** The consequences of Earth's axial tilt extend far beyond temperature. Day length, which varies directly because of tilt, controls photoperiodism in plants — the biological mechanism by which many species regulate flowering, dormancy, and reproduction in response to changing day length rather than temperature. Many birds and mammals use day length as a cue for seasonal behaviors including migration, breeding, and fat accumulation. The tilt-induced variation in solar angle affects atmospheric circulation patterns and precipitation regimes. The seasons, in short, are among the most consequential physical facts about Earth's surface environment for life, and they are all downstream of 23.5 degrees.

**Teaching implications.** The durability of this misconception in educated populations has made it a central case study in the science education literature. Researchers have found that direct instruction — simply telling students the correct answer — is often insufficient to dislodge the misconception. Students who are told that the tilt causes the seasons may repeat this back correctly on tests while retaining the distance intuition at a deeper level. More effective approaches involve confronting the intuition directly with its empirical predictions: if distance causes seasons, what would you expect? Then examining whether those predictions hold. The Southern Hemisphere test, the perihelion date in January, the polar night phenomenon — these are the empirical handles that make the tilt explanation *click* rather than merely register.

**Planetary comparison.** Uranus offers a striking illustration of how extreme tilt effects can be. Uranus's axis is tilted about 98 degrees — essentially on its side relative to its orbital plane. Its poles point nearly toward the Sun for half the Uranus year. This creates seasons of extraordinary intensity: each pole experiences roughly 42 Earth years of continuous sunlight followed by 42 Earth years of continuous darkness. Uranus's orbital eccentricity is modest. Its dramatic seasonal extremes are entirely a product of tilt. This is the tilt principle taken to its logical conclusion.

**Venus, the anti-seasons planet.** Venus has an axial tilt of only about 2.6 degrees — nearly perpendicular to its orbital plane. It also has an extremely circular orbit, with an eccentricity of only 0.007 (compared to Earth's 0.017). Venus therefore experiences essentially no seasons at all: neither tilt nor orbital variation produces any meaningful difference in solar illumination across its surface over the course of its year. Its surface temperature of about 465 degrees Celsius is remarkably uniform across latitudes and time, a product not of any seasonal variation but of its extreme greenhouse effect. The comparison is instructive: a world with minimal tilt and minimal eccentricity has minimal seasons.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim does not typically appear in the catalogues of formal fact-checking organizations in the way that political claims or viral news stories do. It is not a fabricated claim spread through social media but an educational misconception that spreads through intuitive reasoning. As such, it lives primarily in the domain of science education research and public science communication rather than in the domain that Snopes, PolitiFact, or similar organizations typically address.

**Science education research.** The primary documentary record of this misconception comes from research in science education, beginning with work published in the 1980s and continuing through contemporary studies. Researchers including Philip Sadler (Harvard-Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics) and colleagues have surveyed the prevalence of the misconception across grade levels and documented the conditions under which instruction either does or does not correct it. This body of research treats the misconception not as misinformation but as a predictable outcome of how humans apply intuitive physical reasoning, and the remediation literature focuses on conceptual change pedagogy rather than debunking.

**NASA and observatory institutions.** NASA's educational resources directly address this misconception, noting that "it is Earth's tilt that causes the seasons, not its distance from the Sun." The explanation is a standard feature of astronomy education materials from observatories, science museums, and planetariums worldwide. The American Museum of Natural History, the Smithsonian, and similar institutions have produced accessible explanations specifically designed to address this intuition. These institutions treat the correction not as a debunking exercise but as an opportunity to explain the more interesting and more correct tilt-based model.

**The *A Private Universe* documentary.** The most influential public-facing treatment of this misconception is the 1987 Harvard-Smithsonian documentary, which is widely used in teacher training programs in the United States and abroad. The documentary has been viewed and discussed by millions of educators and is frequently cited in science education literature as evidence for the persistence of intuitive misconceptions even after formal instruction. It is not a fact-check in the traditional sense but it serves the same corrective function: documenting the prevalence of the claim and offering the accurate replacement.

**The pedagogical consensus.** Across science education literature and informal science communication, there is strong consensus that the distance hypothesis is incorrect and that the tilt hypothesis is correct. The challenge is not establishing the truth but communicating it in a way that actually displaces the intuitive alternative. Best practices, per the research literature, involve engaging with the intuition rather than simply overriding it — acknowledging that the physical reasoning is locally valid, showing where it breaks down empirically (Southern Hemisphere seasons, perihelion timing), and then building the tilt explanation on top of that productive confrontation with evidence.
