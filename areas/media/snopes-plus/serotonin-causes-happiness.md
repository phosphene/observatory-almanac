# Does Low Serotonin Cause Depression?

## The Claim

For decades, a near-universal cultural message has shaped how millions of people understand their own mental health: depression is a chemical imbalance, specifically a shortage of serotonin in the brain, and antidepressant medications called SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) work by correcting this deficit. The story is elegant, mechanistic, and comforting in its own way — it frames a deeply human experience of suffering as something biological, treatable, fixable. You're not weak. Your brain chemistry is off. Take this pill.

This idea has been repeated in pharmaceutical advertising, medical consultations, popular science journalism, and casual conversation for so long that it has become part of ordinary speech. People "have low serotonin" the way they have low iron or low vitamin D. The framing transforms depression from a complex, multi-dimensional experience into a correctable deficit — like a thermostat set too low. The serotonin hypothesis became not just a hypothesis but a consensus, seemingly unchallengeable, endlessly repeated, embedded in the way patients describe their own condition and doctors describe their patients' conditions back to them.

The problem is that the science behind it has been contested, quietly but persistently, for many years — and a major 2022 systematic review brought that challenge into the open with unusual force, making explicit what cautious researchers had been noting for two decades: the story we tell about serotonin and depression does not match what the evidence actually shows.

## The Evidence

In July 2022, psychiatrist Joanna Moncrieff and colleagues published an umbrella review — a systematic review of systematic reviews — in *Molecular Psychiatry*, one of the leading journals in its field. The study examined the full body of evidence for the serotonin theory of depression across six distinct research domains: serotonin levels and their metabolites in blood and cerebrospinal fluid, serotonin receptor studies (particularly the 5-HT1A and 5-HT2A subtypes), serotonin transporter studies (examining the SERT protein that clears serotonin from synapses), genetic studies, experimental depletion studies (which attempt to temporarily lower serotonin), and studies examining how stress interacts with serotonin-related genes.

Their conclusion was striking: after reviewing hundreds of studies and meta-analyses spanning more than four decades of research, there was no consistent or convincing evidence that depression is associated with, or caused by, lower serotonin levels or lower serotonin activity. The findings across domains were either null, inconsistent, or in some cases pointed in the opposite direction from what the hypothesis would predict.

The most heavily studied domain — serotonin transporter (SERT) studies — showed, in the largest and most methodologically rigorous meta-analyses, either no difference between people with depression and healthy controls, or higher SERT levels in people with depression. Since higher SERT activity means more rapid clearance of serotonin from synapses (i.e., *less* available serotonin in the synapse), this finding would imply that depressed people have lower synaptic serotonin — which sounds like support for the hypothesis. But the interpretation is far more complex: SERT levels also respond to antidepressant treatment, stress, and many other factors, making causal inference from cross-sectional observations unreliable.

Genetic studies provided perhaps the clearest null result. Large genome-wide association studies (GWAS) involving hundreds of thousands of participants found no consistent association between variants in serotonin-related genes — including the serotonin transporter gene SLC6A4, the site of the famous "short allele" that decades of smaller studies had implicated in stress-related depression — and depression diagnoses. When sample sizes became large enough to detect even modest genetic effects with high statistical power, the predicted effects did not materialize.

Tryptophan depletion studies — which temporarily reduce serotonin availability by depleting its amino acid precursor — showed effects on mood in some populations (particularly those with a personal or family history of depression or those already taking antidepressants) but not in controls without such histories. This is inconsistent with the strong causal claim that low serotonin causes depression in otherwise healthy people.

The paper was peer-reviewed, published in a high-impact journal, and co-authored by a team of researchers without ideological stake in undermining psychiatric treatment. Moncrieff is a professor of critical and social psychiatry at University College London, and her prior work, while skeptical of mainstream pharmaceutical psychiatry, is known for careful methodology rather than polemic.

## The Pharmaceutical Narrative and Its Reach

This was not a fringe paper. It drew enormous media attention precisely because the gap it documented was so striking — not a gap between a hypothesis and nature, but a gap between the scientific literature and the public-facing narrative sustained for decades by one of the most profitable pharmaceutical categories in history.

Direct-to-consumer pharmaceutical advertising, legal in the United States and New Zealand but not in most other countries, played a major role in shaping public understanding of depression as a serotonin problem. Eli Lilly's early Prozac campaigns, Pfizer's Zoloft advertisements featuring the now-iconic animated blob whose "chemical messengers" needed rebalancing, SmithKline Beecham's Paxil marketing — all deployed the chemical imbalance frame consistently through the 1990s and 2000s. The Zoloft animations showed a gray lump surrounded by sparse neurotransmitter molecules, implying that the antidepressant restored the rightful density of serotonin in the synapse. These were not descriptions of proven mechanisms; they were marketing metaphors that acquired the authority of fact through sheer repetition.

The clinical setting compounded this. General practitioners and psychiatrists, under time pressure, with limited ability to communicate biochemical nuance to patients who wanted reassurance, found the chemical imbalance framing genuinely useful. It reduced stigma — if you have a biological deficit, you're not weak or morally culpable for your suffering. It justified treatment. It gave patients a framework for understanding their own experience. These are real benefits. The problem is that the framework wasn't accurate, and inaccuracy has costs.

## What the Science Actually Supports

None of what the Moncrieff review documented means antidepressants don't work. SSRIs show efficacy in randomized controlled trials, meta-analyses of those trials, and in clinical practice — particularly for moderate-to-severe depression. Patients who respond to SSRIs often respond substantially and in ways that are meaningful and lasting. The clinical value of SSRIs is not what the 2022 review contested.

What is contested is the *mechanism*. We do not fully understand why SSRIs help the patients they help, and "because they raise serotonin levels in the synapse, and low serotonin caused the depression" is almost certainly an oversimplification, and possibly a complete misdirection. Several alternative or complementary mechanisms have been proposed and studied.

The neuroplasticity hypothesis has accumulated substantial support: SSRIs and other antidepressants increase levels of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), promote hippocampal neurogenesis, and support synaptic plasticity in ways that may underlie their antidepressant effects. Interestingly, ketamine — a rapid-acting antidepressant with a completely different mechanism (NMDA receptor antagonism) — also promotes neuroplasticity through BDNF, suggesting this pathway may be more central to antidepressant action than serotonin per se.

The inflammatory hypothesis has emerged as one of the most productive research frameworks for depression. A substantial proportion of depressed patients show elevated inflammatory markers — C-reactive protein, interleukins, tumor necrosis factor — and depression is strongly associated with conditions of chronic inflammation including metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, and autoimmune conditions. Inflammatory cytokines affect neurotransmitter synthesis and metabolism, including serotonin, but also dopamine and glutamate. Anti-inflammatory interventions show antidepressant effects in some populations. The relationship between inflammation and depression is bidirectional and complex, but it points toward biological mechanisms that are quite different from the simple low-serotonin story.

Social and psychological factors — adverse childhood experiences, chronic stress, social isolation, economic insecurity, trauma, loss, meaninglessness — have strong and well-replicated associations with depression that operate through biological pathways (including the inflammatory and neuroplasticity mechanisms just described) but are not reducible to a serotonin deficit. The psychosocial model of depression is not anti-biological; it is a model of how lived experience gets translated into biology. It is more consistent with the evidence than the serotonin hypothesis and opens up different treatment possibilities: psychotherapy, social interventions, addressing structural causes of distress.

## The Consequences of the Mismatch

When people understand their depression primarily as a serotonin deficit, several things follow that are not necessarily in their interest. They may undervalue or neglect non-pharmacological treatments with strong evidence bases: cognitive-behavioral therapy, interpersonal therapy, behavioral activation, exercise, mindfulness-based interventions. If the problem is a biochemical deficit in the brain, fixing one's relationships or changing one's circumstances might seem beside the point — your serotonin is just low, that's all, take the pill.

This doesn't mean people shouldn't take antidepressants — many people benefit substantially from them. It means that a model that treats depression as primarily a pharmaceutical problem may leave effective tools on the table, and may lead patients to feel that they haven't "fixed" the underlying cause when they stop medication if the underlying cause was never accurately identified.

There is also a question of what patients are consenting to when they start antidepressants. If a doctor says "this will correct your chemical imbalance" and the patient understands that to mean that their serotonin level is measurably low and will be measurably corrected — which is what the simple framing implies — that isn't accurate. The honest answer is that SSRIs help some patients with depression through mechanisms we don't fully understand, that they are worth trying in moderate-to-severe cases based on the evidence of their effects, and that the mechanism is more complex and contested than the serotonin story suggests.

## Critical Reception and the Limits of the Review

The 2022 Moncrieff paper received pushback as well as attention. Critics made several points worth taking seriously.

Some argued that the serotonin hypothesis had already been abandoned by serious psychiatric researchers, making the paper a criticism of a position that the field no longer held. There is truth to this — academic psychiatry had been moving away from the simple low-serotonin model for at least a decade, with leaders in the field acknowledging its limitations in academic publications. The gap was between academic discourse and the narrative reaching patients. Moncrieff's defenders responded that whether or not professional researchers had privately moved on, the public narrative had not changed, and documenting this gap was precisely the point.

Others raised concerns about the public health consequences of the paper — that discouraging patients from taking antidepressants by undermining the justifying narrative could cause harm if patients stopped effective treatment. This is a legitimate concern about science communication, but it does not constitute a scientific critique of the review's findings. The distinction between "this paper might be harmful" and "this paper is wrong" is important.

Some methodological critiques pointed to the heterogeneity of the studies included, the difficulty of measuring serotonin activity in living humans, and the possibility that the null findings reflect measurement limitations rather than genuine absence of effect. These are substantive scientific points about what the evidence can and cannot tell us — they are appropriate grounds for saying the question is not fully resolved, not grounds for saying the evidence supports the serotonin hypothesis.

## The Bigger Picture: How Narratives Outrun Evidence

The serotonin story illustrates something important and generalizable about how scientific consensus can diverge from scientific evidence — particularly when commercial interests, clinical convenience, and public communication pressures all push in the same direction. A hypothesis that was always tentative became a fact in public discourse, not because the evidence strengthened but because the story was useful and the institutions promoting it were powerful.

Every field of medicine has its oversimplifications, its outdated models that linger in practice and popular understanding long after the evidence has moved on. The history of medicine is substantially a history of confident consensus being revised — often against resistance, always with a lag between evidence and practice. What makes the serotonin case notable is the scale of the messaging, the directness of the pharmaceutical industry's involvement in shaping that messaging, the length of time the narrative was sustained, and the directness of the contradiction between the professional literature and what patients were being told.

The 2022 umbrella review did not discover something new. It assembled and synthesized what was already in the literature and made clear that the gap between evidence and narrative had become indefensible. That kind of synthesis work — making visible what the evidence actually says — is exactly what responsible science journalism and science communication requires. The alternative, allowing the pharmaceutical narrative to define public understanding because disrupting it feels risky, is not a service to patients or to science.

## What You Can Do With This

If you have been taking antidepressants and found them helpful, this research says nothing against continuing. SSRIs work for many people; that clinical reality is separate from the question of mechanism. If you have been treating your own depression primarily as a serotonin problem — telling yourself you need to stay on medication because your serotonin is just constitutionally low — it is worth knowing that the picture is more complex. That complexity actually opens up more options, not fewer.

If you have not responded to antidepressants, the null or inconsistent serotonin evidence is consistent with your experience — depression is heterogeneous, different people respond to different interventions, and the chemical imbalance framework can make non-response feel like a personal failure when it is actually information about which treatments fit your particular biology.

If you are a journalist, educator, or healthcare communicator who has been using the chemical imbalance framing as a shorthand for explaining depression: the Moncrieff review is worth reading in full. The evidence does not support the simple story, and the simple story has costs beyond its inaccuracy — it shapes how people understand their own experience, what treatments they seek, and what they expect from those treatments. Accuracy, even when it complicates a reassuring narrative, is ultimately more respectful of the people you're communicating with.

The brain is not a jar of serotonin that needs topping up. Depression is real, serious, often responds to treatment, and is among the most important medical problems facing contemporary societies. But the treatments work in ways we are still working to understand, and that understanding is more complex, more interesting, and ultimately more useful than the story we've been telling. Sitting with that complexity is not comfortable. It is, however, honest.
