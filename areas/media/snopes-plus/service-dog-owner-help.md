## The Claim

On June 12, 2018, a Tumblr user identified as Tessa Connaughton posted a brief account of an incident in which she fell, her service dog left her side, and the dog returned with a human who was able to help. Connaughton has epilepsy and described the dog as specifically trained to seek assistance during a seizure. Her post concluded with a sentence that became the kernel of the viral claim: *"If a service dog without a person approaches you, it means the person is down and in need of help."*

The post was reshared on Twitter, covered by the *Huffington Post*, and picked up by the *Today Show*, where a dog trainer confirmed the behaviour is real and advised people to "follow them." By November 2020, when Snopes published its fact-check in response to reader queries, the claim had become a durable piece of social knowledge — the kind of thing people file under "things you should know in case of an emergency."

The claim as stated is: a service dog that approaches a stranger is communicating that its owner is in distress and requires assistance. The implied guidance is: if you see such a dog, follow it.

---

## What's Actually True

**Some service dogs are specifically trained to seek help. This is real.** The behaviour described by Connaughton — a dog that detects its owner's medical emergency and goes to find a human helper — exists within a recognised category of assistance animal training. The American Kennel Club (AKC) and several accredited assistance dog organisations describe "seizure response dogs" as animals trained to perform specific protective and alert behaviours during epileptic and non-epileptic seizures. These behaviours can include lying beside the owner to cushion falls, activating medical alert devices, opening doors, and going to find another person.

The last behaviour — finding a third party — requires an extraordinary level of training. The dog must learn to leave its owner (its primary orientation point), navigate toward unfamiliar humans, communicate urgency through persistent approach and behaviour, lead the human back to the owner, and do all of this in novel and unpredictable environments. This is not a task most working dogs are assigned, and it is not accomplished by generic obedience training. It requires months of specialised work and real-world proofing in environments similar to where the dog will actually work.

**Seizure alert vs. seizure response — an important distinction.** Seizure *response* dogs, as described above, react to seizures as they happen. Seizure *alert* dogs are dogs that reportedly detect an impending seizure before it occurs — through olfactory cues (there is some evidence that metabolic changes preceding seizure may produce detectable volatile compounds), changed gait, or other subtle cues. Paws with a Cause, one of the accredited organisations that trains both types, notes clearly that seizure alerting behaviour is not formally trainable in the way response behaviour is — it may develop spontaneously in dogs with strong handler bonds, but it cannot be systematically taught and tested. The scientific evidence for alert behaviour is preliminary and its reliability remains unclear.

**The legal framework creates a broad category that the social claim implicitly narrows.** Under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), a service animal is defined as a dog (or in limited circumstances a miniature horse) trained to perform work or tasks directly related to a person's disability. The ADA covers a wide spectrum: mobility assistance dogs, guide dogs for the blind, hearing alert dogs, psychiatric service dogs, diabetic alert dogs, allergy detection dogs — and seizure response dogs. This diversity is important: a guide dog working with a blind owner, a mobility service dog assisting a wheelchair user, or a psychiatric service dog for someone with PTSD has different training, different working protocols, and different responses to their handler becoming unavailable. Very few of these dogs are trained to seek help from strangers. Most are trained to stay with their handler.

The viral claim generalises from one specifically trained response to all service dogs — a generalisation that the Snopes "Mostly True" verdict correctly flags as problematic, though it does not fully unpack the implications.

**What unaccompanied working dogs actually need.** When a working dog is found without its owner and appears distressed, the appropriate response is context-sensitive:

- If the dog is behaving insistently — approaching repeatedly, moving back and forth, making eye contact, clearly trying to communicate direction — following the dog toward its owner is a reasonable action. This matches the trained behaviour Connaughton described.
- If the dog appears disoriented, lost, or panicked rather than purposefully directed, the situation is different: the animal may have separated accidentally, the owner may be at a distance, and the dog's behaviour may not be purposeful communication.
- In all cases: do not touch, feed, or distract a working service dog. These animals have strong task orientation; interrupting that orientation serves no one.

**What if the owner is not having a medical emergency?** The viral framing assumes that an unaccompanied service dog means a medical crisis. This is possible but not the only explanation. The dog's harness or vest may have come off a clip; the handler may be in a building the dog cannot access; the dog may have become temporarily separated in a crowd. Following the dog is generally low-risk as a bystander response, but the assumption that it always means cardiac event or seizure is an oversimplification.

## Why People Believe This

The viral uptake of this particular claim is comprehensible on several levels. It combines several features that make social-media health PSAs spread effectively.

**It gives people a clear, actionable rule.** Human cognition functions better with simple heuristics than with probability distributions. "If dog approaches, follow dog" is a slogan; "some seizure response dogs are trained to seek help when their owners are incapacitated, and you may be able to tell from the dog's purposeful, directed behaviour whether this is one of those cases" is not a slogan. The simplification is what makes it shareable.

**It invokes an emotionally resonant scenario.** A person alone, having a seizure, whose only advocate is a dog trying to communicate with indifferent strangers — this is a scene of vulnerability that activates prosocial instincts. People share it because they want to believe they would help if they encountered it. The emotional logic is correct even if the factual generalisation is imprecise.

**It appears to be endorsed by expertise.** A dog trainer appeared on the *Today Show* and said "follow them." This is technically accurate for dogs specifically trained to seek help, but the broadcast framing — accompanying a viral social media claim — implied broader applicability than the statement alone warranted.

**Service dogs exist in a space of public fascination and incomplete understanding.** Most people have limited direct experience with trained assistance animals. They know service dogs are highly trained, that they perform specific tasks, and that they are accorded special legal status. The leap from "highly trained" to "trained to do this specific thing" is small enough to feel plausible even when it is a generalisation.

The disability community, it should be noted, has a fraught relationship with this kind of viral awareness content. While the Connaughton post was genuine and her experience real, the widespread circulation of simplified rules about service dogs contributes to a culture in which non-disabled people feel entitled to interact with, test, or question the legitimacy of working dogs. The ADA explicitly prohibits businesses from demanding proof that a dog is a service animal; it limits permissible questions to whether the animal is required due to a disability and what work it is trained to perform. Viral posts that inadvertently encourage public interaction with working dogs can undermine the very safety system they mean to publicise.

## Verdict

**The claim is true in a specific and important respect, and misleading in its generalised form.**

Some service dogs — specifically those trained as seizure response dogs and, less commonly, other emergency alert assistance animals — are trained to seek human help when their owner is incapacitated. If such a dog approaches you exhibiting purposeful, directed behaviour, following it is a reasonable and potentially life-saving action.

The claim becomes misleading when stated as a universal rule applying to all service dogs. Guide dogs, mobility dogs, hearing alert dogs, psychiatric service dogs, and diabetic alert dogs are not trained to seek strangers' help, and encountering one without its owner most likely indicates an accidental separation, not an owner incapacitation requiring emergency response.

The practical guidance that remains valid: if a dog in a service animal vest approaches you with apparently purposeful behaviour — and particularly if the behaviour includes attempts to lead you in a direction — taking it seriously is the right response. Call out, ask if anyone needs help, follow the dog toward wherever it's oriented. You may not be dealing with a trained seizure response dog at all, but you are dealing with a dog trying to tell you something, and the cost of being wrong in the direction of helping is low.

The Snopes "Mostly True" verdict is correct. The more useful characterisation is: true for a specific trained capability, and generalisable as a practical heuristic even if the mechanism doesn't always apply, but important to understand in context rather than as a universal fact about service dogs.

## The Wider Picture

The service dog ecosystem in the United States is substantially unregulated on the training side. The ADA does not require any certification, registration, or formal attestation of training. A person can declare their dog a service animal without any third-party verification. This is intentional — the Act's drafters recognised that requiring expensive certification would effectively exclude people with disabilities who could not afford it — but it has produced a landscape in which the quality, depth, and type of training varies enormously among dogs that legally qualify as service animals.

Accredited programs — those affiliated with Assistance Dogs International (ADI) or trained according to AKC standards — typically involve 18–24 months of training, professional evaluation, and task-specific proofing. These are the programs most likely to produce seizure response dogs capable of the seeking-help behaviour described in the viral claim. At the other extreme, basic service dog training from non-accredited sources may produce an animal that performs a simple task reliably in familiar environments but has not been trained to respond to complex emergency scenarios.

An important related category: emotional support animals (ESAs) and therapy animals are not service animals under the ADA. They do not have the same public access rights, and they are specifically not trained to perform disability-related tasks. Much of the public's confusion about service animals — and much of the legitimate frustration of service animal users who find their dogs questioned or challenged — flows from the conflation of these categories.

The broader public health implication of the original claim is worth affirming at a systemic level: people with epilepsy who work alone, live alone, or face public seizures are genuinely vulnerable. The gap in emergency response for individuals who become incapacitated without witnesses is a structural problem, and trained assistance animals fill a genuine niche. Creating a public culture in which people respond appropriately to distress signals from service animals is a legitimate goal. The viral post, whatever its imprecision, may have saved lives.

What would improve the situation is not just better public understanding of service dogs, but better public readiness to help strangers in medical emergencies generally — including knowing how to recognise a seizure, how to respond appropriately (do not restrain; do not put anything in the mouth; call emergency services; time the event; stay until the person is oriented), and how to distinguish a post-ictal state from other kinds of incapacitation.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated the claim "Mostly True," distinguishing between what is true (some service dogs are trained to seek help) and what is false (not all service dogs have this training). This is an accurate and careful verdict.

**The gap this entry fills (Enhance):** The Snopes article correctly establishes the basic true/false mapping but does not substantially explain the training landscape that produces seizure response dogs, the distinction between seizure response and seizure alert behaviours, or the legal framework that creates ambiguity about which dogs have which capabilities. It also does not discuss the disability community's complex relationship with viral service dog content, or the public education case for understanding this behaviour even if the universal claim overstates it.

For fact-checkers, this type of claim presents a specific challenge: it is "mostly true" in a way that could lead readers to either over-apply the advice (approaching any unaccompanied dog as a seizure emergency) or dismiss it after learning the nuance (deciding that since it's not universally true it's not worth remembering). The most useful verdict is one that preserves the actionable core — follow the dog if it seems to be purposefully seeking your attention — while providing enough context to calibrate the response. That requires more space than a typical fact-check affords.

The viral format of the original claim — a first-person testimony from someone with epilepsy describing a genuine experience — is its own epistemic category. It is not technically "misinformation" even in the qualified cases where it overgeneralises. It is a true thing, compressed into a universal rule for shareability, that becomes partly misleading at scale. This is a pattern that standard true/false fact-checking handles imperfectly and that benefits from deeper contextual analysis.
