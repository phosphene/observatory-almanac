# Sex Addiction Is Not a Real Clinical Diagnosis and Cannot Justify Criminal Behavior


## 1. The Claim

"Sex addiction is not a real clinical diagnosis and cannot justify criminal behavior" — a compound claim that circulates most intensely in the aftermath of high-profile criminal cases where defendants or their attorneys invoke compulsive sexual behavior as a mitigating factor.

The claim achieved its most prominent recent cultural moment in March 2021, when Robert Aaron Long killed eight people at three Atlanta-area spas and massage parlors, and an initial sheriff's press conference statement characterized Long's self-reported "sex addiction" as a factor in his motivation. Critics objected fiercely: the statement appeared to validate a psychiatric category that does not exist in official diagnostic manuals, and to frame a racially targeted mass murder as a product of individual pathology rather than targeted violence. The backlash produced a wave of commentary asserting that sex addiction is not a real diagnosis.

The compound claim encompasses two separable assertions: (1) that "sex addiction" does not exist as a recognized clinical entity, and (2) that even if it did, it could not serve as a legal justification or defense for violent crime. These two assertions have different epistemic statuses and different levels of support, and conflating them produces significant confusion about what is actually at stake.

The claim as a cultural-political intervention also does distinct rhetorical work: it challenges the individualization of harms (framing systemic violence as personal pathology), contests the medicalization of moral failure, and resists frameworks that might diffuse accountability for targeted violence. These are legitimate concerns independent of the diagnostic question.


## 2. What's Actually True

**On the diagnostic question:** The clinical status of compulsive sexual behavior is genuinely contested, and the binary framing — "real diagnosis" vs. "not real" — misrepresents the actual state of psychiatric nosology.

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), published by the American Psychiatric Association in 2013, does not include "sex addiction," "hypersexual disorder," or any equivalent category as a diagnosis. A proposal to include hypersexual disorder in the DSM-5 was reviewed and rejected by the DSM-5 Work Group on Sexual and Gender Identity Disorders (Kafka, 2010, *Archives of Sexual Behavior*). The reasons included concerns about insufficient empirical support, inadequate psychometric validation of proposed criteria, and questions about whether the proposed condition was sufficiently distinct from mood disorders, impulsivity disorders, and obsessive-compulsive spectrum conditions.

However, the ICD-11 — the International Classification of Diseases, Eleventh Revision, published by the World Health Organization and in use internationally — does include "Compulsive Sexual Behaviour Disorder" (CSBD) as a recognized condition under Impulse Control Disorders (Kraus, Krueger, Briken, First, Stein, Kafta, Voon, Stein, and colleagues, 2018, *World Psychiatry*). The ICD-11 classification defines CSBD as a persistent pattern of failure to control intense, repetitive sexual impulses or urges that causes distress or impairment in functioning. This is not a fringe position: the ICD-11 is used by WHO member states, is the standard for international health statistics, and its inclusion of CSBD reflects a different judgment about the evidence than the DSM-5 exclusion — not the absence of any scientific recognition.

The claim that sex addiction "isn't real" thus relies specifically on DSM authority. Within DSM framing, there is no recognized diagnosis. Within ICD framing, there is. Whether the condition constitutes an "addiction" in the neurobiological sense is a further question: Kraus, Voon, and Potenza (2016, *Neuropsychopharmacology*) reviewed neurobiological evidence for similarities between compulsive sexual behavior and substance use disorders, finding partial overlap in neural mechanisms (particularly involving reward circuitry and dopaminergic systems) but also important differences. The "addiction" label is contested among specialists even for those who recognize CSBD as a disorder.

This does not mean all claims made under the "sex addiction" banner are valid. The concept has been used to explain away infidelity, minimize responsibility for assault, and pathologize non-normative but consensual sexual behavior. The clinical literature on CSBD explicitly distinguishes between distressing, uncontrolled behavior and simply high-frequency consensual sexual activity or sexual interests that deviate from social norms.

**On the legal question:** Regardless of diagnostic status, sex addiction or compulsive sexual behavior disorder cannot and does not constitute a legal justification or complete defense for violent crime in any U.S. jurisdiction or in the mainstream of international criminal law. This is unambiguous.

Criminal law recognizes mental disorder defenses (insanity, not guilty by reason of mental disease or defect) under narrow criteria that require evidence that a defendant did not understand the nature of their actions or did not understand that their actions were wrong. Impulse control disorders — and compulsive sexual behavior, even if clinically recognized, would be classified as an impulse control disorder, not a psychosis — do not meet the legal standard for insanity defense in any U.S. jurisdiction (Slovenko, 2002, *Psychiatry in Law / Law in Psychiatry*). In some jurisdictions, mental disorder may be introduced as a mitigating factor at sentencing rather than as a defense to criminal liability, but this is a reduction of sentence severity, not an exculpation.

Forensic psychiatry maintains a clear and long-established principle: the question of whether a person has a psychiatric condition is legally distinct from the question of whether that condition excuses or mitigates criminal responsibility. The concept of "volitional impairment" — whether a disorder undermined a person's capacity to control behavior — is relevant to diminished capacity arguments, but impulse control disorders are generally not held to completely negate culpability. Morse (2011, *University of Pennsylvania Law Review*) argues that behavioral compulsion claims routinely overstate volitional impairment in legal contexts, and that the law correctly sets a high bar for excusing behavior on the basis of impulse-control deficits.

In the Atlanta case specifically: no legal defense of sex addiction was advanced. Long pleaded guilty and was sentenced to multiple life terms. The claim that such a defense would have been available or viable had zero legal merit.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The "sex addiction isn't real" claim is unusually interesting epistemically because it is partly motivated by sound political reasoning and partly inaccurate in its diagnostic claims — and these two components reinforce each other in popular discourse through a mechanism that can be named: **counter-medicalization as skeptical proxy**.

When a socially harmful behavior — targeted violence, infidelity, harassment — is framed as a medical condition, critics appropriately worry that medicalization diffuses accountability, transfers responsibility from agent to diagnosis, and shifts conversation from harm to pathology. This concern is legitimate and well-grounded in the sociology of deviance and medicalization literature (Conrad, 2007, *The Medicalization of Society*, Johns Hopkins University Press). Challenging the medical framing by denying the diagnosis is a rhetorical move that encodes the accountability argument as a factual claim.

The claim therefore finds widespread acceptance not primarily because people have reviewed the DSM and ICD, but because it resonates with justified hostility to accountability-diffusing narratives. "Sex addiction isn't real" carries the implicit force of "you don't get to call murdering eight people a medical condition." This is a correct ethical conclusion reached via a factual shortcut that conflates nosological status with moral relevance.

A secondary mechanism is **authority asymmetry in public consciousness**: most people have heard of the DSM and regard it as the authoritative classifier of mental illness. Very few know that the ICD-11 categorizes CSBD as a recognized disorder. The DSM/ICD divergence on this point is not public knowledge, so framing the diagnostic question as settled by DSM exclusion appears accurate.

A third mechanism specific to the sex addiction context is the legitimate critique of the concept's historical uses in treatment and popular culture. "Sex addiction" became a cultural phenomenon partly through celebrity rehab culture — Tiger Woods, David Duchovny, and similar cases brought the concept into mainstream visibility as a middle-class explanation for infidelity and promiscuity. These uses genuinely trivialized the concept, made it seem like a convenient excuse, and generated warranted skepticism about whether what was being described was a genuine clinical condition or social permission to avoid accountability for harmful behavior. This cultural pollution of the concept makes it easier to dismiss entirely.


## 4. The Evidence Landscape

The evidence base decomposes into three separate questions: the phenomenological evidence for compulsive sexual behavior as a clinical pattern, the neurobiological evidence for its relationship to addiction, and the forensic-legal evidence for its relevance to criminal responsibility.

**Phenomenological evidence:** Multiple validated assessment instruments have been developed for measuring compulsive sexual behavior, including the Sexual Compulsivity Scale (Kalichman and Rompa, 1995, *Journal of Sex Research*), the Hypersexual Behavior Inventory (Reid, Garos, and Carpenter, 2011, *Sexual Addiction and Compulsivity*), and the Compulsive Sexual Behavior Disorder Scale (Kraus and colleagues, 2021, *Journal of Behavioral Addictions*). These instruments show good psychometric properties and predict functional impairment. The phenomenological reality — that some individuals experience persistent, distressing failure to control sexual behavior with significant life consequences — is not seriously contested. What is contested is how to classify it diagnostically.

**Neurobiological evidence:** Voon, Mole, Banca, Porter, Morris, Mitchell, Lapa, Karr, Harrison, Potenza, and Irvine (2014, *PLOS ONE*) found that individuals with compulsive sexual behavior showed increased activation of reward-related brain regions (ventral striatum, anterior cingulate, amygdala) in response to sexual cues, a pattern partially similar to that observed in substance-use disorders. Additionally, Mechelmans, Irvine, Banca, Porter, Mitchell, Mole, Lapa, Karr, Harrison, Potenza, and Voon (2014, *PLOS ONE*) reported enhanced attentional bias toward sexual stimuli in individuals with compulsive sexual behavior. These neurobiological findings are consistent with a reward-dysregulation model but do not establish that compulsive sexual behavior constitutes addiction in the full neurobiological sense. Schiebener, Laier, and Brand (2015, *Journal of Behavioral Addictions*) found evidence for executive dysfunction in individuals with problematic pornography use, consistent with impaired inhibitory control.

**Forensic and legal evidence:** Forensic psychiatry literature consistently finds that impulse control disorder claims fail to meet legal standards for diminished criminal responsibility across jurisdictions. Kafka (2010, *Archives of Sexual Behavior*) explicitly noted, in the proposal for hypersexual disorder in the DSM-5, that the diagnosis was not intended to function as a legal defense. Phenix and Hoberman (2016, *Sexual Offending: Predisposing Conditions, Assessment, and Management*) review the use of paraphilic and compulsive sexual behavior diagnoses in forensic contexts and conclude that clinical diagnosis does not transfer to legal exculpation.

**On racial and social justice dimensions:** In the Atlanta case specifically, the application of "sex addiction" framing to an apparent racially motivated mass murder was criticized not only on diagnostic grounds but on accuracy grounds. The spa workers killed were primarily of Asian descent; the pattern of targeting and self-described motivation involved racialized sexualization. Bonilla-Silva (2003, *Racism Without Racists*, Rowman & Littlefield) and subsequent critical race scholarship on the sexualization of Asian women provide a framework for understanding why the pathologizing frame was inadequate: it erases the social context within which the perpetrator's "addiction" and "temptation" were structured.


## 5. Verdict

**Partially true but misapplied.** The claim is right that "sex addiction" as a specific diagnostic label does not exist in DSM-5, and right that no such condition constitutes a legal defense or justification for violent crime. It is wrong that there is no recognized clinical entity corresponding to compulsive sexual behavior: ICD-11 includes Compulsive Sexual Behaviour Disorder as an impulse control disorder, and there is a substantial phenomenological and neurobiological literature supporting the existence of the pattern. The correct formulation is: there is a genuine, contested clinical entity; its nomenclature and classification are disputed between DSM and ICD frameworks; and its existence as a disorder — even if recognized — has no bearing on criminal liability for violent acts, which requires a much higher showing than impulse-control impairment.

The political and ethical work that "sex addiction isn't real" tries to do — resisting accountability-diffusing frames and challenging the medicalization of harm — is legitimate and important. The factual claim that sex addiction has no clinical basis is an oversimplification that does not accurately represent the current state of psychiatric nosology. These two things can both be true.
