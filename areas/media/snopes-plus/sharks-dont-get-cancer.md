# "Sharks Don't Get Cancer"


## 1. The Claim

The claim that "sharks don't get cancer" represents one of modern medicine's most persistent and commercially exploited myths. This assertion suggests that sharks possess some inherent biological immunity to cancer, making them different from virtually all other complex organisms. The claim has been used to justify the harvesting of millions of sharks annually for cartilage-based supplements, positioning shark tissue as a potential cancer treatment or prevention method.

The myth gained particular prominence through I. William Lane's 1992 book "Sharks Don't Get Cancer: How Shark Cartilage Could Save Your Life," which became a bestseller and spawned a multi-million-dollar industry. Lane, who held a commercial interest in shark cartilage supplement companies, presented the claim as established scientific fact, despite the absence of rigorous peer-reviewed evidence supporting shark cancer immunity.

This narrative has persisted across decades, appearing in health magazines, alternative medicine circles, and even some educational materials. The claim often extends beyond simple cancer immunity to suggest that shark cartilage contains active compounds that could treat human cancers, leading to its classification as a dietary supplement in many jurisdictions despite lacking FDA approval as a medicine.

The commercial formulation typically presents sharks as evolutionary marvels that have somehow "solved" the cancer problem through 400 million years of evolution. This narrative appeals to naturalistic fallacies and the assumption that evolutionary age correlates with medical superiority. Marketing materials frequently emphasize sharks' primitive immune systems and cartilaginous skeletons as evidence of their supposed cancer resistance.

Beyond Lane's initial book, the claim has been perpetuated through numerous derivative publications, documentary films, and online health websites. The myth has proven remarkably resilient to scientific correction, partly because it satisfies psychological desires for simple, natural solutions to complex medical problems. The narrative's persistence also reflects the challenge of correcting misinformation that has become economically entrenched.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim "sharks don't get cancer" fails basic biological and empirical scrutiny on multiple levels. Scientific literature documents numerous cases of cancer in sharks, including the very cartilage tumors that would directly contradict Lane's central thesis.

Dr. Gary Ostrander and colleagues published seminal research in *Cancer Research* (2004) documenting 42 different tumor types found in sharks and their relatives. These included chondrosarcomas—cancers of cartilage tissue—which directly refute the notion that shark cartilage possesses inherent cancer-resistant properties. The study examined over 12,000 shark specimens and found malignant tumors in multiple species, including hammerhead sharks, nurse sharks, and dogfish.

The Ostrander study represents the most comprehensive analysis of shark cancer incidence to date, but it builds on decades of earlier observations. Marine pathologists have documented shark tumors since the early 20th century, with cases reported in scientific literature as early as the 1920s. The Registry of Tumors in Lower Animals, maintained by the Smithsonian Institution, contains extensive records of shark malignancies spanning multiple decades.

Specific case studies have documented particularly striking examples of shark cancers. Dr. John Harshbarger documented a nurse shark with multiple hepatocellular carcinomas affecting liver function. Another case involved a sandbar shark with extensive lymphomas throughout its lymphatic system. These cases demonstrate that sharks not only develop cancer but can suffer from the same aggressive malignancies that affect other vertebrates.

Additionally, the National Cancer Institute maintains records of cancer occurrence across vertebrate species, with documented cases in sharks dating back to early 20th-century marine biology research. Marine pathologists have identified hepatic tumors, lymphomas, and other malignancies in various shark species through routine necropsy examinations.

The biological implausibility of universal cancer immunity in sharks becomes clear when considering cancer's fundamental nature. Cancer results from accumulated genetic mutations that can affect any organism with complex cellular division processes. Sharks, despite their evolutionary age and unique immune systems, remain subject to the same basic cellular mechanisms that can lead to malignant transformation.

Modern molecular biology has revealed that sharks possess the same fundamental cell cycle control mechanisms as other vertebrates, including p53 tumor suppressor genes and DNA repair pathways. These systems can fail in sharks just as they do in humans and other animals. The absence of any unique anti-cancer mechanism in shark genetics contradicts claims of inherent immunity.

Furthermore, comparative oncology research demonstrates that cancer occurs across virtually all complex animal taxa, from mollusks to mammals. The notion that sharks alone would have evolved complete cancer immunity contradicts our understanding of evolutionary biology and cancer genetics. If such immunity were possible through natural selection, it would likely have evolved in multiple lineages, not exclusively in sharks.

Environmental factors affecting shark populations also increase cancer risk. Sharks in polluted waters show higher rates of malignancy, particularly liver cancers associated with bioaccumulated toxins. This environmental responsiveness further undermines claims of inherent cancer resistance and demonstrates that sharks respond to carcinogens similarly to other marine vertebrates.


The scientific consensus regarding shark cancer susceptibility rests on robust observational and experimental evidence accumulated over several decades. Multiple independent research groups have documented cancer cases in sharks using standard histopathological techniques, genetic analysis, and long-term population studies.

The American Cancer Society, National Cancer Institute, and other major oncology organizations have consistently stated that shark cartilage supplements lack scientific support for cancer treatment or prevention. Clinical trials, including a major Phase III study led by Dr. Charles Loprinzi and published in *Cancer* (2005), found no significant benefit from shark cartilage supplementation in cancer patients.

The Loprinzi study, sponsored by the National Cancer Institute, represents the gold standard for evaluating shark cartilage claims. The randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial involved 379 patients with advanced cancer and found no significant differences in survival, quality of life, or tumor response between patients receiving shark cartilage supplements and those receiving placebo treatments.

Additional clinical trials have consistently reached similar conclusions. A study published in the *Journal of Clinical Oncology* (2007) examined shark cartilage supplementation in lung cancer patients and found no therapeutic benefit. Meta-analyses of available clinical data have concluded that shark cartilage supplements show no measurable anti-cancer activity in human subjects.

The epistemological strength of the scientific position lies in its reliance on peer-reviewed research, independent replication, and adherence to established standards for biological evidence. Researchers documenting shark cancers have no commercial interests in their findings and often publish results that contradict popular misconceptions regardless of market implications.

The methodological rigor applied to shark cancer research follows standard protocols in comparative oncology and marine biology. Tumor identification relies on histopathological examination, immunohistochemistry, and genetic analysis—the same techniques used to diagnose human cancers. This methodological consistency strengthens confidence in findings across species boundaries.

Furthermore, the burden of proof for extraordinary claims like universal cancer immunity properly rests with claimants, not with the scientific community. The absence of credible evidence for shark cancer immunity, combined with positive evidence of shark cancers, provides strong epistemological grounds for rejecting the myth.

The persistence of the "sharks don't get cancer" claim despite contrary evidence illustrates how commercial interests can distort public understanding of biology. The supplement industry's continued promotion of shark cartilage products demonstrates the epistemological challenges posed when financial incentives align against scientific accuracy.

Regulatory responses have attempted to address these epistemological problems through increased oversight of supplement health claims. The FDA has issued warning letters to companies making unsubstantiated cancer treatment claims for shark cartilage products, though enforcement remains inconsistent given the supplement industry's regulatory protections under DSHEA (Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act).

The broader epistemological lesson from this case involves recognizing how commercial interests can systematically distort the evaluation and communication of scientific evidence. The shark cancer myth demonstrates the importance of considering source credibility, financial conflicts of interest, and methodological rigor when evaluating health claims in commercial contexts.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The epistemological foundation supporting the "sharks don't get cancer" claim reveals a pattern of commercial motivation disguised as scientific authority. I. William Lane's background provides crucial context: he was not a cancer researcher or marine biologist, but rather a businessman with direct financial interests in companies producing and marketing shark cartilage supplements.

Lane's book, while citing some legitimate research on anti-angiogenic compounds, made sweeping extrapolations far beyond what the cited studies supported. He took preliminary laboratory findings about certain compounds potentially inhibiting blood vessel formation and constructed a narrative about shark immunity that the original researchers never claimed.

The research Lane cited primarily involved work by Dr. Judah Folkman at Harvard Medical School on angiogenesis inhibition. Folkman's research demonstrated that certain compounds could reduce tumor blood vessel formation in laboratory settings. However, Folkman himself never claimed that sharks were immune to cancer, nor did he endorse shark cartilage supplements as cancer treatments. Lane essentially appropriated Folkman's legitimate research to support unrelated commercial claims.

The epistemological problem deepens when examining the research methodology underlying Lane's assertions. His book relied heavily on anecdotal reports, uncontrolled case studies, and misrepresented preliminary findings. The absence of peer review for his central claims represents a fundamental departure from scientific standards for evaluating biological phenomena.

Lane's methodology ignored basic principles of scientific inquiry, including controlled experimentation, statistical significance, and independent replication. His evidence base consisted primarily of testimonials from supplement users and selective citation of research that could be construed as supporting his position. This approach violated established standards for evaluating medical claims and therapeutic interventions.

Moreover, Lane's commercial interests created inherent conflicts of interest that were not adequately disclosed to readers. His companies, including Cartilage Consultants and Lane Labs, stood to benefit directly from widespread acceptance of shark cancer immunity claims. This financial motivation raises serious questions about the reliability of his analysis and the selection of supporting evidence.

The epistemological framework Lane employed reflects broader problems in alternative medicine marketing, where commercial interests drive the interpretation of scientific research. The selective citation of preliminary findings, the extrapolation beyond supported conclusions, and the failure to acknowledge contradictory evidence represent systematic departures from scientific objectivity.

The perpetuation of this myth also reflects broader epistemological problems in how health information circulates in commercial contexts. Publishers and retailers have strong incentives to promote dramatic health claims regardless of scientific validity, creating echo chambers where commercially motivated assertions gain apparent authority through repetition rather than verification.

The role of media amplification in spreading the myth demonstrates how epistemological standards can be compromised when commercial and editorial interests align. Health magazines, alternative medicine websites, and popular science publications often prioritize engaging content over scientific accuracy, allowing myths like shark cancer immunity to gain cultural momentum despite scientific refutation.

This case also illustrates the epistemological challenge of correcting misinformation once it becomes economically entrenched. The shark cartilage supplement industry has financial incentives to perpetuate the myth regardless of scientific evidence, creating persistent resistance to factual correction. This economic entrenchment makes the shark cancer myth particularly resilient to standard approaches to misinformation correction.


## 4. Verdict

**REJECTED (R)**: The claim that sharks don't get cancer is scientifically false and commercially motivated. Extensive peer-reviewed research documents cancer cases in multiple shark species, including cartilage cancers that directly contradict the claim's central premise.

The myth originated with I. William Lane's 1992 commercial book promoting shark cartilage supplements, not from legitimate cancer research. Lane's commercial interests in supplement companies created inherent conflicts of interest that were inadequately disclosed to readers and the public. His methodology violated basic scientific standards by relying on anecdotal evidence, selective citation, and extrapolation beyond supported findings.

Scientific evidence against the claim is overwhelming and comes from multiple independent sources. Dr. Gary Ostrander's comprehensive 2004 study documented 42 different tumor types in sharks and their relatives, including chondrosarcomas—cancers of the very cartilage tissue promoted as cancer-resistant. The Registry of Tumors in Lower Animals contains extensive documentation of shark malignancies spanning decades of research.

Clinical trials have consistently failed to demonstrate any anti-cancer benefits from shark cartilage supplements, with major studies published in leading medical journals showing no significant effects on cancer progression or survival rates. The National Cancer Institute-sponsored Phase III trial by Dr. Charles Loprinzi represents the gold standard for evaluating these claims and found no therapeutic benefit.

The biological basis for universal cancer immunity in sharks is implausible given current understanding of cancer mechanisms and evolutionary biology. Sharks possess the same fundamental cellular processes that can lead to malignant transformation in other organisms, including DNA repair pathways and cell cycle controls that can fail under various circumstances.

The perpetuation of this myth has contributed to the decline of shark populations worldwide, as millions of sharks are killed annually for cartilage harvesting based on false medical claims. Conservation biologists identify the supplement industry as a significant threat to multiple shark species already stressed by overfishing and habitat loss.

The epistemological gap classification is **epistemologically-loaded** because the claim serves commercial interests rather than reflecting genuine scientific uncertainty. The evidence against shark cancer immunity is overwhelming and comes from multiple independent research groups with no financial stake in the outcome.

This case demonstrates how commercial health claims can persist in popular culture despite scientific refutation, highlighting the importance of distinguishing between peer-reviewed research and marketing materials when evaluating biological assertions. The regulatory framework allowing supplements to make health implications without pharmaceutical-level evidence creates environments where scientifically refuted claims can maintain market presence.

The scientific consensus is clear: sharks get cancer, shark cartilage supplements lack anti-cancer properties, and the "sharks don't get cancer" myth represents a harmful distortion of marine biology that has contributed to both human health misinformation and wildlife conservation challenges. The persistence of this myth despite contrary evidence illustrates the need for improved science literacy, stronger regulatory oversight of supplement health claims, and recognition of how commercial interests can systematically distort scientific understanding.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The "sharks don't get cancer" myth operates within broader contexts of alternative medicine marketing, conservation biology, and science communication that extend far beyond the specific claim itself.

From a conservation perspective, the shark cartilage industry has contributed significantly to global shark population decline. An estimated 100 million sharks are killed annually, with cartilage harvesting representing a substantial portion of this mortality. The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) lists numerous shark species as threatened or endangered, with commercial exploitation for pseudomedical products among the contributing factors.

The conservation impact of the shark cartilage industry extends beyond direct mortality to ecosystem-level effects. Sharks function as apex predators in marine ecosystems, and their depletion can trigger cascading effects throughout food webs. The removal of sharks for cartilage harvesting contributes to these ecological disruptions, with consequences for marine biodiversity and fishery sustainability.

Specific species have been particularly affected by cartilage harvesting. The spiny dogfish shark, commonly used for cartilage extraction, has experienced severe population declines in the North Atlantic. Similarly, hammerhead sharks, valued for their large cartilage content, face extinction risks partly due to supplement industry demand.

The myth also reflects patterns in alternative medicine where dramatic claims about "natural" treatments gain popularity despite lacking scientific support. Shark cartilage supplements join a category of products marketed with implied or explicit anti-cancer properties, including laetrile, coffee enemas, and various herbal preparations, none of which have demonstrated efficacy in controlled trials.

The alternative medicine context reveals how the shark cancer myth fits into broader narratives about natural healing and pharmaceutical industry distrust. Proponents often frame shark cartilage as a suppressed cure that established medicine ignores due to financial interests. This conspiratorial framing helps insulate the myth from scientific criticism by reframing evidence-based skepticism as industry bias.

Educational implications extend into science literacy and critical thinking instruction. The shark cancer myth provides a case study in how commercial interests can distort biological understanding and why scientific literacy includes evaluating source credibility, financial conflicts of interest, and the quality of evidence supporting health claims.

The case demonstrates the importance of teaching students to distinguish between peer-reviewed research and commercial health claims. Educational approaches might emphasize how to identify conflicts of interest, evaluate research methodology, and recognize the difference between preliminary findings and established scientific consensus.

The persistence of this myth also highlights challenges in science communication. Accurate information about shark biology, cancer mechanisms, and clinical research requires more nuanced explanation than simple marketing messages, creating asymmetries in how true and false information spread through popular media.

Science communicators face the challenge of making accurate information as compelling as commercial health claims. The shark cancer myth succeeds partly because it offers simple solutions to complex problems, while accurate explanations of cancer biology and clinical research require more sophisticated understanding of biological processes.

Marine biologists working on shark conservation have noted that the cancer immunity myth complicates their educational efforts, as they must simultaneously convey accurate information about shark biology while countering commercially motivated misinformation that has become embedded in popular culture.

Conservation educators report that the myth creates confusion about shark biology more broadly, leading to misconceptions about shark physiology, behavior, and ecological roles. Correcting the cancer immunity myth often requires extensive re-education about basic shark biology and the nature of cancer as a biological phenomenon.

The case also illustrates broader problems with dietary supplement regulation in many jurisdictions, where products can be marketed with health implications without the rigorous testing required for pharmaceutical drugs, creating regulatory environments where myths can persist commercially even after scientific refutation.

The regulatory gap between supplements and pharmaceuticals allows shark cartilage products to remain on the market despite the absence of efficacy evidence. This regulatory framework essentially permits the commercial exploitation of scientific myths, creating market incentives for perpetuating misinformation.

International trade implications also emerge from the shark cartilage industry, as products manufactured in countries with lax supplement regulation can be exported to markets worldwide. This global dimension complicates efforts to address the myth through domestic regulatory action alone.

The economic scale of the problem is substantial, with the global shark cartilage supplement market valued at hundreds of millions of dollars annually. This economic entrenchment creates powerful interests opposed to myth correction and contributes to the persistence of misinformation despite scientific refutation.

Cultural factors also influence the myth's persistence across different societies. In some cultures, traditional beliefs about marine animals and natural healing create receptive environments for claims about shark cancer immunity. Understanding these cultural contexts is essential for developing effective communication strategies to counter the myth.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly addressed the specific claim "sharks don't get cancer" in their fact-checking database. This absence is notable given the claim's widespread circulation and commercial impact. The lack of Snopes coverage may reflect the claim's origin in commercial health literature rather than viral social media content, or it may indicate that the scientific consensus against the claim is so well-established that fact-checkers have focused on more contentious issues.

However, related claims about shark cartilage as a cancer treatment have been addressed by various medical fact-checking organizations, including the American Cancer Society, which explicitly warns against shark cartilage supplements and notes the absence of scientific evidence supporting their efficacy.

The Snopes approach to medical claims typically emphasizes peer-reviewed research and official health organization positions. In this case, the overwhelming scientific consensus against shark cancer immunity aligns with Snopes' methodology for evaluating health-related misinformation. The absence of specific Snopes coverage likely reflects the claim's clear refutation by established medical authorities rather than any ambiguity about its veracity.

Medical fact-checkers generally prioritize claims that circulate widely on social media or generate significant public confusion. The shark cancer myth, while commercially significant, may not have reached the viral thresholds that trigger comprehensive fact-checking attention. This gap highlights how some forms of misinformation—particularly those embedded in commercial health products—can persist without direct fact-checker intervention.

The broader pattern of Snopes medical fact-checking suggests they would likely classify this claim as "False" based on the substantial contradicting evidence from marine biology and oncology research. Their standard approach would emphasize the peer-reviewed literature documenting shark cancers and the absence of credible evidence supporting the immunity claim.
