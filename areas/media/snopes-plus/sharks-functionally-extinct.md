## The Claim

In July 2020, headlines in science journalism outlets and general media declared that reef sharks were becoming "functionally extinct." The claim originated from a study published in the journal Nature on July 22, 2020, and was amplified by press releases from Dalhousie University, where the lead researcher was based. Depending on which outlet a reader encountered, the headline might have read "One-Fifth of World's Coral Reefs Are Now Shark-Free" or "Reef Sharks Are Functionally Extinct in Parts of the World" or, in more alarming formulations, simply "Reef Sharks Are Going Extinct."

The claim spawned several questions from readers who wondered: Are reef sharks actually going extinct? Does "functionally extinct" mean the same thing as extinct? Does this apply to all sharks globally, or just certain species? How was the research conducted, and how reliable are its conclusions? These ambiguities — between functional extinction and true extinction, between reef-specific species and the broader shark category, between local disappearance and global extinction — constitute the empirical and epistemic core of what requires careful tracking here.

## What's Actually True

The core claim is largely accurate, but requires careful handling of the term "functionally extinct" and the scope of the study.

The research in question — MacNeil, Chapman, Cinner et al. (2020, "Global Status and Conservation Potential of Reef Sharks," *Nature*, July 22, 2020) — was the product of the Global FinPrint project, a conservation and research program that deployed approximately 15,000 baited remote underwater video stations (BRUVS) at hundreds of coral reef sites across 58 countries over a four-year data collection period. BRUVS stations consist of a camera mounted on a weighted frame with a standardised fish bait package (approximately one kilogram) suspended in front of the lens. Stations are deployed at the reef bottom, left to record for a set period, and retrieved; the footage is then analysed to count and identify shark species. The technique provides standardised, comparable survey data that does not depend on diver presence (which can alter animal behaviour) and does not require the substantial logistical investments of traditional survey methods.

Across more than 15,000 hours of video and records of 59 shark species, the study found that reef sharks were functionally absent from approximately one-in-five of the reef systems surveyed. In the six reef systems where the functional absence was most extreme — reefs in the Dominican Republic, the French West Indies, Kenya, Vietnam, the Windward Dutch Antilles, and Qatar — the researchers recorded only three individual reef sharks across 800 hours of combined survey video. This is a near-total absence.

"Functionally extinct" in this context means something specific and distinct from "extinct." A species is biologically extinct when the last individual of that species has died. A species is functionally extinct when its population has declined to the point where it can no longer perform its ecological role — when it cannot effectively influence the ecosystem processes that depend on its presence. A functionally extinct predator no longer meaningfully regulates prey populations, no longer shapes the foraging behaviour and spatial distribution of other species through the risk effects of its presence, and no longer contributes to nutrient cycling and energy transfer processes that depend on its hunting activity. The species may continue to exist in other locations; it may even be reproducing; but in the specific ecosystem studied, it has effectively disappeared as an ecological force.

The reef sharks studied include whitetip reef sharks (*Triaenodon obesus*), blacktip reef sharks (*Carcharhinus melanopterus*), lemon sharks (*Negaprion brevirostris*), great hammerheads (*Sphyrna mokarran*), and several other species that spend all or most of their lives in association with coral reef habitats. These are not all sharks; many shark species do not live near reefs and were not assessed in this study. The Snopes article correctly flagged this as the critical qualification: the finding of functional extinction in some reefs does not extend to all sharks worldwide, and headlines that used "sharks" without qualification were imprecise.

The study also documented the positive baseline: reef systems with strong shark conservation performed dramatically better. Countries where shark fishing was banned, where science-based fisheries management existed, or where designated shark sanctuaries had been established showed substantially higher shark abundance. Nations that had established shark sanctuaries recorded approximately 50% more sharks than comparable reef systems in nations without such protections. The researchers estimated that putting protective measures in place could produce a 15% increase in shark populations across currently depleted regions — not a full recovery, but a meaningful and achievable improvement.

## Why People Believe This (and Why the Precision Matters)

The term "functionally extinct" carries a specific technical meaning in conservation biology and a different, more alarming resonance in public communication. Understanding why that gap exists, and what it costs, is central to the epistemic work this entry is trying to do.

**The extinction word and its weight.** Extinction is among the most emotionally charged concepts in public science communication. It connotes finality, irreversibility, uniqueness of loss. When audiences encounter headlines using the word "extinct" or any of its derivatives, they activate a framework that includes these connotations. "Functionally extinct" sounds, to a non-specialist reader, like a slightly qualified form of regular extinction — extinction-but-not-quite, extinction on the way in. This reading is misleading in important ways: functional extinction is reversible in many cases; regular extinction is not. A reef system that currently supports no sharks might, with appropriate fishing restrictions, see shark populations recover within years to decades. The loss of the last individual of a species is geological in timescale.

**The koala correction as parallel case.** Snopes' own history with this story includes a relevant correction: an earlier version of the piece cited koalas as an example of another species that had become "functionally extinct," a claim that had circulated in 2019 following statements by the Australian Koala Foundation. The Snopes article was subsequently corrected after experts noted that while koala populations were severely stressed in some regions, the "functionally extinct" designation had not been formally applied by appropriate scientific authorities. The correction was made on July 27, 2020. This parallel case is instructive: the "functionally extinct" descriptor invites misapplication because it seems to provide a coherent, technically grounded way of expressing a severity of decline that ordinary language handles poorly. "Severely imperilled but not yet gone" is accurate but produces less clear communication than "functionally extinct," which is why the latter phrase is prone to adoption and misapplication.

**Overfishing and metric legibility.** The mechanism driving reef shark decline — overfishing driven by demand for shark fins and shark meat — is well-understood, consistently documented, and in some senses familiar from previous decades of marine conservation communication. The demand for shark fins is concentrated in East and Southeast Asian culinary traditions, particularly the shark fin soup served at formal banquets and celebrations; the demand for shark meat is more broadly distributed globally, including in European markets where shark meat is sometimes sold under obscuring product labels. Dulvy et al. (2014, *eLife Sciences*, published as part of the IUCN Shark Specialist Group analysis) estimated that approximately 100 million sharks are killed by human fishing activities annually, with overfishing remaining the primary driver of population decline for at least 25% of shark and ray species assessed by the IUCN — a quarter of assessed species threatened with extinction by overfishing alone.

**The political economy of "chum cam" science.** The Global FinPrint study used a technically elegant and logistically challenging methodology: 15,000 BRUVS deployments across 58 countries. The scale of this undertaking, and the clarity of the resulting data, represents a significant advance over previous assessments, which relied primarily on catch records. Catch records systematically underestimate decline because they measure what is caught — a variable that reflects both population abundance and the effort and technology applied to catching; a declining catch can mean fewer sharks or fewer boats, and the two are not always distinguishable without independent population data. BRUVS data, collected with standardised methodology, provides the independent population-level assessment that catch records cannot.

## Scientific Evidence: What the Literature Says

**The MacNeil et al. (2020) study and its design.** MacNeil, Chapman, Cinner, et al. (2020, *Nature*, vol. 583, pp. 801–806) constitutes the primary source for the claims. The study's design involved deploying 15,715 BRUVS stations at 371 reef systems across 58 countries, all affiliated with the Global FinPrint consortium led by Dalhousie University. The analysis included data from more than 15,000 individual videos. The statistical models used to estimate reef shark abundance and assess drivers of decline integrated data on fishing pressure, governance quality, human population density, market access, and conservation status. After controlling for habitat quality and environmental covariates, the study found that governance quality and fishing restriction status were the strongest positive predictors of reef shark abundance.

**Functional extinction in ecological theory.** The concept of functional extinction was developed and formalised in the ecological literature over the preceding two decades. Redford (1992, *BioScience*) introduced "the empty forest syndrome" — the observation that forest ecosystems could appear structurally intact while having lost the large animals that maintain their functional dynamics. Terborgh et al. (2001, *Science*) provided empirical demonstration that predator removal from island lake ecosystems in Venezuela produced dramatic trophic cascades: without puma and jaguar predation, herbivore populations exploded, destroying regenerating forest. This work established the theoretical baseline for understanding what it means for a predator to be functionally absent. Baum and Worm (2009, *Trends in Ecology and Evolution*) extended the analysis to marine food webs, demonstrating that large-bodied apex predators in oceanic systems — including sharks — exert behavioural and density effects on prey populations far beyond what simple abundance calculations would predict.

**Trophic cascade effects of reef shark removal.** Ruppert, Travers, Smith, Heartley, and Bradshaw (2013, *PLOS ONE*) published a meta-analysis of the effects of shark removal from reef ecosystems, documenting consistent trophic cascades including increased abundance of mesopredators (mid-level predators that sharks normally suppress), decreased abundance of herbivorous fish (which mesopredators preferentially consume), and consequent reductions in coral cover through increased algal grazing. The removal of reef sharks from a coral ecosystem is therefore not a simple subtraction of a species — it reorganises the entire trophic structure of the community, with effects cascading downward to the primary producers that form the architectural base of reef systems.

**Recovery potential.** Chapman, Frisk, Wiley, Feldheim, et al. (2011, *PLOS ONE*) documented the recovery potential of lemon shark populations in Bimini, Bahamas, following protective measures, finding that populations could recover meaningfully within a decade under appropriate conditions. MacNeil et al. (2020) estimated a 15% global increase in reef shark abundance achievable through protective measures, representing a best-case scenario rather than full recovery but establishing that functional recovery of the ecosystem role is not merely theoretical. Countries cited as positive examples include the Bahamas, Australia, French Polynesia, and the United States — all of which implemented some form of shark fishing restriction combined with effective enforcement.

## The Epistemic Gap: What Fact-Checking Doesn't Settle

The Snopes verdict — "Mostly True" — accurately captures the relationship between the claim and the evidence: the functional extinction finding is supported by rigorous research, but the headline-level claim as circulated was imprecise about scope (all sharks vs. reef sharks) and about the specific meaning of "functional extinction."

What the fact-check does not address, and what represents genuine scientific frontier territory, concerns the timescales and mechanisms of potential recovery. The Global FinPrint study documented the current state of reef shark populations and identified governance and conservation policy as the primary determinants of population health. But the study was cross-sectional — a snapshot across many reefs at a single period — rather than longitudinal, and the transition dynamics between functionally absent and functionally present remain incompletely characterised. Specifically: how long does it take a reef system to functionally recover reef shark populations once overfishing pressure is removed? What is the minimum viable shark density at which trophic regulation effects begin to manifest? Are the trophic cascades triggered by shark absence reversible, or does the reorganised mesopredator ecology create stable alternative equilibria that resist recovery even when shark numbers increase?

These questions have partial answers in the existing literature — Robbins, Hisano, Connolly, and Choat (2006, *Science*) demonstrated resistance to recovery following trophic reorganisation in coral reef herbivore communities; Heithaus, Frid, Wirsing, and Worm (2008, *Trends in Ecology and Evolution*) argued for "landscape of fear" effects mediated by shark presence that require sustained shark populations to produce — but remain insufficiently resolved to support confident predictions about specific reef systems.

The political economy gap is also significant: the MacNeil et al. study identified governance quality, market access, and poverty as among the primary drivers of reef shark depletion via overfishing. Nations with poor governance and subsistence fishing pressure cannot simply transplant the conservation policies of the Bahamas or French Polynesia. The paper's identification of governance as the key variable is a sociological as much as an ecological finding, and it points toward a body of literature on conservation governance, environmental justice, and the distributive dimensions of species protection that ecological fact-checks rarely engage with directly but which shape whether any conservation recommendation is practically achievable.

## Verdict

**Mostly True.** Reef sharks are becoming functionally extinct in significant portions of their global coral reef habitat. A peer-reviewed study published in *Nature* (MacNeil et al., 2020) using 15,000 underwater camera deployments across 58 countries found reef sharks functionally absent from approximately one-in-five reef systems surveyed. This finding is robust, methodologically sound, and scientifically significant.

The important qualifications: the "functionally extinct" designation refers to specific reef ecosystems where the sharks have locally disappeared, not to the global extinction of reef shark species. "Functional extinction" means the species can no longer perform its ecological role in that reef; it does not mean the last individual has died. The study focused on reef-associated species specifically (whitetip, blacktip, lemon, great hammerhead, among others), not all sharks globally. And crucially, the study documented that recovery is possible: reef systems in countries with effective shark conservation policies supported dramatically more sharks, demonstrating that the decline is not irreversible.
