# Sick vampire bats socially distance themselves from the colony


## 1. The Claim

At various points during the COVID-19 pandemic — and in the subsequent years of public discourse about pandemic mitigation — a claim about vampire bats began circulating with particular enthusiasm across science communication channels, popular press science sections, and social media accounts devoted to remarkable animal behaviour: that when vampire bats feel ill, they voluntarily withdraw from their social group and avoid close contact with healthy colony members, engaging in a form of spontaneous social distancing long before human public health authorities issued any such recommendations to human populations.

The claim presents the common vampire bat (*Desmodus rotundus*), already one of the more culturally charged animals in the popular imagination given its blood-feeding lifestyle and its association with supernatural mythology, as an unlikely model of disease-aware social behaviour. Where bats in general have been entangled in popular culture with horror, contagion, and disease — an association that became particularly fraught during COVID-19 given early scientific hypotheses about zoonotic transmission — this claim inverts the frame: rather than vectors of disease carelessly contaminating healthy populations, vampire bats emerge as sophisticated practitioners of the same quarantine logic that human epidemiologists recommend for preventing disease spread.

The claim typically arrives citing a specific study — usually identified as having been published in 2020 and led by researchers including Gerald Carter at Ohio State University and Simon Ripperger, at that time affiliated with the American Museum of Natural History and the Free University of Berlin — and generally describes an experimental design in which bats were made to feel sick using an immune-stimulating compound and their subsequent social behaviour was monitored using miniaturised tracking technology.

The claim is striking on multiple levels: it concerns a genuinely surprising and counterintuitive finding about a largely misunderstood animal; it has obvious resonance with the pandemic moment in which it circulated; and it raises deep questions about the evolutionary origins of social distancing behaviour and whether what looks like a sophisticated public health strategy might have ancient roots in the behavioural repertoire of social animals generally.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The study.** The primary research underlying the claim is a paper by Gerry Carter, Rachel Page, Gerald Wilkinson, and colleagues titled "Sick bats stay home alone: Experimental disease causes decreased roosting associations in vampire bats," or a closely related version of that title, published in *Behavioral Ecology* in 2020 (Carter, G.G., Ripperger, S.P., Wilkinson, G.S., & Page, R.A., 2020, *Behavioral Ecology*). The research was conducted at a field site in Trinidad, using a captive colony of wild-caught common vampire bats housed in conditions that preserved natural roosting arrangements — a critical methodological point, as the study examined behaviour in a group context that approximated natural colony structure.

The experimental design was careful and methodologically sophisticated. Researchers injected a subset of bats with lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a bacterial cell-wall component that stimulates the innate immune system and produces a sickness response — elevated body temperature, fatigue, reduced activity, and other features of the sickness behaviour syndrome — without causing actual infection with a live pathogen. This produces "sick-feeling" bats whose disease cannot spread to colony members, allowing researchers to distinguish behavioural changes in sick animals from any possible disease-transmission effects that might produce behavioural changes in healthy colony members. A control group received saline injections and did not exhibit sickness behaviour.

The sick bats were monitored using lightweight proximity-logging devices that maintained continuous records of which bats were in close spatial proximity to which other bats throughout the roosting and activity period. The key finding was that LPS-treated bats had significantly fewer, shorter, and less spatially close associations with other colony members during the period in which they were experiencing sickness behaviour. They spent more time alone — roosted apart from the main cluster — and were contacted by and initiated contact with colony members at substantially lower rates than the saline-control bats and than their own baseline recorded behaviour. The effect was most pronounced during the acute phase of the sickness response and diminished as the LPS effects wore off.

**The behavioural mechanism.** The social withdrawal observed in sick vampire bats reflects a phenomenon that behavioural ecologists and evolutionary psychologists call the "behavioural immune system" — a concept formalised by Mark Schaller and colleagues (Schaller, M., & Park, J., 2011, *Current Directions in Psychological Science*) referring to evolved behavioural repertoires that reduce disease exposure risk in social animals through avoidance, withdrawal, and disgust-based social exclusion. The behavioural immune system operates alongside the physiological immune system (white blood cells, antibodies, inflammatory responses) as a complementary line of defence — the physiological system deals with pathogens once they have entered the body; the behavioural system attempts to prevent entry in the first place.

The sickness behaviour syndrome — which includes reduced activity, social withdrawal, anorexia, sleep, and huddling — has been observed in a very wide range of vertebrate species and is understood as an evolved response that serves multiple functions simultaneously: conserving energy for immune activity, reducing exposure of vulnerable immune-compromised animals to predators and social conflict, and reducing the probability of transmitting the pathogen to social partners. The vampire bat study contributes a particularly clean experimental demonstration of this last function because the research design isolates it from confounds.

**Why vampire bats specifically.** Common vampire bats are among the most highly social of all bat species. Females form stable long-term roosting associations, engage in reciprocal food-sharing (sharing blood meals by regurgitation with hungry roostmates who will reciprocate in the future), and have coalition structures that persist across years. This social organisation is well-documented in the research of Gerald Wilkinson and colleagues going back to the 1980s, with the food-sharing work published in *Nature* (Wilkinson, G.S., 1984, *Nature*) being among the most-cited demonstrations of reciprocal altruism outside the primate literature. The richness and stability of vampire bat social networks makes them an excellent system in which to study socially mediated disease behaviour — there is sufficient variety in relationship types (strong associates, weak associates, unfamiliar colony members) to examine whether sickness-related social withdrawal is selective.

The Carter et al. study found evidence suggesting that sick bats do not withdraw uniformly from all social partners but may maintain more contact with close associates than with peripheral colony members — a pattern consistent with a model in which sick animals balance disease-avoidance by others (which might be expected to be stronger toward close associates, who have more to lose from infection) against the maintenance of key social bonds. The precise selectivity of the withdrawal remains an active area of investigation.

**Robustness of the finding.** The general finding — that social animals reduce their contact rates when experiencing sickness behaviour — has been replicated in many species beyond vampire bats. Manduca sexta caterpillars aggregate less when sick (Brandt & Barbosa, 2002, *Ecological Entomology*). Lobsters infected with viruses alter their social behaviour (Bhattacharya & Bhattacharya, 2017, *Journal of Experimental Marine Biology and Ecology*). Mice and rats consistently reduce social investigation and interaction during LPS-induced sickness (Dantzer & Kelley, 2007, *Trends in Neurosciences*). The vampire bat result is thus consistent with a general pattern while contributing a particularly vivid and tractable model system with rich known social network structure.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — Self-Medication, Social Architecture, and the Ecology of Contagion

The vampire bat social distancing finding, while compelling, opens onto questions that are harder to answer than the factual question of whether it happens.

**The question of mechanism and agency.** The claim as it circulates typically describes sick bats as "voluntarily" withdrawing or "choosing" to socially distance. The research supports the finding that sick bats have fewer social contacts; it is more circumspect about the mechanistic description of this as voluntary choice. The sickness behaviour syndrome produces a cluster of physiological and behavioural changes through well-characterised neurobiological pathways — chiefly through the action of cytokines (interleukin-1β, tumour necrosis factor-α, interleukin-6) on brain regions including the hypothalamus and basal ganglia (Dantzer, R., O'Connor, J.C., Freund, G.G., Johnson, R.W., & Kelley, K.W., 2008, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*). Social withdrawal may be partly a direct product of fatigue and reduced locomotor activity rather than a goal-directed strategy to avoid transmitting disease, partly a response to reduced attractiveness to social partners who behaviorally avoid sick individuals, and partly — perhaps — the product of something like goal-directed pathogen management.

Distinguishing among these mechanisms requires experiments that the vampire bat study was not designed to perform, and the distinction matters for how we interpret the finding. A bat that withdraws from colony contact because it is too weak and fatigued to move to the roosting cluster is doing something epistemically different from a bat that monitors its infectious status, estimates transmission probability, and adjusts its social proximity to minimise disease spread. Both would produce reduced contact rates in the data, but only the latter represents anything analogous to deliberate social distancing in the public health sense.

The honest assessment is that the mechanistic question is unresolved and that the behavioural data alone cannot distinguish these scenarios. This is not a criticism of the research, which was not designed to address mechanism; it is a specification of where the limits of current knowledge lie.

**Disease ecology and the adaptive value of social distancing.** Regardless of mechanism, the existence of reduced contact rates in sick social animals raises a profound question in disease ecology: to what extent do the evolved behaviours of hosts shape the transmission dynamics of their pathogens? This is a question that epidemiological models have historically underdeveloped. Standard mathematical models of infectious disease transmission (SIR-type compartmental models beginning with Kermack & McKendrick, 1927, *Proceedings of the Royal Society of London*) treat social contact structure as a parameter — sometimes a network parameter, but still a background variable — rather than as something that adapts in real time to the infectious state of individuals in the population.

If sick individuals reduce their contact rates in a manner that the pathogen cannot easily counteract — because the sickness behaviour responses are triggered by general immune activation rather than pathogen-specific signals — then the evolved behavioural immune system may constitute a significant and undermodelled component of natural disease resistance in social species. A pathogen that infects an animal and triggers the sickness behaviour syndrome may thereby reduce its own transmission probability, creating an evolutionary dynamic in which the pathogen's virulence is constrained by its own induction of host social withdrawal. This is a form of evolutionary arms race between pathogen transmission interests and host disease-avoidance interests that has received growing theoretical attention but limited empirical study at the resolution that the vampire bat system makes possible.

**Individual variation and social network position.** Research by Carter and Page using longitudinal social network data from vampire bat colonies has documented that individual bats vary substantially and consistently in their centrality within the social network — some bats are highly connected to many colony members; others maintain a small number of closer but fewer relationships. This variation has consequences for both disease transmission dynamics and for the effects of social withdrawal on individual welfare. A highly connected bat that becomes sick and withdraws may have a larger effect on transmission dynamics than a peripherally connected bat. The same bat may also have more to lose socially from withdrawal, having invested more in building and maintaining a large social network. These asymmetries create a complex adaptive landscape for the evolution of sickness behaviour that is empirically tractable in the vampire bat system and largely unexplored.

The intersection of social network theory, disease ecology, and behavioural immunology represented by this work is genuinely emerging, and the vampire bat system — with its long-term natural history, tractable social network structure, and the experimental manipulation tools available to Carter and colleagues — may be one of the most productive field systems for developing it. The observation that sick bats reduce social contact is thus both a confirmed specific finding and an entry point into a domain of research whose full implications are still being worked out.


## 4. Verdict

**True — experimentally confirmed and conceptually robust.** Sick vampire bats do reduce their social associations with colony members during periods of sickness behaviour, as demonstrated by Carter, Ripperger, Wilkinson, Page, and colleagues (2020, *Behavioral Ecology*) in a carefully controlled experiment using LPS-induced sickness in a natural-colony context. The finding is consistent with the broader behavioural immune system literature across many vertebrate taxa, gains support from the extensive pre-existing work on vampire bat social networking by Wilkinson and others, and is methodologically sound.

The popular-communication version of the finding requires one significant qualification: the claim that bats "voluntarily" socially distance or "choose" to isolate implies goal-directed agency that the study's data cannot confirm and that the underlying neurobiology does not clearly support. Reduced social contact during sickness is documented; the mechanism — whether it is primarily fatigue-driven, socially driven by withdrawal of healthy bats from the sick individual, or goal-directed pathogen management — is an open empirical question. The observation stands; the interpretation as a parallel to deliberate human social distancing requires more care.

The claim is true in the strongest sense: the specific behaviour described has been empirically observed and published in a peer-reviewed journal. It is neither an urban legend, a misinterpretation, nor a projection of human behaviour onto animal subjects without empirical basis.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The vampire bat social distancing finding captured enormous public attention when it appeared in mid-2020 because it arrived at a moment when millions of people were themselves navigating novel social distancing requirements and were acutely alert to any scientific validation or parallel for the practice. The timing is worth examining as a media phenomenon in its own right.

Science journalism during the early months of the COVID-19 pandemic saw a pattern in which animal behaviour findings — particularly findings about non-human animals employing strategies analogous to human pandemic responses — received coverage disproportionate to their significance within their home disciplines. Vampire bats were joined by lobsters, insects, and various other species in a loose genre of "nature was already doing social distancing" reporting. This coverage served a cultural function — normalising and naturalising public health recommendations by connecting them to the most ancient and fundamental level of biological organisation — but it also risked overstatement.

The vampire bat research is the most epistemically solid of these examples because it is based on an actual LPS experiment with a control condition and continuous proximity logging rather than on anecdotal observation or extrapolation from captive studies. The research team's primary contributions are verifiable, peer-reviewed, and were conducted by researchers with pre-existing deep expertise in vampire bat social ecology. This distinguishes it from some other viral-pandemic-era animal behaviour claims.

More broadly, the finding participates in a growing recognition that the traditional division between individual immunity and population-level disease dynamics may be incomplete. Evolutionary biologist William Hamilton's "kin selection" framework for understanding the evolution of altruistic behaviour (Hamilton, W.D., 1964, *Journal of Theoretical Biology*) extended beyond the individual organism as the unit of selection; subsequent work by David Sloan Wilson and others on multilevel selection theory has extended this further. The behavioural immune system as it operates in social animals may constitute a multilevel adaptation — one that serves the individual (reducing exposure to dangerous pathogens at a time when the organism is already immunologically vulnerable) and the group (reducing transmission within the colony) simultaneously, and where these interests partially align rather than conflict.

Epidemiologists studying the emergence and spread of novel zoonotic pathogens — including researchers focused on bat-borne viruses given the documented reservoir relationships between bats and several families of viruses of human concern — have noted that the evolved disease-avoidance behaviours of host species may substantially modify transmission dynamics in ways that standard surveillance and modelling approaches do not capture. Studying these behaviours in tractable systems like vampire bats under controlled conditions is one of the methodological approaches that may help close this gap.

For science communicators, the vampire bat story illustrates the productive potential and the associated risk of translating animal behaviour research for public audiences during a public health crisis. When done carefully — citing the specific publication, noting the experimental conditions, qualifying the mechanism question, acknowledging the distance between LPS-induced sickness and natural infection — the story enriches public understanding of disease ecology, animal cognition, and the evolutionary depth of social behaviour. When done carelessly — presenting bats as deliberate, human-like decision-makers practicing social distancing as a conscious strategy — it risks anthropomorphism and overstatement that leaves the general reader with a false model of the finding.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated this claim as "True" based on the Carter et al. (2020) peer-reviewed publication and the team's supporting documentation. The fact-checking procedure for claims of this type — empirical animal behaviour findings reported in peer-reviewed journals and then simplified in popular communication — is generally more tractable than for contested political or historical claims, because the primary source is a published paper that can be read and assessed on its own terms.

The epistemically responsible approach involves three verification steps: (1) confirming that the paper cited exists and was published in the journal identified; (2) identifying what the paper actually measured and claimed, as distinct from what popular communication attributed to it; and (3) assessing whether the popular communication's version accurately represents the paper's claims or whether it introduced distortions through simplification, anthropomorphism, or scope expansion.

For the vampire bat social distancing claim, step (2) is the most important: the paper demonstrated reduced social contact in LPS-treated bats; popular communication sometimes described this as bats "choosing" or "deciding" to isolate, which encodes a mechanistic claim not supported by the paper. Identifying this distinction is the kind of scientific literacy task that fact-checkers working with empirical science claims increasingly need to perform — not just checking whether a paper exists, but checking whether the popular communication's interpretation is a fair rendering of what the paper found.

For general audiences, the claim as an illustration of science communication: the vampire bat finding is genuinely remarkable, genuinely confirmed, and genuinely illuminating about the intersection of animal social behaviour and disease ecology. The things that make it remarkable — that an animal whose evolutionary lineage predates Homo sapiens by millions of years deploys what looks functionally like social distancing — are real. The mechanisms that generate this behaviour are different from the rational deliberation that human social distancing involves, and those differences matter. Holding both of these truths simultaneously — the finding is real and the popularised interpretation is partially imprecise — is the accurate position, and it is a more interesting position than either dismissing the claim as hype or accepting the "bats are consciously doing social distancing" version uncritically.
