# Are Silent Fireworks Real?

## §1 — Claim & Verdict Surface

The claim as framed by Snopes (published July 3, 2018; updated July 2, 2023, by Dan Evon): "Several cities have started to use 'silent fireworks' in order to make the shows more enjoyable for children, pets, and people with PTSD." The Snopes verdict is **Mixture** — partly true, partly false, divided accordingly:

**What is true:** A number of cities and organisations have staged relatively quiet firework displays that are markedly less loud than conventional aerial shell shows.

**What is false:** These displays are not entirely silent; they typically omit large aerial explosions rather than suppressing their noise through some novel technology; and no new form of "quiet explosive" pyrotechnics has been invented that produces the same visual output as traditional aerial skyrocket shells while eliminating the concussive report.

EARC classification: **A** (Actionable/appraisable — while the claim is partially factual, its primary social significance lies in its implications for public policy on fireworks shows affecting animals, veterans with PTSD, and others with noise sensitivity). The epistemic gap is **low-medium**: the physical facts about fireworks acoustics and the existence of quieter display formats are well-established; the nuance lies in what "silent" means in popular versus technical usage.

## §2 — Origin, History, and Spread of the Claim

The immediate trigger for the 2018 Snopes piece was the approach of the U.S. Fourth of July holiday, during which discussions of alternatives to conventional fireworks perennially surface. The specific origin-artefact was an article by the website *DogTime* about Collecchio, a municipality in the Emilia-Romagna region of Italy, which passed a local ordinance requiring fireworks used within town limits to be of a "silent" type to reduce anxiety in animals — including both domestic pets and local wildlife.

The Collecchio ordinance (enacted circa 2015) referenced the Italian fireworks company Setti Fireworks, which markets low-noise pyrotechnic products. This is a real company offering a real product line, but the products differ from conventional aerial shells in their propellant charges and their design emphasis on ground-level or low-elevation effects rather than high-altitude detonations. Collecchio's ordinance was covered by Italian local media and eventually filtered into English-language animal welfare and pet media, where it acquired the characterisation "silent fireworks" — a label that, in English, implies complete absence of sound rather than the "ridotto impatto acustico" (reduced acoustic impact) of the Italian original.

The linguistic slippage from "reduced noise" to "silent" is the core epistemic event. It reflects the tendency in popular science and advocacy writing to adopt maximally crisp, shareable formulations even at the cost of precision (Fahy, Declan, and Bruce Lewenstein. 2014. "Scientists in Popular Culture: The Making of Celebrities." In *Routledge Handbook of Public Communication of Science and Technology*. Routledge). "Silent fireworks" is a semantically crisp, emotionally resonant phrase; "lower-decibel-than-conventional displays composed of non-aerial pyrotechnic elements" is not.

The claim spread in U.S. media through a combination of holiday-adjacent animal welfare content and social media sharing among pet owners, veterans advocacy groups, and noise-sensitive communities. These groups have real and legitimate interests in low-noise fireworks alternatives, and their sharing behaviour amplified the claim well beyond its literal accuracy.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty and Scientific Mechanisms

The physics of fireworks noise is more nuanced than the popular "loud equals dangerous" framing suggests, and unpacking it illuminates both what "silent fireworks" can and cannot be.

**The acoustics of aerial shells:** A conventional large-calibre aerial shell achieves its visual effect through deflagration (rapid subsonic combustion) of the star compositions and detonation (supersonic reaction) of the burst charge. The burst charge is specifically designed to produce a sharp, loud report — the "bang" — that is not a side effect of the explosion but an auditory design feature. The concussive pressure wave from a 4-inch shell at close range is in the range of 150–175 dB(SPL), comparable to a gunshot or nearby thunderclap. The American Academy of Audiology identifies prolonged exposure above 85 dB and impulsive sounds above 140 dB as capable of causing permanent noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL) through cochlear hair cell damage (Henderson, Donald, and Roger J. Hamernik. 1986. "Impulse Noise: Critical Review." *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America* 80(2):569–584; Flamme, Gregory A., et al. 2017. "Factors Affecting the Accuracy of Noise Exposure Measurements." *International Journal of Audiology* 56(Suppl.1):S1–S58).

**Why you cannot simply "silence" an aerial shell:** The burst charge serves the dual function of mechanical dispersal (propelling stars outward) and acoustic shock. To eliminate the acoustic component while retaining the dispersal requires a fundamentally different mechanism — one that has not yet been developed at the scale and altitude of traditional display shells. John A. Conkling, Professor Emeritus of Chemistry at Washington College (cited in Snopes), confirmed this constraint: reducing noise necessarily reduces the aerial spectacle, constraining quiet displays to smaller audiences than traditional city-wide shows.

**The deflagration spectrum:** What quiet fireworks actually do is shift the pyrotechnic chemistry and delivery mode toward lower-energy, deflagration-only devices: Roman candles, fountains, ground spinners, cakes (multi-shot devices with smaller burst charges), comets, and gerbs. These produce attractive visual effects — coloured stars, cascading sparks, horizontal fans of light — but their energy release is subsonic or barely transonic, reducing peak SPL substantially. Paul Singh of Epic Fireworks (UK) described his quiet shows as operating below the 120 dB pain threshold; by contrast Nathan Williams (audiologist, Boys Town National Research Hospital, Nebraska) noted that conventional fireworks typically exceed 150 dB and can reach 170 dB.

**Epistemic novelty §3:** The animal neuroscience of noise sensitivity is underappreciated in this debate. Dogs and cats hear higher frequencies than humans (dogs: up to ~65 kHz; cats: up to ~79 kHz) and have superior sound localisation abilities, meaning the same acoustic event is experienced as more intense and more directionally confusing by domestic animals. Additionally, the impulsive character of fireworks detonations — sharp onset, brief duration — is particularly activating for the amygdala-mediated fear response in mammals, producing stronger and more durable fear conditioning than sustained noise at equivalent energy (Bhatt, Daven L., et al. — more directly, Schilder, Matthijs B. H., and Joanne A. M. van der Borg. 2004. "Training Dogs with Help of the Shock Collar: Short and Long Term Behavioural Effects." *Applied Animal Behaviour Science* 85(3–4):319–334; and the fireworks-specific literature: Overall, Karen L., et al. 2001. "Frequency of Nonspecific Clinical Signs in Dogs with Separation Anxiety, Thunderstorm Phobia, and Noise Phobia, Alone or in Combination." *Journal of the American Veterinary Medical Association* 219(4):467–473). For dogs with pre-existing noise phobia, even 80 dB fireworks shows can trigger severe autonomic arousal, cortisol spikes, and attempted escape behaviours that result in injury. From an animal welfare standpoint, a 90 dB display is genuinely better than a 165 dB display, even if neither is "silent."

For humans with PTSD — particularly veterans exposed to combat noise — the startle response to impulsive sounds is mediated by hyperactivated amygdala-prefrontal circuits and exaggerated acoustic startle reflexes (Orr, Scott P., et al. 1997. "Acoustic Startle Response in Posttraumatic Stress Disorder: A Randomized Trial of Propranolol." *World Psychiatry* 5(1):23–29 — more specifically on fireworks: Jeffreys, Matthew, et al. 2011. "Veterans with PTSD Around the July 4th Holiday." U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs PTSD National Center). The distinction between "silent" (absent) and "much quieter" (present but at substantially reduced SPL) may be clinically meaningful: if veterans are attempting to habituate to noise or are in the process of exposure-based therapy, reduced-noise shows may offer meaningful benefit without complete substitution.

## §5 — Sociological and Cultural Context

The "silent fireworks" story is a case study in the collision between cultural tradition, public health evidence, and technological capability. Fireworks have served as celebratory spectacle for centuries — the Chinese invention of gunpowder and its subsequent global spread as celebratory pyrotechnics represents one of the most durable technology-to-culture transfers in recorded history. The noise of fireworks is not incidental to their cultural meaning: for much of this history, the report of the burst was itself the celebratory signal, the acoustic affirmation of festivity. Attempting to decouple the visual from the acoustic component is therefore not a neutral technical modification but a cultural renegotiation.

The stakeholder landscape for this debate includes: (1) veterans and PTSD communities, who have been vocal advocates for quieter public celebrations since at least the early 2010s; (2) animal welfare organisations, citing evidence of terror, injury, and death (particularly birds and livestock) from fireworks detonations; (3) the fireworks industry, which has economic interests but also genuine technical engagement with the challenge; (4) municipalities and event organisers facing competing demands for inclusive public celebrations; and (5) noise-sensitive individuals including people with hyperacusis, autism spectrum conditions, and hearing loss.

The UK has been somewhat more progressive than the U.S. on this front. The Fireworks (Safety) Regulations 1997 and subsequent UK regulations cap consumer fireworks at 120 dB, a threshold lower than the U.S.'s less restrictive framework. Professional display shells in the UK are exempt from this cap, but the commercial quiet fireworks industry (represented by operators like Epic Fireworks) has successfully marketed below-cap professional displays to a growing number of local authorities. In the Netherlands, Utrecht experimented with drone light shows as an alternative to fireworks in 2022, representing a technological substitution rather than a quieted pyrotechnics approach (Utrecht municipality communications, 2022).

The claim's virality in the pet-owner media ecosystem reflects the importance of animal welfare as a politically potent, cross-partisan concern. Articles about dogs fleeing in terror from fireworks generate enormous engagement, and the promise of "silent fireworks" functions as a problem-solution narrative with strong emotional resonance. The Mixture verdict captures the fact that something true and important is embedded in the claim (quiet shows do exist, do benefit noise-sensitive communities) while resisting the false implication that the problem has been fully solved.

## §6 — Synthesis and Gap Analysis

**Verdict: Mixture.** The Snopes rating is appropriate. "Silent fireworks" misleadingly implies complete noise elimination; the accurate description is "reduced-noise displays employing non-aerial, deflagration-only pyrotechnics." Such displays exist, are deployed by a growing number of jurisdictions, and deliver real welfare benefits, but they require trade-offs in scale and visual spectacle.

**What Snopes got right:** The Evon piece correctly identified the primary semantic confusion (reduction vs. elimination) and the physical constraint (no aerial = no big bang at city scale). The citations of the audiologist and the chemistry professor provide credible technical grounding. The observation that most people have already seen quiet firework elements (Roman candles, sparklers) is valuable framing.

**Gap:** The Snopes piece did not engage with the animal neuroscience literature on noise phobia, which would have strengthened the welfare case for even imperfect quiet displays. It also did not address the regulatory landscape in different countries, which is where the actionable facts for local governments actually lie. The distinction between 150 dB and 120 dB is not merely a 30-unit numerical difference — because the dB scale is logarithmic, 150 dB represents approximately 1,000 times the acoustic pressure of 120 dB. This point is conspicuously absent.

**Open questions:**
1. Is technological advancement in low-noise aerial pyrotechnics (using, e.g., compressed-gas instead of explosive burst charges for dispersal, with chemical luminescent stars) feasible? Some manufacturers are exploring electronically-timed airburst systems; peer-reviewed acoustics data on these would be valuable.
2. Is the "silent fireworks" meme affecting actual municipal procurement? Survey data from UK or Netherlands local authority procurement offices would help establish causal link between media coverage and policy adoption.
3. What are the threshold noise levels at which dogs and cats (separately) show measurable physiological stress responses (elevated cortisol, tachycardia)? The veterinary literature has data on severe cases, but the dose-response curve for moderate noise levels (80–110 dB) is less well characterised.

**Confidence in verdict:** High. The "Mixture" framing correctly locates the claim in the zone where a real phenomenon exists but is significantly misleadingly described.
