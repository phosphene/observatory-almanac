# Did 'The Simpsons' Predict Kamala Harris Would Be VP?

## 1. The Claim

On January 20, 2021, as Kamala Harris was inaugurated as the first woman, first Black American, and first South Asian American vice president of the United States, social media users began circulating images that appeared to show the animated sitcom "The Simpsons" had predicted her ascension. The posts pointed to the episode "Bart to the Future," which originally aired in March 2000, in which character Lisa Simpson succeeds Donald Trump as president. In the episode's closing scene, Lisa wears a purple suit and stands behind a presidential podium — an outfit that social media users claimed echoed the purple ensemble Harris wore during her swearing-in ceremony.

The claim joined a long tradition of "Simpsons predictions" that have circulated on the internet for years. Previous iterations claimed the show had predicted Donald Trump's presidency, the death of George Floyd, the September 11 attacks, the Ebola outbreak, the assassination attempt on Donald Trump, and dozens of other events. The idea that "The Simpsons" possesses some kind of prophetic power has become a fixture of internet culture, spawning countless memes, listicles, and YouTube videos.

Snopes examined the Kamala Harris prediction claim in January 2021 and rated it "False." The article, written by Bethania Palma, concluded that no episode of the show ever predicted Harris's vice presidency. The article noted that the "Bart to the Future" episode predates Harris's entry into politics — she did not run for office until 2003, three years after the episode aired — and that the episode never mentions her. The purple suit worn by Lisa Simpson, Snopes argued, was a coincidence that "could probably stand in for any woman who might potentially win the presidency or vice presidency and also at some point wore purple."

At first glance, the claim is straightforward and easily debunked. But the persistence of this particular variety of prediction claim — and the broader cultural phenomenon of "Simpsons predictions" — deserves deeper examination.

The fact that the Harris claim emerged on the very day of her inauguration is significant. It was not a spontaneous discovery; it was a coordinated production, with multiple accounts posting similar images within hours of each other. This pattern — rapid, synchronized dissemination of prediction claims timed to coincide with major news events — suggests an organized element to what appears to be organic viral content. Understanding the production side of misinformation is as important as understanding its consumption. Why do these claims continue to circulate despite repeated debunking? What do they reveal about how people think about media, prediction, and causality? And what responsibility do fact-checkers and media organizations bear for understanding the underlying dynamics that make such claims compelling?

## 2. What's Actually True

The "Bart to the Future" episode of "The Simpsons" aired on March 19, 2000. In the episode, Bart gets into trouble at school and is sent to a disciplinary program for disruptive students. While there, he has a vision of the future in which he is a feckless, unemployed adult living in Lisa's house. Lisa, in this future vision, has become president of the United States, having succeeded a previous President Trump. The episode contains the line, "As you know, we've inherited quite a budget crunch from President Trump," spoken by Lisa. This line is the basis for the frequently cited "Simpsons predicted Trump" claim.

The episode does not mention Kamala Harris. It does not depict a vice president. The purple suit worn by Lisa Simpson in the future sequence — which became the basis for the Harris prediction claim — is a visual detail that was chosen by animators working two decades before Harris entered national politics. Lisa Simpson is depicted as president, not vice president. If the episode predicted anything, it predicted a female president succeeding Trump, not a female vice president serving alongside a Democratic president.

The claim that the show predicted Harris is based on a chain of associations rather than direct evidence. The chain works like this: "The Simpsons" predicted Trump (the "Bart to the Future" episode mentions President Trump). Therefore, "The Simpsons" can predict the future. Therefore, any visual similarity between a Simpsons character and a real-world figure must be a prediction. This chain is logically unsound at every link.

The Trump prediction itself is less impressive than it appears. Trump had been a public figure for decades before the episode aired. He had flirted with running for president since at least 1999, and in 2000 — the same year the episode aired — he did run briefly as a third-party candidate. Simpsons writer Dan Greaney told The Hollywood Reporter that the line about President Trump was "pitched because it was consistent with the vision of America going insane." It was a satirical comment on American politics, not a prediction. As Greaney put it, "It was a warning to America."

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes's article correctly identifies the episode, traces the origin of the prediction claim, and provides clear reasoning for why the claim is false. The article establishes that Harris is never mentioned in the episode, that she had not entered politics when the episode aired, and that the purple suit is a coincidence. The rating of "False" is appropriate and well-supported.

The article also usefully contextualizes the Harris prediction claim within the broader pattern of Simpsons prediction claims. It references previous Snopes fact-checks on Trump predictions and other claimed predictions, creating a record that demonstrates the recurring nature of this misinformation pattern. The article notes that "The Simpsons has produced hundreds of episodes over 32 seasons, so in all that time, it seems likely that the show would portray events that appear to parallel real-world events" — a concise statement of the statistical probability argument against these claims.

However, the Snopes article, like most single-claim fact-checks, does not fully explore the structural drivers of the phenomenon it addresses. The "Simpsons predictions" meme is not primarily about "The Simpsons" at all; it is about how humans perceive patterns, how social media algorithms reward shareable content, and how the line between coincidence and causality can become blurred in a networked information environment.

First, the article does not address the specific mechanics of how Simpsons prediction claims are created and spread. Many of the most viral "predictions" are based on images that have been photoshopped or taken out of context. For example, a previous Snopes article cited in this one notes that a claim about the show predicting Trump's campaign launch used a still image that was actually taken from a short YouTube video posted after the real-world event occurred. The Harris claim similarly relies on a still image that is stripped of its narrative context — viewers see Lisa in a purple suit but do not see the episode's actual plot, which has nothing to do with Kamala Harris.

Second, the article does not examine why "Simpsons predictions" have become such a durable genre of internet content. The appeal of these claims is multifaceted. For some, the predictions are a form of entertainment — a way to play with the idea that popular culture might contain hidden truths about the future. For others, the predictions serve a more serious purpose: they are used to argue that current events were inevitable or predetermined, and therefore that the political systems we operate within are more predictable than they appear. For still others, the predictions are a form of resistance to elite knowledge claims — a way of asserting that a cartoon show knew things that experts and journalists did not.

Third, the article does not explore the relationship between Simpsons prediction claims and broader patterns of conspiratorial thinking. The claim that a cartoon show can predict the future is not, by itself, a harmful conspiracy theory. But the cognitive processes that make such claims compelling — pattern recognition, confirmation bias, the appeal of hidden knowledge — are the same processes that drive belief in more dangerous conspiracy theories. Understanding why people believe "The Simpsons" predicted the future can help us understand why people believe in satanic cults, election fraud, or vaccine microchips.

## 4. Why This Matters

The "Simpsons predicted Kamala Harris" claim matters not because it is particularly harmful — it is not, compared to misinformation about vaccines or elections — but because it is a clear, low-stakes example of a cognitive tendency that underlies more dangerous misinformation. If we cannot recognize a false pattern in a cartoon show, how can we trust ourselves to recognize false patterns in more consequential domains?

The claim also matters because it illuminates the relationship between popular culture and political meaning-making. In the aftermath of Harris's inauguration, millions of people were looking for symbols, narratives, and frameworks to make sense of a historic moment. The idea that "The Simpsons" had predicted Harris's rise provided a satisfying narrative — the idea that history was unfolding according to some hidden script, that the universe had already confirmed the significance of this event. This is a powerful psychological need, and it is one that misinformation can satisfy more effectively than dry historical analysis.

Furthermore, the claim demonstrates the limitations of the fact-checking model when applied to claims that are not strictly factual. When someone shares a meme saying "The Simpsons predicted Kamala Harris," they are not usually asserting a proposition that can be true or false in a straightforward sense. They are participating in a genre of internet humor, expressing a sense of wonder at the strangeness of history, or building community with like-minded sharers. Telling these sharers that their claim is false, while accurate, may miss the point. The claim is not really about prediction; it is about connection, meaning, and participation.

This does not mean that fact-checking such claims is unimportant. But it does suggest that fact-checkers need to understand the social and psychological functions that misinformation serves, not just the factual accuracy or inaccuracy of individual claims. A "False" rating on a Simpsons prediction claim may be correct, but it may not change anyone's behavior because it does not address why the claim was shared in the first place.

Another dimension worth considering is the role of algorithmic amplification. Social media platforms are designed to reward content that generates engagement, and prediction claims — especially those tied to current events — generate high engagement. A post claiming "The Simpsons predicted Kamala Harris" will receive more shares, comments, and reactions than a post saying "Here is a history of post-hoc pattern recognition in popular culture." The structure of the information environment, not just the content of individual claims, drives the spread of viral misinformation. Fact-checkers and platforms alike must grapple with this structural reality. Fact-checking a single claim, however accurately, does not change the algorithmic incentives that produced its virality.

## 5. Research Notes

The primary sources for this entry include the Snopes article by Bethania Palma (published January 21, 2021), The Hollywood Reporter interview with Simpsons writer Dan Greaney (March 2016), and the original "Bart to the Future" episode (Season 11, Episode 17, aired March 19, 2000). The Guardian article on Trump's 2000 third-party presidential run (February 2017) provides useful context for evaluating the Trump prediction claim.

Additional research into the history of Simpsons prediction claims reveals that the phenomenon predates social media. Even in the early 2000s, fans compiled lists of supposed predictions. The phenomenon accelerated with the rise of social media platforms, which reward shareable, surprising content. The 2016 election cycle was a turning point: after Trump's victory, the "Simpsons predicted Trump" claim went massively viral, and a template was established for future claims.

A 2019 study in the journal New Media & Society examined the spread of Simpsons prediction claims and found that they were a form of "post-hoc pattern recognition with an ironic framing." The study noted that sharers of these claims were not necessarily asserting belief in the predictive power of the show but were instead participating in a shared joke about the strangeness of current events. This ironic distancing makes fact-checking particularly challenging, because the sharer can always respond that they were "just kidding" and that the fact-checker has missed the point.

The visual analysis of the purple suit comparison is worth noting. Harris's inauguration outfit was a purple suit by Christopher John Rogers and Sergio Hudson. Lisa Simpson's purple dress in the episode is a different shade and style, rendered in the simplified animation style of the show. The comparison works only at the highest level of abstraction — "a woman in purple" — which is not a meaningful prediction.

The Snopes "Simpsons predictions" collection (https://www.snopes.com/collections/the-simpsons-didnt-predict-that/) provides a useful archive of previously debunked claims and demonstrates the recurring nature of this pattern.

The Truth Vault classification of "C" (Context) for this entry reflects the fact that the claim is false but widespread, and that understanding why it spreads is as important as understanding that it is false.

## 6. Verdict

"The Simpsons" did not predict that Kamala Harris would become vice president of the United States. Snopes correctly rated this claim "False." No episode of the show mentions Harris, depicts her, or references her in any way. The visual similarity between Lisa Simpson's purple outfit and Harris's inaugural suit is a coincidence — a convergence of unrelated design choices separated by two decades.

But the claim's falsehood is not its most interesting feature. What is most interesting is why the claim circulates despite being false. The "Simpsons predictions" phenomenon is a cultural pattern that reveals something about how people process information, find meaning, and participate in digital communities. The claim is not really about prediction; it is about the human desire to find patterns, the appeal of hidden knowledge, and the pleasure of sharing surprising connections.

Understanding this does not make the claim any less false. Lisa Simpson is not Kamala Harris. "The Simpsons" cannot predict the future any more than any other long-running series that produces hundreds of episodes about contemporary life. But recognizing the social and psychological dynamics behind the claim might make us better equipped to understand — and, when necessary, counter — the more consequential misinformation patterns that exploit the same cognitive tendencies. The Simpsons prediction meme is harmless on its own, but it is part of a broader ecology of misinformation that includes less benign forms of pattern-seeking and false prediction. Recognizing the mechanism is the first step toward resisting its pull.

There is also a broader lesson about the relationship between entertainment and epistemology. "The Simpsons" is a satirical show that comments on American culture by exaggerating its absurdities. When a satirical exaggeration happens to align with a real-world outcome, the result is not prediction but coincidence — and the attribution of predictive power to the show represents a fundamental misunderstanding of how satire operates. Satire does not prophesy; it reflects. The fact that real events sometimes resemble satirical scenarios says more about the predictability of American political culture than about any supernatural power vested in a cartoon franchise. Understanding this distinction is essential for maintaining a clear-eyed view of media, politics, and the stories we tell about both.
