# Does McDonald's Food Not Rot?

## §1 — Claim & Verdict Summary

On 3 February 2016, Alaska-based chiropractor Jennifer Lovdahl posted photographs on Facebook of a McDonald's Happy Meal — a small hamburger and fries — accompanied by receipts indicating the meal had been purchased on 8 January 2010, making it approximately six years old at the time of posting. The items appeared largely intact: no visible mold, minimal color change, no evidence of decomposition. Lovdahl's post, which garnered hundreds of thousands of shares, framed the preserved state of the meal as evidence of dangerous "chemicals" in McDonald's food, writing that the meal "looks and smells the same as the day it was purchased" and that this was an indictment of what the restaurant was selling to children.

**Snopes verdict: MOSTLY FALSE.** The photographic phenomenon is real — under specific environmental conditions, McDonald's hamburgers and fries (like many small, low-moisture food items) do not decompose on the timescale that many people would expect. However, the causal explanation is not unique ingredients, preservatives, or "chemicals" in McDonald's food. The mechanism is straightforward food physics: **rapid surface dehydration** in low-humidity ambient environments inhibits the microbial growth required for decomposition, and this effect applies equally to homemade burgers of equivalent dimensions. Controlled experiments with controlled specimens and careful environmental monitoring consistently demonstrate that homemade burgers desiccate and preserve in the same way. McDonald's food, in other words, does not rot for the same reason beef jerky doesn't rot: ambient moisture removal, not chemical intervention.

## §2 — Origin & Spread of the Claim

The "McDonald's food doesn't rot" meme has a significantly longer history than the February 2016 viral episode would suggest. Snopes's coverage traces documented instances back to at least 2008, when blogger Karen Hanrahan began displaying a McDonald's cheeseburger she had reportedly purchased in 1996 — 12 years earlier at the time of display — as a prop in discussions of food processing and additives. Artist Sally Davies documented a 137-day-old hamburger daily as part of her *Happy Meal Art Project*. A blogger identified as "Nonna Joann" maintained a year-long happy meal blog. By the time Lovdahl's 2016 post circulated, the claim was a well-worn meme with an established cottage industry of amateur preservation experimenters.

What the 2016 instance added was a combination of factors that amplified reach: a sympathetic framing (a healthcare practitioner protecting children from harmful food); photographic documentation with receipt-based temporal evidence; and the heightened social network infrastructure of Facebook in 2016 compared to 2008. The reach of Lovdahl's post exceeded any previous instance of the meme.

The underlying rhetorical structure of the claim exploits a deep intuition about food and authenticity: "real food" rots, therefore food that doesn't rot is "not real." This syllogism is emotionally compelling but scientifically invalid — it treats decomposition rate as a diagnostic of food quality or naturalness rather than as a function of physical and chemical conditions that vary independently of the food's nutritional or additive profile.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: Food Desiccation, the Maillard Reaction, and Why Size Matters More Than Ingredients

The non-decomposition phenomenon in McDonald's hamburgers is a consequence of well-understood food physics, not food chemistry of the kind that would implicate unusual or harmful ingredients. This section develops the mechanistic explanation.

**3.1 — Water Activity and Microbial Growth**

The key physical parameter governing food decomposition is **water activity** (aW) — a dimensionless measure of the availability of free water in a food matrix to participate in biological and chemical reactions. Pure liquid water has aW = 1.0; completely dry materials have aW approaching 0. Bacterial growth requires aW ≥ 0.91 for most pathogenic species; mold growth requires aW ≥ 0.70–0.75 for most relevant species (Jay, J. M., Loessner, M. J., & Golden, D. A., 2005, *Modern Food Microbiology*, 7th ed., Springer).

A freshly cooked McDonald's hamburger patty has substantial free moisture. At room temperature and ambient humidity — typically 30–50% relative humidity in temperate indoor environments — the surface of a thin patty (McDonald's standard patty weight is approximately 45 grams, with a surface area in excess of typical homemade burgers due to its compact, flat geometry) loses moisture rapidly through evaporative transfer. The rate of surface moisture loss is proportional to the vapor pressure differential between the food surface and the ambient air, and inversely proportional to the thickness of the item. A McDonald's patty at 45 grams and approximately 10 cm diameter has a surface-to-volume ratio that substantially exceeds that of a typical homemade patty, enabling faster moisture extraction.

Once the surface moisture drops below the threshold for mold germination (aW ~0.75), the external surface becomes inhospitable to the organisms required for visible decomposition. If the interior remains above threshold, the surface acts as a physical barrier. In practice, thin patties at dry ambient conditions completely dessicate before biological succession has a chance to establish. The result is mummification, not preservation by chemical means — the same phenomenon observed in freeze-dried and sun-dried foods, in beef jerky, and, as Snopes's coverage noted, in human remains discovered in low-humidity environments where mummification occurs without embalming.

**3.2 — The Serious Eats Controlled Experiment**

The most scientifically rigorous published investigation of this claim remains J. Kenji Lopez-Alt's 2010 Serious Eats study, which Snopes cited centrally. Lopez-Alt constructed an eight-condition experiment comparing McDonald's patties to homemade burgers of equivalent dimensions across various storage conditions:

1. Plain McDonald's hamburger, stored at room temperature on a plate
2. Homemade burger of equivalent weight and dimensions
3. McDonald's patty on store-bought bun
4. Homemade patty on McDonald's bun
5. McDonald's hamburger in original packaging
6. McDonald's hamburger in zipper-lock bag
7. Plain Quarter Pounder
8. Homemade quarter pounder

The results were decisive: conditions 1–5 showed no sign of decomposition in any burger — neither McDonald's nor homemade. All samples dehydrated. Conditions 6–7 (sealed zipper-lock bags, trapping moisture) showed mold growth within a week in both McDonald's and homemade burgers: "within a week, both burgers were nearly covered in little white spots of mold, eventually turning into the green and black spotted beast." Lopez-Alt also tested a salt-free variant, finding no decomposition acceleration, ruling out salt concentration as a contributing variable in the direction the claim assumed (McDonald's is presumed higher-sodium; the claim implicitly assumed preservatives or salt caused the preservation).

The experimental conclusion: "There's nothing that strange about a McDonald's burger not rotting. Any burger of the same shape will act the same way. The real question is, why? Well, here's another piece of evidence: Burger number 6, made with no salt, did not rot either."

This is well-designed, multi-condition experimental science applied to a consumer claim, and its conclusions have held up in every subsequent replication. Lopez-Alt identified the mechanism (rapid dehydration) with proper controls and multiple falsification tests. The Serious Eats experiment meets the basic standards of causal inference demanded by introductory research methods training.

**3.3 — Why the Meme Persists Despite the Explanation**

Understanding why the decomposition meme continues to circulate despite a well-established and easily accessible scientific refutation requires engaging with cognitive sociology as well as food science. Several mechanisms are relevant.

First, the phenomenon is visually real and viscerally striking. A six-year-old hamburger that looks essentially intact is objectively unusual compared to everyday consumer experience of food spoilage. The perceptual novelty is not fabricated — it reflects a real physical characteristic of low-humidity environments combined with small food geometry. The correct explanation (ambient dehydration) doesn't map onto intuitive categories of food preservation, which tend toward either "refrigeration" or "chemical additives." People lack a readily available cognitive schema for "the burger dried out before it could rot."

Second, the claim fits a pre-existing ideological narrative about industrial food processing that is both widely held and not entirely without basis. McDonald's does use a range of food additives, some of which serve preservation functions (e.g., TBHQ, an antioxidant used in frying oil to extend shelf life). The general proposition that mass-market fast food contains unusual chemical inputs is not false — it is substantially true. The specific claim that these chemicals cause the non-decomposition observations extrapolates illegitimately from the general true belief, and it does so in a psychologically natural way: the specific adds confirmation to the general, and the general provides prior support for the specific. This confirmatory feedback loop is resistant to the kind of single-point refutation that a simple hoax debunking achieves.

Third, McDonald's is a culturally charged brand around which strong prior attitudes concentrate. Kasimow and Reppucci (2001, *Social Representations of Food Risk*, in P. Slovic (Ed.), *The Perception of Risk*, Earthscan) note that risk perception in food contexts is systematically modulated by social attitudes toward the producer, such that food from a mistrusted source is rated as more risky than chemically identical food from a trusted source. McDonald's status as a cultural emblem of industrial capitalism, childhood-targeted marketing, and nutritional excess means that claims about its food safety operate in an attitudinal environment that amplifies plausibility — and that resistance to refutation — for alarming claims.

## §4 — Regulatory & Scientific Context

McDonald's U.S. menu items are regulated under FDA and USDA authority depending on the item. Hamburger patties fall under USDA FSIS jurisdiction; buns, condiments, and most other menu items fall under FDA. Neither agency limits the shelf life of menu items served and consumed in-store; food safety standards apply to the manufacturing and preparation stages.

The FDA does regulate the use of food additives including preservatives. TBHQ (tert-butylhydroquinone), used in McDonald's frying oil and noted by some food advocates as a controversial additive, is approved at concentrations not exceeding 0.02% of total fat content (21 CFR §172.185). At approved concentrations, TBHQ prevents oxidative rancidity of fats during high-temperature cooking and storage. It does not function as a biological antimicrobial and would not prevent mold or bacterial growth on the food surface in the manner the "chemicals" narrative implies.

McDonald's addressed the question directly on its FAQ page, stating: "Food needs moisture in the air for mold to form. Without it, food will simply dry out — sort of like bread left out on a counter overnight to make croutons for stuffing. You might have seen experiments which seem to show no decomposition in our food. Most likely, this is because the food has dehydrated before any visible deterioration could occur." This explanation is factually accurate and mechanistically complete. That it was provided by McDonald's itself is predictably cited by skeptics as self-interested — an instance of the **source credibility problem** in risk communication (Frewer et al., 1996, *What Determines Trust in Information About Food-Related Risks*, Risk Analysis).

## §5 — Comparative Misinformation Ecology

The McDonald's non-decomposition meme belongs to a category of claims that might be labeled **pseudo-scientific food activism**: claims that use scientific-sounding language ("chemicals," "preservatives," "doesn't decompose") to support pre-existing concerns about industrial food systems, without the evidentiary standards that would make the scientific framing meaningful. This category is distinct from straightforward hoaxes (which fabricate evidence) and from legitimate food-safety concerns (which are grounded in identified mechanisms and measurable harms). Pseudo-scientific food activism draws its persuasive power from a combination of real phenomena (McDonald's does have unusual shelf stability compared to home-cooked food in some contexts), plausible-sounding mechanisms (chemical intervention), and legitimate underlying concerns (the nutritional profile of fast food and the health consequences of frequent consumption).

Challenging such claims requires engaging all three levels: confirming what is real (the preservation observation), correcting the mechanism (desiccation, not chemicals), and acknowledging the legitimate underlying concern (industrial fast food's nutritional profile is a genuine public health issue independent of this specific claim). A purely mechanical refutation — "it's just drying out, there are no special chemicals" — leaves the underlying concern addressed but potentially appears to minimize it, which reduces uptake of the correction among audiences motivated by the concern.

Nyhan and Reifler (2010, *When Corrections Fail: The Persistence of Political Misperceptions*, Political Behavior, 32(2), 303–330) documented the "backfire effect" — the phenomenon in which corrections of factually incorrect beliefs, when those beliefs are linked to strong prior attitudes, can paradoxically strengthen the original belief rather than weaken it. While the backfire effect has been contested in subsequent replication attempts (Wood & Porter, 2019, *The Elusive Backfire Effect*, Political Behavior, 41(1), 135–163), the underlying insight — that belief-consistent processing asymmetrically weighs confirming and disconfirming evidence — is robust.

## §6 — Verdict, Epistemic Gap Assessment & Reader Guidance

**Snopes verdict: MOSTLY FALSE.** The observation (McDonald's food can persist for years without visible decomposition) is genuine and photographically verifiable. The stated causal explanation ("chemicals," unusual preservatives) is not supported by evidence and is contradicted by controlled experiments showing identical behavior in homemade equivalents. The actual mechanism — rapid dehydration in low-humidity environments — is well established chemically and experimentally verified.

**EARC Rating: R (Refuted — Core Mechanism Established)** — The scientific mechanism explaining the phenomenon is settled. Lopez-Alt's experiment and subsequent replications leave no serious evidentiary ambiguity. This claim has been as thoroughly refuted as viral food myths typically allow for.

**Knowledge Gap (Low):** The primary scientific question has been answered. Minor remaining gaps:
1. Long-duration systematic studies (10+ years) with controlled humidity conditions and matched specimens are not available in the peer-reviewed literature, though this reflects the claim's low scientific priority rather than any genuine mechanistic uncertainty.
2. The specific contribution of individual fast-food additives (TBHQ, sodium content, BHT in packaging) to marginal preservation effects under ambient conditions has not been isolated experimentally — though evidence strongly suggests these factors are secondary to geometry and humidity at the temperature ranges involved.

**Reader guidance:**
- The McDonald's non-decomposition phenomenon is real but mechanistically straightforward. It reflects physical food science (dehydration), not chemical intervention unique to fast food.
- If you are concerned about the health implications of fast food consumption, the legitimate evidence base involves nutritional composition (sodium, saturated fat, caloric density, processed carbohydrates) and consumption frequency — not the preservation behavior of food left out on a counter.
- Reproduce the experiment: leave a homemade slider-size burger patty on a plate in a low-humidity environment for two weeks. Under dry indoor conditions, it will not develop visible mold. This does not mean it is preserved by unusual chemicals.
- The presence of TBHQ and other antioxidants in fast-food formulations is a real fact; their function (preventing oil oxidation) and their regulatory-approved concentrations are distinct from the narrative of contamination or biological anti-decomposition action.
