## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In late September 2021, Mars Wrigley Confectionery — the parent company owning the Skittles brand — formally announced via press release that the original lime flavor of Skittles would be returning to the candy's standard "Original" packaging in October 2021, permanently replacing the green apple flavor that had supplanted lime in 2013. The announcement generated substantial social media engagement, reflecting nearly eight years of vocal consumer advocacy for the lime flavor's restoration.

Snopes contributor Madison Dapcevich (2021) assigned this claim the rating **True**. The change was formally announced by Mars Wrigley on September 28, 2021, with the press release specifying that lime would rejoin the four other original flavors — strawberry, orange, lemon, and grape — in select store rollouts beginning in October 2021, followed by a national expansion over subsequent months.

This entry traces the full history of the lime-to-green-apple substitution, the consumer advocacy movement that sustained pressure for reversal over nearly a decade, the commercial and sensory science context for flavoring decisions in confectionery markets, and the broader epistemics of consumer product changes as objects of collective memory and identity.

## §2 The 2013 Lime Removal and the Eight-Year Consumer Advocacy Campaign

Skittles were introduced to the United States market in 1979 by Mars, Inc. (then Raiders, Ltd.). The original five flavors — strawberry, lemon, orange, lime, and grape — remained stable for over three decades and became deeply associated with the brand's identity, encapsulated in the slogan "Taste the Rainbow." These five flavors are notable for covering the primary sensory registers of fruit-based confectionery: the berry register (strawberry, grape), the citrus sweet register (orange, lemon), and the citric sharp register (lime), creating a robust flavor spread that complemented rather than competed internally.

In 2013, Mars Wrigley removed lime from the standard Original formulation and replaced it with green apple. The company did not announce the change with a major campaign. According to statements given by a Skittles spokesperson and reported in Dapcevich's (2021) article, the decision was driven by market research indicating that sour apple tested higher than lime in consumer preference surveys at that time. Green apple occupied the same green-visual-coding as lime in the rainbow color scheme, facilitating the substitution without requiring a redesign of packaging color coding.

The consumer response was immediate and sustained. An online petition launched on Change.org in 2016 (three years after the substitution) requesting the return of lime had accumulated 6,790 signatures by October 2021. Reddit discussions about the change circulated regularly over the entire eight-year interval, with the subreddit r/explainlikeimfive hosting a thread asking why the switch occurred that garnered substantial engagement. The Skittles brand eventually reported receiving over 130,000 consumer mentions specifically referencing lime flavor — a figure that the brand's senior manager Fernando Rodrigues cited as a direct input into the decision to reverse the substitution.

Mars Wrigley's official announcement framed the reversal as a direct response to consumer demand: "over the last eight years, while many have loved their time with Green Apple, the majority of consumers have spoken and they want Lime back" (Fernando Rodrigues, Mars Wrigley Senior Brand Manager, cited in Dapcevich, 2021). The company's self-described "unpaid intern" social media presence — a long-running ironic marketing persona for the Skittles Twitter account — also issued a statement playing on the brand's irreverent tone. The press release distributed via PR Newswire on September 28, 2021, confirmed that the change was permanent, not a limited-edition restoration.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Consumer Flavor Memory, Identity Attachment, and the Sensory Science of Reversal

The Skittles lime case presents an unusual example of successful organized consumer advocacy reversing a major confectionery brand decision — and this outcome has distinct epistemic interest for the study of how consumers form durable flavor preferences and how those preferences generate organized advocacy.

The core epistemic novelty here is the question of why lime generated a restoration campaign durable enough to succeed across eight years while the vast majority of confectionery flavor changes generate no such organized response. The relevant literature on sensory memory and flavor preference identity provides several converging explanations.

Zellner and Rozin (1985), writing in *Chemical Senses* (Oxford University Press), demonstrated that conditioned flavor preferences — preferences formed through repeated positive-affect pairing during formative consumption — show exceptional long-term durability, persisting when tested years after the conditioning context was removed. For a candy product consumed primarily in childhood and early adolescence, the 2013 substitution would have affected consumers who had formed lime associations across their developmental years and who experienced the substitution as an unwanted alteration of a sensory-memory anchor, not merely a product choice.

This phenomenon has subsequently been theorized as part of what Holbrook and Schindler (1989), writing in the *Journal of Consumer Research* (Oxford University Press), called "nostalgic bonding" — the mechanism by which products consumed during formative life periods acquire affective significance that remains stable across decades and generates purchase behavior independent of current product evaluation. Skittles lime, discontinued when many original consumers were adults, became an object of nostalgic bonding that the green apple substitute could not dislodge regardless of its own sensory qualities, because the "lime slot" in consumer memory was already occupied by a durably registered affective object.

The epistemic novelty for confectionery product strategy is that this case may represent a systematic underestimation of nostalgic bonding duration and intensity in flavor substitution impact assessments. If Mars Wrigley's 2013 market research correctly identified that sour apple tested higher than lime in general consumer surveys, those surveys may have been measuring hedonic preference across the general population rather than durability of prior association among the specific consumer segment most affected by the change. A consumer who has never tasted a lime Skittle might reasonably prefer green apple; a consumer who formed lime associations over a decade of prior consumption is making a very different evaluative judgment, and the questionnaire responses may have been structurally unable to distinguish these two populations.

The resolution — 130,000 consumer mentions and a Change.org petition with nearly 7,000 signatures — should be understood not as an unusually large consumer rebellion but as a behavioral measure of the strength of nostalgic bonding in a population segment whose preferences were systematically underweighted in the original substitution research design.

Lindstrom (2010), in *Brand Sense* (Free Press/Simon & Schuster), argued specifically that brands with strong multisensory identity profiles — in which a specific taste, smell, texture, or sound is strongly associated with brand identity in memory — are proportionally more vulnerable to substitution backlash because any alteration of the sensory profile is experienced as a disruption of brand identity rather than merely a product change. Skittles' identity as "Taste the Rainbow" centralized sensory variety as the brand promise, making the specific identity of each color-flavor pairing conceptually central to what the brand *was* rather than incidental product specification.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Dapcevich's (2021) investigation confirmed the factual claim, cited the official press release, and provided useful historical and social media context. Several analytical dimensions were not developed:

**The specific sensory science of lime versus green apple**: The article does not address why lime and green apple, both green-coded flavors, might have generated such differentiated consumer affect. Lime (citrus aurantifolia primary flavoring compound: limonene, α-terpineol, and linalool; Dhavamani & Sivakumar, 2019) and green apple (malic acid and ethyl-2-methyl butyrate as primary aroma compounds; Aprea et al., 2012) produce meaningfully different sensory profiles — lime presenting a sharper, more acidic citrus note while green apple is sweeter with a slightly fermented fruit character. The specific confectionery chemistry of artificial flavor synthesis for each also differs, meaning the artificial Skittles representations of each share only the green visual coding.

**The Change.org petition's limited formal influence**: The petition's 6,790 signatures, while meaningful as an engagement indicator, are a minuscule fraction of Skittles' consumer base. The petition itself likely mattered less as a direct political mechanism than as a visible rallying point that aggregated and amplified consumer sentiment already being tracked through other channels (social media mention volume, customer service contacts). The 130,000 social media mentions figure cited by the brand almost certainly had more direct internal influence.

**Whether green apple has any ongoing product presence**: The Snopes article confirms lime's return to Original packs but does not clarify whether green apple was discontinued across all Skittles products or retained in other product lines (e.g., Skittles Sours, limited editions, international markets).

**The international dimension**: Lime was never replaced with green apple in all market territories. In some European and Asian markets, Skittles formulations differ substantially from U.S. Original packs, and the lime/green-apple controversy was substantially a U.S.-centric phenomenon.

## §5 Contextual Framing: The Consumer "Flavor Revolt" Tradition and Its Antecedents

The Skittles lime restoration sits within a recognizable tradition of consumer flavor and formula revolts that have periodically affected major food and beverage brands. The most famous antecedent — indeed the paradigm case for the genre — is the April 1985 introduction of "New Coke" by the Coca-Cola Company, which replaced the original Coca-Cola formula with a reformulated product that had tested better in blind taste tests. The subsequent consumer rebellion, the formal restoration of "Coca-Cola Classic" in July 1985, and the company's acknowledgment that the formula change had been a mistake are now taught as canonical cases in consumer behavior and marketing strategy curricula worldwide.

The Coca-Cola case established the analytical framework most later cases employ: the finding that blind taste test preferences (hedonic evaluation without brand context) do not predict purchase behavior or brand loyalty in real market conditions, because brand identity is constituted by associations that include but extend beyond hedonic evaluation. Pepsi Challenge blind taste tests had shown preference for Pepsi over Coke in head-to-head comparison, but the same consumers chose Coke when they could see the can — a phenomenon that McClure et al. (2004), writing in *Neuron* (Cell Press), attributed to prefrontal cortex engagement when brand identity information was available, showing that brand knowledge literally changes the flavor experience via top-down cognitive modulation of taste perception.

The lime Skittles case differs from New Coke in one significant respect: the Skittles change was much less prominent at the time of its introduction (no major campaign, no public announcement) and the subsequent consumer response was lower-amplitude (no organized boycott, no major press coverage) but far more sustained (nearly eight years versus New Coke's 77-day run). The sustained low-amplitude advocacy model may be more common in contemporary digital environments than the sudden revolt model, because social media provides persistent, low-cost infrastructure for maintaining distributed advocacy without requiring organizational infrastructure.

Other relevant precedents include Heinz's removal of HP Sauce from UK shelves (2007, temporary), the reformulation of Lay's potato chip seasoning (multiple instances), and the modification of Cadbury Dairy Milk recipe following the Kraft/Mondelex acquisition (2010), each of which generated organized consumer opposition through petitions, social media campaigns, and organized letter-writing. What is notable across these cases is the asymmetry between the relative commercial insignificance of the specific product formulation decision and the intensity and duration of consumer affect it generates — an asymmetry explained precisely by the nostalgic bonding and sensory identity mechanisms documented above.

For media literacy purposes, the Skittles lime story is an instructive case in which a "True" verdict — a straightforward confirmation of a product announcement — nonetheless contains substantial analytical depth. The fact that lime is coming back is not merely a consumer interest story: it is evidence about the architecture of consumer memory, the limits of hedonic preference research, and the organizational conditions under which consumer advocacy can actually reverse major brand decisions.

## §6 Verdict, EARC Classification, and Knowledge Gaps

**Verdict: TRUE**

Mars Wrigley Confectionery confirmed via formal press release on September 28, 2021 that Skittles Original packs would permanently replace the green apple flavor — introduced in 2013 — with the original lime flavor beginning in October 2021, with national rollout in subsequent months. The change was characterized as permanent. The lime flavor, one of the five original Skittles flavors since the candy's U.S. introduction in 1979, was restored following sustained consumer advocacy spanning approximately eight years and including over 130,000 social media mentions and a Change.org petition.

**EARC Classification: R (Reception — consumer advocacy and product identity)**

The analytical gap here is reception-focused: the claim is straightforwardly true, but its full significance requires understanding the consumer memory and brand identity mechanisms that generated eight years of sustained advocacy for a flavor restoration, the limits of hedonic preference research that likely underpinned the original 2013 substitution decision, and the significance of this case within the broader tradition of consumer flavor revolts.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**
- Whether green apple flavor was fully discontinued across all Skittles product lines or retained in other formats
- International market status of lime versus green apple in non-U.S. Skittles formulations
- Internal Mars Wrigley research methodology that led to the original 2013 substitution decision
- Consumer preference data from after the October 2021 restoration, measuring actual reception of the returned lime flavor
