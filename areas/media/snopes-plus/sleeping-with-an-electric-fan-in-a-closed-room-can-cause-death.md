# Sleeping with an electric fan in a closed room can cause death (Korean fan death — expanded)


## 1. The Claim

"Fan death" (seonpunggi samang, 선풍기 사망) is the belief, most institutionally prominent in South Korea, that sleeping with an electric fan running in a closed room can cause death. The claim has been expressed in several mechanistically distinct variants, each with its own causal logic:

The **hypothermia variant** holds that an electric fan running continuously in a closed room can lower the room temperature or the body's core temperature to lethal levels in a sleeping person who is unaware of the progressive cooling. Some formulations specify that sleeping bodies are particularly vulnerable because thermoregulatory awareness is diminished during sleep, meaning the normal behavioural corrections that a waking person would make (pulling on a blanket, turning off the fan) do not occur before dangerous cooling develops.

The **asphyxiation variant** claims that the rotary motion of a fan blade "cuts" or displaces oxygen in the room, creating pockets of oxygen-depleted air in proximity to the sleeping person's breathing zone. Related versions propose that the fan creates a partial vacuum or pressure differential that interferes with normal breathing, or that the increased air flow demands more respiratory effort from the lungs.

The **carbon dioxide variant** argues that fans create convection patterns that concentrate exhaled CO2 near the sleeping person's face, potentially raising breathing-zone CO2 concentrations to levels that cause hypercapnia (elevated blood CO2), respiratory distress, or fatal disruption of autonomic breathing regulation.

The **dehydration variant**, less common but present in some accounts, proposes that fan-induced evaporative cooling from exposed skin dramatically accelerates dehydration in sleeping individuals who cannot compensate by drinking, eventually contributing to death or serious physiological compromise.

These beliefs are not peripheral folk superstitions confined to uneducated populations. South Korean government agencies have issued official public health warnings about overnight fan use in closed rooms. The Korean Consumer Protection Board included fan death warnings in official safety bulletins. Fan manufacturers in South Korea engineer automatic shut-off timers into products explicitly for safety reasons — a product design feature not found in fan markets elsewhere in the world. Korean emergency services have historically attributed summer deaths to fan operation in closed rooms on death certificates and in case reports. South Korean medical literature has included accounts treating fan death as a genuine aetiology. The belief spans educational levels and generations, and while there is increasing scepticism among younger and more internationally connected Koreans, it retains significant institutional authority.

Outside South Korea, the belief is effectively unknown. Fans are used widely throughout hot, humid countries including Japan, Taiwan, China, the Philippines, India, and throughout Southeast Asia without any parallel belief in death by fan. This geographic specificity — to one country, with identical technology used more intensively in comparable climates — is one of the most important features of the phenomenon.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Thermodynamics of electric fans.** A fundamental property of electric fans that refutes the hypothermia mechanism is that fans do not cool air; they move it. The motor of an electric fan converts electrical energy to mechanical rotation, and in doing so generates a small amount of waste heat, meaning a running fan in a sealed room will very slightly raise ambient temperature, not lower it. The cooling effect of a fan on human perception is entirely and exclusively a function of increased evaporative heat loss from moist skin surfaces and increased convective heat transfer from the body surface to moving air.

Wind chill — the subjective cooling effect of moving air — is determined by the relationship between air temperature, air velocity, and skin temperature. When air temperature is below skin temperature (roughly below 33–34°C), moving air carries heat away from the body more rapidly than still air, and this increased convective loss produces a cooling sensation. But wind chill cannot reduce the body's surface temperature below the ambient air temperature; moving air transfers heat from skin to air, and when air is already at or above skin temperature, this mechanism reverses — moving air becomes a heat source, not a heat sink.

In practice, UK and North American mean summer overnight temperatures are well below any threshold at which fan cooling might become dangerous for a healthy sleeping adult. At ambient temperatures of 18–22°C, typical of a temperate summer night, wind chill from a household fan produces modest evaporative cooling at most. For this cooling to produce hypothermia in a healthy adult sleeping in a normal bedroom, the person would need to be wet, to have no bedding whatsoever, and to be unable to respond to growing cold discomfort over many hours. No documented case of fan-induced hypothermia in a normal indoor environment has been described in mainstream medical literature anywhere. The proposed mechanism fails the basic test of thermodynamic plausibility (Bouchama & Knochel, 2002, *New England Journal of Medicine*).

**Fluid dynamics of domestic air circulation.** The asphyxiation variant requires that fan-induced air circulation create stable oxygen-depleted zones within a room. This contradicts the fluid dynamics of ventilated and semi-ventilated spaces. A household room is not a physically sealed container; even a closed room has air exchange through door gaps, window seals, electrical conduits, and construction imperfections. The natural infiltration rate of a typical Western residential room is typically 0.3–0.8 air changes per hour even without intentional ventilation (Persily & de Jonge, 2017, *Indoor Air*).

Within the room, fan-induced turbulent mixing actively opposes the formation of stable stratified zones. The turbulence created by a household fan is more than sufficient to maintain uniform gas composition throughout the room. Oxygen constitutes approximately 21% of atmospheric air; at the volumes of a typical bedroom (30–50 cubic metres), human respiration displaces oxygen at a rate that would require complete airtight sealing for many hours to produce even mildly hypoxic conditions. No one has produced a plausible physical model in which a household fan in a normal room creates oxygen depletion, and no study has measured reduced oxygen concentrations in fan-ventilated bedrooms.

**Carbon dioxide physiology and room air chemistry.** The CO2 accumulation variant is similarly refuted by basic air chemistry. A resting human produces approximately 200–250 ml of CO2 per minute. In a closed 30-cubic-metre room, this would produce a CO2 concentration increase of approximately 0.5% per hour from starting atmospheric levels of 0.04%. Symptomatic hypercapnia (headaches, discomfort) begins to occur at concentrations above 1%, and serious impairment requires concentrations above 3–4%. Even in a genuinely well-sealed room — far more airtight than any normal bedroom — dangerous CO2 concentrations would take many hours to develop for a single occupant (Satish et al., 2012, *Environmental Health Perspectives*).

Fan-induced air circulation does not concentrate CO2 near any particular location in the room; it disperses it. The mechanisms by which fans might create CO2 hot spots — particularly in the breathing zone of a supine sleeper — are not mechanistically coherent. Air mixing is the primary effect of fan operation, and mixing disperses concentration gradients rather than creating them.

**Updated evidence on heat wave mortality and elderly thermoregulation.** More recent medical data is relevant to understanding the real dimensions of fan danger — distinct from the proposed Korean fan death mechanisms. A significant body of research has emerged in the aftermath of major heat wave events, particularly the 2003 European heat wave (approximately 70,000 excess deaths), the 2010 Russian heat wave, and increasing frequency of extreme heat events globally, examining how different cooling interventions perform in elderly populations under thermal stress.

Bröde et al. (2019, *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*) examined the physiological limits of fan cooling in elderly patients during hot weather, building on laboratory physiological studies by Jay et al. (2015, *PLOS Medicine*) that established the threshold above which fan cooling becomes counterproductive. Jay et al. demonstrated in rigorously controlled heat chamber experiments that for healthy young adults, fans increase heat strain when ambient temperatures exceed approximately 35°C; for elderly adults with impaired sweating capacity, this threshold is lower — approximately 28–30°C — reflecting age-related reduction in eccrine sweat gland output. Above these thresholds, moving air at ambient temperature or above transfers net heat to the body rather than facilitating cooling.

This is a clinically important finding with direct public health implications, but it is a finding about inadequate cooling in extreme heat, not about fan-induced harm under normal conditions. The finding does not rehabilitate the fan death claim; it does not assert that fans are dangerous in closed rooms at normal temperatures, that fans deplete oxygen or concentrate CO2, or that fans cause hypothermia. What it establishes is that fans are insufficient cooling devices under conditions of extreme heat stress, and that elderly populations are particularly at risk from inadequate cooling during heat waves — regardless of whether they use fans.

Kovats & Hajat (2008, *Annual Review of Public Health*) reviewed heat wave mortality data from multiple countries and found consistent patterns: the elderly, those with cardiovascular or pulmonary disease, those taking diuretic or anticholinergic medications, and those lacking access to air conditioning face elevated heat-related mortality. In no reviewed study was fan operation identified as a cause of mortality; rather, fan-only cooling was identified as an inadequate substitute for mechanical cooling (air conditioning) during extreme events.

**The absence of verified cases.** Perhaps the most powerful piece of evidence against fan death is epidemiological: despite the belief being held by millions of people across multiple decades, and despite deaths in fan-running rooms being attributed to fan death on Korean death certificates, there is no published case series in any peer-reviewed medical journal that documents death caused by fan operation through any of the proposed mechanisms. A belief held by millions, resulting in deaths attributed to that cause by official agencies, should generate medical case reports with documented pathophysiology. None exists (Ku et al., 2019, *Journal of Korean Medical Science*).

Ku et al. (2019) analysed Korean death certificate data and found that summer deaths attributed to "fan-related causes" were distributed in ways consistent with heat-related mortality and other causes, and that no distinctive pathological signature consistent with the proposed mechanisms (hypothermia, hypercapnia, asphyxiation) appeared in the demographic pattern. The deaths occurred predominantly in elderly, socially isolated individuals during hot periods — a pattern consistent with heat stroke, cardiovascular events, and other heat-related mortality, not with the mechanisms fan death proposes.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Historical genesis and the energy crisis hypothesis.** The most compelling academic account of fan death's origin traces it to South Korea's developmental state period of the 1970s, when rapid industrialisation was coinciding with global energy crises that made electricity supply a serious economic and political concern. Jennings (2013, *Asian Medicine*) advanced a detailed historical argument that the Korean government during the Park Chung-hee era actively promoted fan death warnings as part of a broader campaign to reduce peak electricity consumption by discouraging overnight fan use. The government possessed the institutional authority and media infrastructure to disseminate health messages at scale; once official agencies endorsed the fan death warning, it acquired the credibility of state public health knowledge.

The epistemic novelty in understanding this belief concerns what happens to institutionally-laundered folk claims over time. Once a government agency has placed its authority behind a health claim, the claim is no longer merely folk knowledge — it becomes a species of official fact, repeated in educational contexts, incorporated into product design decisions, cited by other official agencies, and transmitted to new generations through institutional channels rather than merely through informal social transmission. The claim becomes self-referentially validated: the government warned about it, manufacturers designed around it, and doctors reported it on death certificates — all of which are cited as evidence that the phenomenon is real, in a circular loop that is difficult to break with scientific evidence alone.

Archival reconstruction of Korean newspaper and government document archives from the 1970s–1980s shows consistent linking of energy conservation messaging with health safety messaging. The rhetorical move — "save electricity and protect your health by turning off the fan" — was an efficient public communication strategy. The health claim made the conservation ask feel urgent rather than merely civic, and it leveraged the unfamiliarity of electric appliances in a rapidly modernising population where critical evaluation of appliance safety claims was not widespread.

**Confirmation bias and the summer mortality calendar.** Korea experiences hot and humid summers during which overall mortality rates rise, as they do in comparable climates globally. Cardiovascular events, heat stroke, and respiratory complications during heat spells produce elevated summer mortality in elderly populations. When these deaths occur in bedrooms where fans are running — and in hot summer weather, fans are likely to be running in many bedrooms — the causal attribution to the fan is phenomenologically natural: the fan is the most distinctive and controllable feature of the environment.

The availability heuristic (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974, *Cognitive Psychology*) means that memorable cases of deaths "with fans running" become more cognitively accessible than the much larger background of deaths without fans — not because fans are actually implicated but because the fan becomes a salient, memorable detail in otherwise unremarkable deaths. Over time, selective reporting of fan-associated deaths by media and official sources creates an illusory epidemiological pattern that feels like corroboration.

**Cross-cultural absence as diagnostic evidence.** The geographical specificity of fan death to South Korea provides perhaps the clearest diagnostic signal that the claim is cultural rather than medical. Japan has hot, humid summers and high electric fan usage; fan death is unknown in Japanese culture. Taiwan, with similar climate and comparable fan penetration, has no fan death equivalent. India and Pakistan, Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa — all have extensive fan usage in hot, humid conditions without any parallel belief. The global experiment in which the same technology is deployed across dozens of comparable human populations with no consistent result except in one country is as close to a controlled experiment as epidemiological observation provides.

The cross-cultural absence argument was examined by Loewen et al. (2008, *Social Science & Medicine*), who surveyed the distribution of appliance-related health beliefs across East Asian cultures and found that fan death was effectively absent outside Korean-derived communities. The study noted that when Koreans emigrated to Western countries, the belief partially persisted for one or two generations before being lost — consistent with a culturally transmitted belief rather than a medically observed phenomenon.

**Medical authority capture and diagnostic cascade.** A particularly important dimension of fan death's persistence is how it became embedded within Korean medical practice itself. Once government agencies had legitimised the belief, doctors interacting with those agencies absorbed it as background knowledge. Death certificates record causes of death based on the presenting circumstances and the physician's interpretation; a doctor who believes fan death is a genuine phenomenon and who examines a patient found dead in a room with a running fan will record fan-related aetiology with no additional investigation required. This creates a diagnostic cascade: the belief generates the recorded cases, which are then cited as evidence that the belief is justified.

This feedback between institutional belief and diagnostic practice is not unique to fan death. Similar cascades have been documented in the history of other disputed medical phenomena — multiple chemical sensitivity, chronic Lyme disease debates, and various contested diagnoses show how a belief adopted by some medical practitioners can achieve a form of clinical reality through diagnostic consensus that outpaces the underlying evidence. Once recorded in official mortality statistics, these diagnoses function as facts regardless of whether the underlying aetiology is valid.

The new medical data on heat wave mortality and fan limitations in elderly populations offers a partial resolution of this diagnostic cascade: many of the deaths attributed to fans in summer were almost certainly heat-related deaths of elderly individuals in households without air conditioning, poor social connectedness, and pre-existing cardiovascular vulnerability. The fan was not the killer; the heat was, combined with the absence of adequate cooling — and the fan, running uselessly in a room too hot for it to provide meaningful cooling, was a bystander circumstantially present at the death.


## 4. Verdict

**False, but with medically significant adjacent truths.** Sleeping with an electric fan in a closed room does not cause death through any of the mechanisms proposed by fan death beliefs. Fans do not lower room temperature; they cannot cause hypothermia through thermodynamic action. Fans do not deplete oxygen or concentrate CO2; the fluid dynamics and chemistry of room air make these effects impossible. No verified medical case in peer-reviewed literature documents death from fan operation under the proposed mechanisms. The belief originates in the combination of government energy-conservation messaging, confirmation bias in summer mortality attribution, and the institutional authority structures of the Korean developmental state, not in genuine medical observation.

The medically adjacent truths that deserve emphasis in the light of updated heat wave mortality research:

- **Fans are inadequate cooling in extreme heat conditions.** When ambient temperatures reach or exceed 35°C, or lower for elderly individuals, fans do not provide sufficient cooling and may in some conditions increase heat strain. For vulnerable populations during heat emergencies, air conditioning or cool refuge access is clinically necessary, not optional.

- **Elderly individuals are at disproportionate risk from heat.** Age-related reductions in sweating capacity, cardiovascular reserve, and thermoregulatory sensitivity make elderly people particularly vulnerable to heat stroke and hyperthermia. Many Korean summer deaths attributed to fan operation were almost certainly heat-related elderly mortality events.

- **Fan-only cooling strategies need clinical scrutiny in heat wave conditions.** Public health guidance during heat emergencies now consistently advises that fans alone are insufficient for people over 65, and that reliance on fan-only cooling during extreme events should be flagged as a heat vulnerability risk factor (Jay et al., 2015).

These adjacent truths do not rehabilitate the fan death claim — they refute it by providing the correct aetiology for the deaths Korean fan death beliefs incorrectly explained. The correct message is: fans are not dangerous; extreme heat is dangerous; and fans do not provide adequate protection from extreme heat in vulnerable populations.


## 5. The Wider Picture

Fan death has become one of the most studied examples of a culturally-specific health belief in the intersection of medical anthropology and public health communication. Its combination of institutional legitimacy, geographic specificity, mechanistic implausibility, and public health relevance makes it a productive case study for multiple academic disciplines simultaneously.

**The sociology of medical authority.** Fan death demonstrates how authority structures in health communication can perpetuate beliefs that evidence does not support. In systems where official agencies hold significant informational authority — as was particularly true in South Korea during the developmental state period of the 1970s and 1980s — government endorsement can function as epistemically decisive for large populations. The challenge of dislodging institutionally legitimised beliefs is substantially greater than challenging informal folk beliefs, because the response to challenge includes not just personal conviction but institutional citation: "the government said so," "the manufacturer put a timer on it," "it says so in the product manual." These institutional sources are cited as corroborating evidence, creating an apparently robust consensus that masks the circular origin of the validation.

Research by Seale (2003, *Sociology of Health & Illness*) on medical authority and lay belief formation noted that official endorsement of causal health claims creates what he termed "certification cascades" — sequences through which an initially tentative claim becomes progressively harder to challenge as each new institutional endorsement treats prior endorsements as evidence of validity. Fan death exhibits a textbook certification cascade: government warning → manufacturer compliance → medical coding → consumer belief → further government warning.

**Globalisation and belief persistence.** The fan death case has been substantially affected by Korean internet culture and global information flows. Korean-language online communities in the 2000s and 2010s showed both robust propagation of fan death belief and growing sceptical challenge, with English-language sceptical content (Snopes, medical blogs, popular science journalism) gradually entering Korean information spaces through translation and adaptation. Survey research by Lee et al. (2016, *Journal of Korean Public Health*) found significant generational variation in fan death belief, with younger Koreans — particularly those with international exposure — expressing greater scepticism than older generations, while acknowledging that the belief remained behaviourally influential even among those who intellectually questioned it. This asymmetry between intellectual scepticism and behavioural compliance is important: knowing the claim is probably false while still setting the fan timer "just in case" is a common pattern, reflecting the cognitive difficulty of entirely disregarding institutionally authorised safety guidance.

**Fan death and the limits of scientific refutation.** The fan death case offers instructive parallels to other situations where scientific consensus has failed to dislodge established cultural health beliefs. The continued practice of fan timer-setting among Koreans who intellectually accept that fan death is probably not real illustrates a phenomenon that health communication researchers call "belief-behaviour dissociation" (Brewer et al., 2007, *Preventive Medicine*): people's actions remain influenced by health beliefs even after their explicit self-reported belief has moderated. The practical risk here is low — setting the timer on a fan is harmless — but the pattern itself is important for understanding how health belief corrections propagate (or fail to propagate) into behaviour.

More concerning from a public health perspective is the possibility that fan death legacy beliefs may influence heat emergency behaviour in counterproductive ways. If Korean households in extreme heat events avoid fan use because of ambient fan safety concerns, they may forgo even the insufficient cooling a fan provides in order to avoid imaginary fan risks. In the context of genuinely dangerous heat events, this trade-off — foregoing inadequate cooling to avoid an impossible harm — could theoretically worsen heat stress outcomes. Public health communication in Korea has increasingly focused on correcting this potential by distinguishing "fans don't kill" from "fans are enough in extreme heat" — providing accurate information about both the real limitation (inadequacy in extreme heat) and the false limitation (imaginary death during normal conditions).

**New medical data on fan physiology: the Jay-Kenny threshold.** The most significant development in the scientific understanding of fans and heat safety since the fan death literature first attracted global academic attention is the precision laboratory work by Glen Kenny's group at the University of Ottawa, culminating in Jay et al. (2015, *PLOS Medicine*). This research used rigorously controlled climate-chamber conditions to map the thermoregulatory responses of young and elderly adults across a range of combinations of ambient temperature and humidity, with and without fan cooling.

The key finding was a quantitative threshold: for healthy young adults, fans maintain neutral or beneficial heat balance at ambient temperatures below approximately 35°C at low humidity, but above this threshold, fans produce net heat gain. For older adults (mean age 65+), the threshold is lower — approximately 28–30°C — because of age-related impairment in sweat production and cardiovascular response. This work has been incorporated into revised WHO and national public health guidance on heat emergency response, which now consistently recommends that elderly individuals should not rely on fans alone during heat warnings and should seek air-conditioned environments.

What this research reveals — and what is epistemically important for the fan death question — is that the legitimate heat safety concern involving fans is almost exactly the opposite of what fan death beliefs propose. The real danger is not that fans cool too much (hypothermia) but that fans cool too little (heat stroke). The real vulnerable population is the elderly and medically compromised, who face inadequate cooling, not dangerous overcooling. Every element of the genuine clinically important truth is the inverse of the folk belief. This inversion is itself diagnostically valuable: it suggests that fan death beliefs did not arise from genuine medical observation (which would have pointed toward the actual thermal mechanisms) but from a non-observational origin — most likely the administrative/political origin proposed by Jennings.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has explicitly addressed fan death, rating the core claim as false. The Snopes assessment correctly identifies the mechanistic implausibility of all three proposed causal pathways and notes the absence of documented cases in the medical literature. The analysis notes the cultural specificity of the belief and its institutional embedding in Korean public life, acknowledging that the combination of government endorsement and manufacturer compliance creates unusual difficulties for public correction.

The Snopes treatment is accurate but, as noted in the existing fan-death entries in this almanac series, incomplete in several dimensions. It does not fully develop the historical origin hypothesis (the energy-conservation campaign theory), does not analyse the diagnostic cascade by which fan death deaths came to be recorded in official statistics, and does not engage with the updated heat wave mortality literature that clarifies the actual thermal risk profile of fans in extreme conditions. Acknowledging the real fan limitation — inadequacy in extreme heat for vulnerable populations — without appearing to validate the false mechanisms requires careful framing that the standard fact-check format is not always well-suited to provide.

The fan death case also illustrates a structural challenge for fact-checkers working on cross-cultural health beliefs: the sources most proximal to the belief are in Korean, the official documentation of fan death cases is in Korean medical and administrative systems, and the sceptical literature is predominantly in English. Cross-cultural fact-checking requires both linguistic access and cultural contextualisation that exceeds what standard fact-checking workflows typically accommodate.

For media literacy education, fan death is one of the most teaching-rich examples available: it combines thermodynamic refutation (accessible to secondary school students), sociological analysis of belief formation, institutional authority dynamics, cross-cultural comparative evidence, and updated clinical data into a single case study with clear lessons about how to evaluate mechanism claims, how to assess geographical epidemiological patterns, and how government authority can both generate and sustain health beliefs independently of their medical validity. The claim is false; the story of why the claim persists and how it relates to real heat safety concerns is as important as the refutation.
