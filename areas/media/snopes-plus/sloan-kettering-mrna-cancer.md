# Memorial Sloan Kettering research shows mRNA can promote cancer

## 1. The Claim

In March 2021, as COVID-19 mRNA vaccines were being administered to tens of millions of people worldwide, a claim began circulating on social media asserting that scientists at the Sloan Kettering Institute—one of the world's most respected cancer research centers—had discovered that mRNA itself promotes cancer. The claim was packaged as a "medical shocker": the same biological molecule used in the Pfizer and Moderna vaccines had been found by elite researchers at America's premier cancer center to interfere with the body's tumor-suppressing mechanisms.

The story originated on March 2, 2021, in an article published by Natural News, a website that has been identified by health agencies and media literacy organizations as a leading source of health misinformation and that generates revenue partly by selling supplements and natural health products. The article's headline described the research as a revelation "to the whole world of vaccine philosophy," claimed that "mRNA itself carries cancer CAUSING changes," and asserted that this was "the complete opposite of what the CDC and the vaccine manufactures are telling everyone right now about the Covid vaccines."

The viral spread of the claim was rapid and extensive. Social media users, many of whom were already skeptical of the mRNA vaccine program for independent reasons, shared the Natural News story with commentary suggesting that health authorities had concealed or ignored research that contradicted vaccine safety. Some versions of the claim were attached to specific allegations: that the CDC knew about the Sloan Kettering research and had suppressed it, that people who had received mRNA vaccines were now at elevated cancer risk, or that the vaccine program constituted a knowing act of harm by public health authorities. Others were more impressionistic: the sheer existence of the research seemed, to many sharers, to constitute proof that "they" had known all along that mRNA—any mRNA—was dangerous.

The emotional structure of the claim was well-calibrated for viral circulation in early 2021. The mRNA vaccine rollout was accompanied by intense public anxiety, a fragmented information environment, legitimate questions about long-term effects for a genuinely novel vaccine technology, and a well-established anti-vaccine media ecosystem that had been amplified during years of pre-pandemic content evolution. The claim did not require particular biological literacy to spread: it simply inverted the official narrative (mRNA vaccines are safe) and attributed that inversion to research from an institution whose name (Memorial Sloan Kettering) signals authoritative credibility even to audiences with no scientific background.

## 2. What's Actually True

The Sloan Kettering research is real. The interpretation placed on it by Natural News and the social media ecosystem that amplified the resulting claim is entirely fabricated. Understanding why requires understanding both what the research actually found and how mRNA vaccines work.

**What the Sloan Kettering research actually studied.** In 2018, a team at the Sloan Kettering Institute published a paper in *Nature* (volume 560) investigating a specific question in cancer biology: why do cells in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) so often lack the DNA mutations that typically explain cancer's characteristic uncontrolled cell growth? Cancer is generally understood to result from accumulated genetic coding errors—mutations in genes that regulate cell division, cell death, and tumor suppression. But CLL cells frequently don't show many such mutations, which poses an explanatory puzzle.

The Sloan Kettering researchers proposed a mechanism. They found evidence that CLL cells can harbor errors in the mRNA transcription process—specifically, that the mRNA copies produced from certain genes could be truncated, missing important sequence segments that code for functional proteins. If the mRNA carrying the blueprint for a tumor-suppressing protein is truncated, the protein it produces will also be truncated and likely non-functional. The cell then lacks a properly functioning tumor suppressor, which could contribute to cancerous growth even without a direct mutation in the underlying DNA. The researchers were proposing mRNA transcription errors as an additional mechanism by which gene expression can go wrong in CLL—a serious and important finding for cancer biology and potentially for diagnostics.

The Sloan Kettering news release summarizing this research, published in 2018, noted that the findings "suggest that alterations to mRNA may be more common than previously appreciated" and that they could help explain CLL's unusual genetic profile. The researchers explicitly stated in their release that "mRNAs are a normal component of all cells" and that their findings were specific to aberrant transcription events in cancer cells—not to mRNA as a category of molecule.

**The fundamental misunderstanding Natural News exploited.** Natural News's error—or, more precisely, deliberate misrepresentation—was to treat the phrase "mRNA" as a single, uniform entity whose properties in one context transfer automatically to all contexts. In reality, the mRNA studied by the Sloan Kettering team is a product of cellular transcription: a copy made by the cell's own machinery when the nucleus is instructed to produce a protein. The specific pathology the researchers identified—truncated mRNA copies—is a product of specific errors in this cellular copying process.

mRNA vaccines contain no cellular transcription. They deliver synthetic mRNA produced in a laboratory by chemical synthesis—a process in which every nucleotide in the sequence is precisely specified and verified. This synthetic mRNA never enters the nucleus of a cell. It never interacts with chromosomal DNA. It never undergoes transcription. The process whose errors the Sloan Kettering team studied is simply not present at any point during or after mRNA vaccination.

This point bears emphasis because it targets the specific misrepresentation at the heart of the claim. The Sloan Kettering research was not about mRNA vaccines. It was not about external mRNA of any kind. It was about errors that occur when a cell's own machinery makes copies of genetic instructions from the nucleus. Those errors cannot occur during mRNA vaccination because the mRNA vaccine's active component is already a finished, precision-manufactured product that goes directly to the ribosome without any cellular copying step. The pathway that can produce pathological truncation does not exist in the vaccination process.

**The researchers' own response.** The Sloan Kettering Institute was sufficiently alarmed by the misuse of their research that they issued a public statement rejecting the Natural News interpretation. In a boldface disclaimer added to the original 2018 news release, the institute stated: "It's important to note that mRNAs are a normal component of all cells and the specific ones discussed here are not involved in mRNA-based vaccines, like the one developed against SARS-CoV-2." The researchers were not ambiguous: they explicitly said their work was being misrepresented and that their findings had no bearing on the safety of COVID-19 vaccines.

**What mRNA vaccines actually do.** An mRNA vaccine delivers a short segment of synthetic messenger RNA into muscle tissue, where it is taken up by cells in the vicinity of the injection site. This mRNA contains the code for producing a specific protein—in the case of COVID-19 vaccines, the spike protein of the SARS-CoV-2 virus. The cell's ribosomes read the mRNA and produce copies of the spike protein. These proteins are displayed on the cell surface, where they are recognized by the immune system, which produces antibodies against them. After the mRNA has been read and the protein produced, the mRNA itself is degraded by normal cellular processes—it does not persist, does not integrate into chromosomal DNA, and does not engage with the nucleus in any way.

This mechanism was not invented for COVID-19 vaccines; synthetic mRNA has been studied as a therapeutic platform for decades, with research into mRNA cancer therapeutics, rare disease treatments, and other applications accumulating throughout the 1980s, 1990s, and 2000s. The Pfizer and Moderna COVID-19 vaccines represented the first mass-market deployment of the technology, but the technology itself was not new and had been studied extensively in pre-clinical and clinical settings.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The belief that mRNA vaccines promote cancer based on Sloan Kettering research is a case study in several converging mechanisms of scientific misinformation—mechanisms that were active before COVID-19 but that reached unprecedented scale and velocity during the pandemic.

**Authoritative name capture.** "Memorial Sloan Kettering" is a name that communicates rigorous, elite, trustworthy cancer research to virtually everyone who has ever had a family member with cancer or followed oncology news. For Natural News's target audience—people skeptical of vaccines and mainstream medicine—the invocation of a prestigious cancer research institution paradoxically amplifies credibility: here is not a fringe voice, but the establishment's own scientists, admitting the danger. The misuse of institutional names is a standard technique in health misinformation, and the Sloan Kettering name was particularly potent given the vaccine's novel technology and the general public anxiety about what synthetic mRNA might do in the body.

**Molecular conceptual compression.** Most readers do not have detailed knowledge of cellular and molecular biology. The word "mRNA" appeared in mainstream awareness almost entirely through the context of COVID-19 vaccines beginning in late 2020. When Natural News asserted that Sloan Kettering had found that "mRNA causes cancer," the average reader had no cognitive framework within which to think about which kind of mRNA, in which cellular context, through which mechanism. "mRNA" functioned as a single, undifferentiated thing. If some research had found a link between mRNA and cancer, that link attached to all mRNA—including the mRNA in vaccines. The scientific distinctions that make the claim false (transcription processes, nuclear interaction, synthetic versus cellular mRNA) were not part of the shared public vocabulary.

**The "they knew" template.** A significant portion of vaccine hesitancy in 2021 was structured around the belief that dangerous information was being suppressed by public health authorities, pharmaceutical companies, and mainstream media. The Natural News claim slotted perfectly into this template: Sloan Kettering had discovered a cancer mechanism; the CDC was telling the public mRNA vaccines were safe; therefore, authorities were knowingly concealing dangerous findings. This narrative structure is self-sealing in familiar ways—any correction or denial is reinterpretable as further evidence of suppression.

**The asymmetry of novelty anxiety.** The COVID-19 mRNA vaccines were, genuinely, the first large-scale use of mRNA vaccine technology in humans. Legitimate scientific uncertainty existed about some aspects of their behavior—not about cancer causation, which had no evidence base, but about questions like the duration of immune response, the behavior of the lipid nanoparticles used to deliver mRNA, and the possibility of rare adverse events. This legitimate uncertainty created ambient anxiety that bad-faith actors could exploit by repackaging it as concern about specific, fabricated risks. Audiences experiencing genuine anxiety about a novel technology were already primed to receive alarming information as plausible.

**Natural News's business model.** Natural News is not primarily a news or commentary organization; it is a supplement and alternative health product sales platform with a publishing arm. Its business model is structured around cultivating distrust of mainstream medicine and pharmaceutical companies, which drives audiences toward the natural health products it sells. The anti-vaccine content that Natural News produces is financially functional: it positions the site as a source of "suppressed" health information and generates traffic, audience loyalty, and purchasing behavior for its supplement lines. This commercial context is rarely legible to users who encounter Natural News content through social media shares, where the source branding may be minimal or absent.

**Pandemic stress and information overload.** The early months of COVID-19 vaccine distribution coincided with extraordinary social stress, political polarization, institutional distrust, and information fatigue. People were consuming more health information than they had ever encountered before, processing it in less favorable cognitive conditions, and frequently encountering contradictory claims from ostensibly authoritative sources. This environment was unusually favorable for misinformation: the normal heuristics people use to evaluate information (Does this match what I know? Does it come from a source I trust?) were under stress, and the emotional stakes associated with vaccine decision-making made motivated reasoning more powerful.

## 4. Verdict

**False.** The claim that Memorial Sloan Kettering research shows mRNA can promote cancer—and that this is relevant to the safety of COVID-19 mRNA vaccines—is false in every meaningful dimension.

The Sloan Kettering research is real, but it studied cellular transcription errors involved in chronic lymphocytic leukemia—a specific disease mechanism in which the cell's own machinery produces truncated copies of mRNA during normal DNA-to-protein production within the cell. This process has no connection to mRNA vaccines, which deliver synthetic, pre-completed mRNA that never interacts with the cell nucleus and never undergoes cellular transcription.

Natural News materially misrepresented the research by treating all mRNA as a single uniform substance and implying that a finding about cellular transcription errors could be generalized to externally delivered synthetic mRNA. The Sloan Kettering Institute explicitly rejected this interpretation in a public statement appended to the original news release.

There is no evidence that mRNA COVID-19 vaccines promote cancer. The specific mechanism Natural News invoked does not exist in the context of mRNA vaccination. The researchers whose work was misappropriated said so directly.

Snopes rated the claim False in a March 8, 2021, article by Alex Kasprak. The rating is correct.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The Sloan Kettering mRNA claim is not an isolated incident—it is one iteration of a persistent genre of vaccine misinformation that combines real science, scientific names, and specialized vocabulary with motivated misinterpretation to produce claims that are difficult for non-experts to evaluate and that leverage institutional credibility against institutional authority.

**The peer-reviewed literature laundering technique.** A recurring technique in health misinformation is what might be called "peer review laundering": using the existence of genuine peer-reviewed research to imply claims that the research doesn't support. The Sloan Kettering mRNA story is a clear example. The 2018 *Nature* paper exists, was peer-reviewed, and studies a real phenomenon. By invoking that paper, Natural News gave its claim the appearance of scientific grounding. The specific interpretation—that the findings apply to mRNA vaccines—required only the suppression of context and mechanism, which is trivially easy when your audience lacks the technical background to detect the gap.

This technique is difficult to counter because it forces fact-checkers into the position of explaining not just that a claim is wrong but why a real piece of research does not mean what it has been made to say—a far more complex communicative task than simply noting that a cited source doesn't exist or is fabricated.

**mRNA vaccines and cancer: the actual research landscape.** It is worth noting for context that the actual research relationship between mRNA technology and cancer runs in the opposite direction from the conspiracy claim. Researchers have been developing mRNA-based cancer treatments—vaccines that train the immune system to recognize and attack tumor antigens—for years. Clinical trials of mRNA cancer vaccines are ongoing for pancreatic cancer, melanoma, and other tumor types. Moderna and BioNTech, the companies that developed the COVID-19 mRNA vaccines, both have cancer vaccine programs that use the same fundamental technology. The idea that mRNA is dangerous to cancer biology is not merely wrong; it actively inverts the direction of the research agenda, in which mRNA is being developed as a cancer-fighting tool.

**Natural News and the information ecosystem.** Natural News, which operates under the ownership of Mike Adams (who uses the persona "Health Ranger"), has been identified by researchers at Harvard, the WHO, and the Guardian's analysis of COVID-19 misinformation as one of the most prolific sources of health misinformation in the English-language internet. The site has been deplatformed from Facebook and some other social networks but continues to operate and distribute content through email lists, Telegram channels, and sites like Brighteon (a video platform Adams also operates). Its articles have been identified as upstream sources for misinformation that circulates through more mainstream-appearing channels, making direct source attribution difficult and expanding the claim's effective reach.

**The role of specialized vocabulary in misinformation.** The mRNA cancer claim illustrates a broader feature of health misinformation: the exploitation of scientific vocabulary to confer authority without conveying scientific meaning. When Natural News writes that "mRNA itself carries cancer CAUSING changes," the phrase borrows the register of molecular biology to make a claim that would be dismissed on sight if stated in lay language ("some molecules in your cells go wrong when you have leukemia, and vaccines use a different kind of molecule, so vaccines cause cancer"). The scientific vocabulary creates the impression of precision and expertise while obscuring the basic conceptual incoherence of the argument. Combating this requires not just fact-checking specific claims but cultivating general scientific literacy—an educational challenge that no individual fact-check can accomplish.

**The cumulative toll of vaccine misinformation.** The Sloan Kettering mRNA claim was one of dozens of vaccine misinformation narratives circulating simultaneously in early 2021. Research on vaccine hesitancy consistently finds that exposure to multiple distinct misinformation claims, even when each is individually disbelieved, cumulatively increases hesitancy through a process sometimes called "junk news flooding." The sheer volume of alarming claims creates a perception that "where there's smoke, there's fire"—that even if no single claim is reliable, the pattern of concern must indicate something real. Understanding the cumulative impact of individual claim corrections, and developing strategies to address this pattern-recognition effect, is one of the active research questions in misinformation studies.

**The Sloan Kettering disclaimer as a case study in researcher response.** The decision by Sloan Kettering to issue a direct disclaimer rejecting the Natural News interpretation of their research was both appropriate and, by early-2021 institutional standards, unusually swift. Many research institutions have historically been reluctant to wade into public misinformation disputes involving their published work, partly out of concern that engagement amplifies the misinformation controversy and partly because of bureaucratic caution about public communications. Sloan Kettering's willingness to directly and clearly state "this is not what our research means" was a model response—and its effect was limited, because the disclaimer reached far fewer people than the original misinformation claim. This asymmetry—in which corrections issued through official institutional channels reach smaller audiences than viral social media claims—is one of the structural features of the information environment that makes health misinformation particularly resistant to correction.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes published its fact-check of the Natural News claim on March 8, 2021, six days after the Natural News article appeared—a relatively fast turnaround in the professional fact-checking timeline. The article was written by Alex Kasprak, who has specialized in science and health misinformation, and was rated False.

The fact-check's methodology followed a multi-step process that is worth examining in detail because it illustrates both the analytical rigor and the communicative challenges of fact-checking scientifically complex claims.

**Authentication.** Snopes first established that the Sloan Kettering research cited by Natural News was genuine—confirming the existence of the 2018 *Nature* paper, the research team, and the institute's own news release. This step is essential because one possible failure mode in science misinformation is fictitious citation: claims that invoke non-existent papers, misidentify real researchers, or fabricate institutional affiliations. In this case, the research was real, which meant the fact-checking task was to explain the misrepresentation rather than the fabrication.

**Mechanism explanation.** The core of the Snopes fact-check involved a careful explanation of how mRNA vaccines work at the cellular level—the distinction between cellular transcription and external mRNA delivery, the role of the ribosome versus the nucleus, and the reason why transcription errors (the mechanism the Sloan Kettering team studied) cannot occur during mRNA vaccination. This kind of mechanistic explanation is unusual in standard journalistic fact-checking and represents the kind of explanatory depth that science fact-checking requires but that standard newspaper corrections do not provide.

The Snopes piece used an analogy to explain the DNA-mRNA-ribosome relationship: the nucleus as a library of instructions, the mRNA as a "work order" sent to a protein-creating factory (the ribosome), and the mRNA vaccine as a work order that arrives pre-completed, bypassing the library entirely. This analogical scaffolding is an important technique for bridging the gap between the molecular biology content required to evaluate the claim and the general audience of the fact-check—helping readers follow the substance of the rebuttal without requiring specialist training.

**Primary source consultation.** The Snopes fact-check incorporated Sloan Kettering's own disclaimer, which constitutes primary-source evidence directly from the institution whose research was misused. This is an important evidentiary step: it shifts the question from "is the fact-checker's interpretation correct?" to "do the researchers themselves agree with the fact-checker's interpretation of their work?" When the researchers explicitly say their work has been misrepresented, that is the most authoritative possible rebuttal to a claim based on misrepresentation.

**Tone and framing.** The Snopes fact-check is notably direct in its characterization of Natural News's claim. Kasprak describes the foundational error as "comically flawed" at the opening and later characterizes the site as having "typed the words 'mRNA' and 'cancer' into a search engine" and concluded its work was done. This rhetorical choice—ridicule rather than neutral refutation—reflects a considered judgment that the claim is not a good-faith scientific error but a deliberate misrepresentation for commercial and ideological purposes, and that treating it with academic evenhandedness would lend it unearned credibility. The approach has been debated in misinformation research: some argue that mockery antagonizes the audiences it most needs to persuade; others argue that clearly marking bad-faith actors as such is important for epistemic hygiene.

**The limits of the correction.** The Snopes fact-check was among the most thorough and technically accurate responses to the Natural News claim. Its reach was nevertheless a small fraction of the original claim's viral distribution. The Natural News article, by March 8, had already circulated across multiple social media platforms, been shared in WhatsApp groups and Telegram channels, and been incorporated into the claim portfolios of anti-vaccine communicators who would continue to reference it for months. The fact-check reached, primarily, people who already trusted Snopes—that is, people who were likely already skeptical of Natural News and unlikely to need the correction. The people most likely to have believed the original claim were least likely to encounter or attend to the correction.

This audience selectivity problem is one of the central unsolved challenges in professional fact-checking. Corrections issued through formal fact-checking platforms tend to preach to the converted. The populations most susceptible to specific misinformation claims are often those with the least exposure to, and trust in, mainstream fact-checking institutions. Developing mechanisms to route accurate information to audiences who distrust the institutions producing that information is a critical open problem in the applied misinformation research field—one that the Sloan Kettering mRNA case illustrates with particular clarity.
