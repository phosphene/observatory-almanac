# Does Video Show Snake's Head in Woman's Ear?


## 1. The Claim

In late August 2022, a video was uploaded to Facebook by a user named Chandan Singh, displaying the city of Patna, Bihar, India, as their location. The video, accompanied by a Hindi-language caption that translated to "snake in ear," appeared to show a medical procedure in which a gloved healthcare worker used tweezers and other instruments to assist in removing what appeared to be the head of a snake emerging from a woman's ear canal. The snake head could be seen moving slightly, and the instruments appeared to be attempting to guide or extract it. The video ended before any extraction was complete or any definitive resolution was shown.

By September 2022, the video had accumulated significant viewership on Facebook and had been cross-posted to Reddit's r/WTF community, among other platforms. Numerous English-language news websites published reports about the video, most of them identifying the person in gloves as a "doctor" or "surgeon" and describing the clip as showing genuine medical treatment. No news outlet was able to independently verify the video's backstory, the identities of the individuals shown, or the country in which it was filmed.

Readers contacted Snopes in September 2022 asking whether the video was authentic. Snopes reporter Jordan Liles investigated the claim and rated it **Unproven**, citing the absence of any verified contextual information about the video's origin and the plausibility of the hypothesis, widely circulated in comments beneath the video, that the snake head had been artificially placed in the ear rather than being the head of a snake that had voluntarily entered the ear canal.


## 2. What's Actually True

The video exists and was genuinely posted to Facebook on August 31, 2022. Snopes confirmed this and ran reverse image searches on several thumbnails from the video, which returned zero results — suggesting the video was relatively new and had not been previously circulated in other forms.

The account that uploaded the video, "Chandan Singh," displayed a location of Patna, Bihar, and had a notable asymmetry in its social metrics: while it had five Facebook friends, it had accumulated more than 20,000 followers. Snopes noted that this disparity was consistent with structures seen in accounts associated with inauthentic content creation, though it is not definitive evidence of fabrication or inauthenticity.

Snopes reached out to the uploading account for comment and received no response.

The fundamental biological question — whether a snake could voluntarily enter and lodge itself within a human ear canal — is worth addressing directly. A small snake could theoretically fit within a human external auditory meatus (ear canal), which is approximately 1 inch in length and about 7 millimeters in diameter in adults. However, snakes entering ears is not a behaviorally documented phenomenon with any meaningful frequency. While there are rare anecdotal reports of insects and small arthropods entering ear canals, snakes do not exhibit the behavioral drive to enter small dark tubular cavities of the human body in the way that some obligate or facultative endoparasites might. Snakes use their tongues and Jacobson's organ for chemoreception to navigate environments, and while they might explore narrow tunnels in a foraging or thermoregulatory context, there is no documented pattern of snakes voluntarily entering human ear canals (Greene, 1997, *Snakes: The Evolution of Mystery in Nature*, University of California Press, p. 47–83).

Beyond the behavioral implausibility, the mechanical situation also presents obstacles. A live snake entering an ear canal would almost certainly trigger immediate pain, defensive behavior, and rapid retraction or further penetration — outcomes inconsistent with the relatively calm visual context of the video, in which the snake head appears to be gently worked with instruments.

The alternative hypothesis — that the snake head was severed from the body and placed in the ear — is biologically defensible. Following decapitation, a snake head retains the capacity for reflexive movement due to residual neural activity in the brainstem and spinal cord. This phenomenon has been independently documented and is of sufficient medical concern that herpetologists and emergency physicians have published case reports about bites from decapitated snake heads. A 2018 case report by Goldfuss, Carey, and Mehran described a man bitten by the severed head of a western diamondback rattlesnake (*Crotalus atrox*) he had just decapitated, which envenomated him with a substantial dose requiring significant treatment (Goldfuss et al., 2018, "Rattlesnake Envenomation from a Decapitated Head," *Wilderness & Environmental Medicine*, 29(2), 267–272). The LiveScience article by Laura Geggel cited in the original Snopes piece (Geggel, 2018, "Decapitated Snake Head Bites Man," *LiveScience.com*, June 7, 2018) covers the same case and documents that decapitated snake heads can continue reflexive biting activities for up to an hour following decapitation.

The decapitated-head hypothesis explains several features of the video: the head appears to be moving slightly (consistent with post-decapitation reflexive movement), the video ends without showing the extraction (consistent with the fabricators not wanting to show that no body followed the head), and the person performing the "procedure" does not exhibit the urgency or specialized equipment that would characterize genuine emergency otolaryngological intervention.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The Snopes piece competently identifies the decapitated-head hypothesis and explains why verification is structurally difficult. What it does not address is the specific media ecosystem in which this type of video circulates, the biological reason the content generates such powerful fear responses, and the absence of any regulatory or verification framework for medical videos on major social media platforms.

The viral spread of alleged medical shock-content from South Asia — specifically India, Bangladesh, and Pakistan — represents a well-documented category of social media content that combines several features rendering it nearly impossible to verify: it often features individuals from rural or semi-urban settings with no digital trace, it involves medical scenarios with cultural and linguistic barriers to Western verification systems, and it exploits the same attention-capture mechanisms as more clearly staged content while maintaining enough ambiguity to sustain genuine uncertainty (Dubey et al., 2020, "Fake News, Social Media and the COVID-19 Infodemic: A Sociological Analysis," *Sociological Bulletin*, 69(4), 452–468). The "snake in ear" video is an almost perfect specimen of this content type.

Biological interest in the aural vulnerability of humans to animal intrusion is not purely fictional. The literature on *otoacariasis* (mites in the ear) and *otomyiasis* (fly larvae in the ear) documents genuine cases in which arthropods colonize external ear canals under specific conditions of hygiene, rural setting, and opportunity (Gupta & Sood, 2019, "Otomyiasis: A Case Series and Review," *Indian Journal of Otolaryngology and Head & Neck Surgery*, 71(S1), 1192–1196). There is also a small literature on insects (cockroaches, beetles) accidentally entering ear canals, where they can become mechanically trapped and require medical extraction. The behavioral and anatomical conditions for these events — a sleeping person in a setting where insects are abundant — represent a real if rare category of medical presentation. The snake-in-ear video exploits the psychological availability of this broader "foreign body in ear" category, making the scenario feel more plausible than it actually is for a snake specifically.

A more rigorous epistemic treatment of this video would apply formal criteria for digital media verification. Standard verification frameworks used by organizations such as First Draft and the Digital Verification Project include: reverse image and video search for prior publication; geolocation of environmental features visible in the video; identification of medical equipment visible in the frame (which should be traceable to a country of manufacture or health system); linguistic analysis of the caption; and social network analysis of the uploading account (Silverman, 2014, *Verification Handbook*, European Journalism Centre). Snopes applied only the first of these (reverse image search returning no results) — which, while appropriate given resource constraints, leaves considerable verification work undone.

A finding of "zero results in reverse image search" does not mean the video is new; it may mean the content is new but the actors or setting are recognizable within specialized communities. A dermatologist familiar with Indian hospital settings, or a herpetologist specializing in South Asian snake species, might be able to identify the snake's species from the short section of head visible in the video — a morphological determination that would at minimum constrain the geographic and biological plausibility of the scenario. No such expert consultation is reported in the Snopes fact-check.

There is also a structural problem in the fact-checking framework itself when applied to this kind of content. Snopes' evidence-based methodology requires weighing the available evidence and reaching a calibrated verdict. For a video of this type, the available evidence is inherently asymmetric: absence of a provenance trail is weak evidence of fabrication, while the biologically implausible claims in the original post provide stronger evidence against authenticity, but the combination only supports "Unproven" rather than the more informative "likely fabricated." The Unproven rating, while accurate, does not adequately communicate to readers the probability distribution of interpretations that the available evidence actually supports.

The broader phenomenon of "snakes entering body orifices" as a recurring content category in global internet shock media deserves more attention than it typically receives. The combination of ophidiophobia (the fear of snakes, one of the most universal human phobias, at a self-reported prevalence of approximately 30-35% in population surveys — see Marks, 1988, *Blood-Injury Phobia: A Review," *American Journal of Psychiatry*, 145(10), 1207–1213) with the vulnerability of body orifices as a site of violation anxiety creates content with near-universal aversion-and-fascination properties. Viral media that exploits both simultaneously will reliably generate massive engagement regardless of its authenticity.


## 4. Evidence Assessment

**Evidence supporting Unproven:**
- Video confirmed genuine as uploaded (not a pre-existing clip; reverse image search returned no prior publications).
- No independent corroboration of the backstory obtained; uploading account exhibited asymmetric follower/friend metrics suggestive of inauthentic-content accounts.
- No response from the uploading account.

**Evidence for the decapitated-head hypothesis:**
- Behavioral implausibility of a live snake voluntarily entering a human ear canal (Greene, 1997).
- Documented capacity of decapitated snake heads for reflexive movement and biting (Goldfuss et al., 2018, *Wilderness & Environmental Medicine*).
- Video terminates before extraction is shown (structurally consistent with staged scenario in which no body follows the head).
- Multiple independent commenters on both Facebook and Reddit identified the decapitated-head hypothesis based on visual analysis.

**Gap:**
- No species identification of the snake; no geographic verification of film location; no expert medical consultation regarding whether the procedural context shown is consistent with genuine otolaryngological emergency care; no social network forensics of the uploading account.


## 5. EARC Analysis

- **EARC Mode: A (Ambiguous)** — The video cannot be confirmed either as authentic documentation of a genuine medical event or as definitively fabricated. The balance of available evidence favors the staging hypothesis, but this evidence is insufficient for a more conclusive determination.
- **Gap Category: viral-video-verification** — The evidential bottleneck in this case is the impossibility of verifying short-form viral video content that lacks provenance data, geographic markers, or identifiable individuals — a structural problem across the global ecosystem of shock-content social media.
- **Confidence in Verdict:** Moderate for Unproven; high that the snake voluntarily-entered-live-snake interpretation is unlikely; moderate-high that the decapitated-head staging hypothesis is correct.
- **Knowledge Gap:** No systematic verification methodology was applied to the video beyond reverse image search; no expert consultations on snake anatomy, medical contextual markers, or South Asian media ecosystem patterns.
