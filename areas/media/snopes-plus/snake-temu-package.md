## The Claim

On February 8, 2024, an Oklahoma woman named Patty Wills posted a photograph to her personal Facebook account showing what she claimed was a live snake found inside a Temu package. According to the post — whose text Snopes transcribed in full — Wills had ordered pants from Temu, the China-based online retailer that had become one of the most downloaded shopping apps in the United States. After the package arrived, she left it in a clothes basket for one week. When she finally opened it, she found a live snake in a sealed, clear plastic bag inside the package.

The post's text read: "Ok friends I ordered some thing from Temu and it's been sitting in a cloths basket for a week now I just took the pants out of it and OMG and yes it was alive."

By the time Snopes examined the claim, the post was approaching ten thousand shares. Commenters on the original post and on shares of it were responding with a range of emotional reactions — alarm, disgust, and humor — and multiple users stated that the story had convinced them to stop ordering from Temu. One user who apparently feared snakes posted a "kill it with fire" GIF. Another added: "Fire is the only option."

The story, as reported, implies a supply chain contamination event: that a live snake was somehow packed into Temu's fulfillment chain in China, survived shipping in a sealed plastic bag for whatever transit time the order required plus one additional week of sitting in an Oklahoma home, and was discovered alive. This is the claim as presented.

## What's Actually True

Snopes correspondent Jordan Liles investigated the claim by consulting two herpetologists and monitoring the response within specialist online communities. The biological verdict was consistent across sources: the account as Wills presented it is almost certainly false, with the far more plausible explanation being that the snake — which was real and alive and photographed — entered the plastic bag in Oklahoma, not in China.

The first expert consulted was John Kleopfer, a herpetologist with the Virginia Department of Wildlife Resources. After reviewing the photograph and gathering additional assessments from two colleagues, Kleopfer told Snopes by email: "It appears to be a juvenile coachwhip, which are native to Oklahoma." He identified the snake as a coachwhip (*Masticophis flagellum*), a native Oklahoma species, and stated unambiguously that "it wouldn't have been able to survive in a sealed bag for a week. So her story that it came from Temu is complete nonsense."

The second expert, Stephen Mackessy — a professor at the University of Northern Colorado with a background in herpetology — evaluated the photograph as "not ideal" for a firm species determination but offered that he agreed with the assessment that it appeared to be a newborn North American racer (*Coluber constrictor*), a species that bears a close resemblance to the juvenile coachwhip. Both the coachwhip and the racer are native to Oklahoma; neither is present in China; both are entirely harmless to humans. Mackessy characterised the "kill it with fire" commentary as evidence of public ignorance about snakes: "It is completely harmless — and regardless, the comments on the original post ('burn it up, fire is the only option') just serve to remind us how fearful people can be when they are ignorant of the actual facts."

Members of the specialist Facebook group "Snake Identification: Discussion and Resources" — which has a membership of approximately 160,000 people — independently reviewed Wills' post when it was shared in the group for analysis. Members identified the snake as a probable juvenile North American racer and assessed that it was "far more likely" that the snake had entered the bag after the package arrived in Oklahoma than that it had traveled from China inside the sealed package.

Mackessy offered an important nuance to the biological picture that prevents the story from being fully closed: he noted that while it is "VERY unlikely" that the snake originated in China, animals do sometimes travel long distances in cargo. Mackessy recounted a personal experience with a small viper that appeared in wicker furniture a Denver business had imported from Taiwan. The basic mechanism — small reptiles capable of tolerating adverse conditions for extended periods potentially stowing away in shipments — is real and documented. However, the specific claim in Wills' story faces two problems: first, the species identification suggests a native North American snake, not a Chinese species; second, coachwhips and racers could not survive in a sealed plastic bag for even a fraction of the time period Wills described.

The Snopes investigation attempted to contact both Wills (by phone and Facebook Messenger) and Temu (by email). Neither responded by publication time, and the article notes it will be updated if responses are received. As of the time of the Snopes article, the claim remained unresolved in the sense that Wills had not confirmed or retracted it, and Temu had not responded to the allegation.

## Why People Believe This

The virality of Wills' post — approaching ten thousand shares within a few weeks — reflects several converging factors that make snake-in-package claims particularly effective at generating panic and sharing behavior.

**The pre-existing suspicion of Temu.** Temu's rapid rise in American e-commerce had been accompanied by significant public skepticism and concern. Snopes had previously investigated whether Temu was "a Communist China-based scam that spies on users" (June 2023) — a claim that had circulated widely enough to require a dedicated fact-check. By early 2024, Temu had become a locus of consumer anxiety about product quality, data security, and supply chain transparency. A snake-in-package claim arrived into this anxious environment and found receptive soil: it confirmed existing suspicions about the trustworthiness of China-based e-commerce in an especially visceral and vivid way. One commenter's statement — "Real or not, this is another reason I will NEVER buy from Temu" — captures the dynamic precisely. For some consumers, the story was true enough in the sense that it confirmed something they already believed, regardless of whether the specific incident occurred as described.

**The fear response to snakes as a cognitive override.** Snakes occupy a distinctive position in human threat-response psychology. Research on human visual attention has demonstrated elevated detection speed for snake-like stimuli compared to other threatening or non-threatening stimuli (Öhman, Arne, and Susan Mineka, "Fears, Phobias, and Preparedness: Toward an Evolved Module of Fear and Fear Learning," *Psychological Review*, vol. 108, no. 3, 2001, pp. 483-522, American Psychological Association). The evolutionary explanation — that rapid automatic snake detection provided survival advantages in ancestral environments — is well-supported by studies showing that snake detection is faster than conditioned through learning and is resistant to extinction in a way that learned responses to non-evolutionary threats are not. The visceral reaction to the idea of finding a live snake in a package — even for people who rationally understand that the specific snake is harmless — draws on this deep biological substrate and produces sharing behaviour that bypasses more deliberate epistemic evaluation.

**The epistemic novelty of the supply chain contamination frame.** The notion that goods manufactured and packed in distant countries might arrive with unexpected contents is not entirely unreasonable. Cargo stowaway events — insects, spiders, small reptiles, and rodents transported inside shipping containers or packed goods — are documented phenomena. The US Department of Agriculture intercepts hundreds of thousands of invasive species at ports of entry every year, including insects, plant material, and occasionally small animals traveling in commercial cargo. This documented background reality gives the claim sufficient plausibility that a casual consumer has no obvious grounds for immediate rejection. The claim is not in the category of "physically impossible" — it's in the category of "extremely unlikely given the specific biological facts, but a category of thing that does happen."

**The sealed bag as false authentication.** Wills' account specifically includes a detail that functions as an authenticating element: the snake was found in a "sealed, clear plastic bag" inside the outer packaging. This detail is psychologically significant because it implies industrial rather than opportunistic origin — a randomly wandering snake would not be found in a sealed bag. The sealed bag implies that the snake was packed, whether accidentally or otherwise, as part of the package's contents. But as herpetologists noted, this detail also refutes the biological plausibility of the story: a juvenile coachwhip or racer could not survive for a week in a sealed plastic bag. The authenticating detail is also the exculpatory detail — from the experts' perspective, the bag being sealed is evidence that the story is not what it appears to be, since the snake would have suffocated. The most probable explanation is that the bag was not sealed when the snake entered it, or that the seal was imperfect.

**The structure of unverifiable social media claims.** Wills' post is a member of an important class of viral claims: first-person accounts of unusual experiences posted to personal social media accounts, accompanied by a photograph that shows the claimed result (a snake) without showing the causal chain (a snake being shipped from China in a package). These claims are structurally difficult or impossible to definitively verify or refute from outside. Wills did not respond to Snopes' contact attempts. Temu did not respond. Without cooperation from the claimant, the evidence base is limited to: (a) the existence of the post and photo, which is confirmed; and (b) the expert assessment of the claim's biological plausibility, which is strongly negative but not logically conclusive. The possibility that something genuinely unusual occurred — a snake that somehow entered the packaging before sealing in a Temu fulfillment facility and survived transit — cannot be reduced to absolute zero, even if experts find it implausible. This irreducible residual uncertainty is what prevents a definitive "False" verdict.

## Verdict

**The claim cannot be verified, but the biological evidence strongly suggests the account is false as stated.**

The herpetological consensus is clear: the snake in the photograph is almost certainly a juvenile North American coachwhip (*Masticophis flagellum*) or a closely related North American racer (*Coluber constrictor*) — species native to Oklahoma, not China. Neither species could have survived in a sealed plastic bag for the week the package reportedly sat unopened in Wills' home. The far more plausible explanation is that the snake entered the bag in Oklahoma, after the package arrived, whether by crawling in from the surrounding environment or through some other local mechanism.

John Kleopfer (Virginia Department of Wildlife Resources, email communication, 28 Feb. 2024) characterised the account as "complete nonsense" on biological grounds. Stephen Mackessy (University of Northern Colorado, email communication, 28 Feb. 2024) concurred that origination in China was "VERY unlikely" while noting the general phenomenon of animal cargo stowaways as genuine. The independent assessment from the Snake Identification specialist community on Facebook aligned with the herpetologists' species identification and skepticism.

Snopes was unable to contact Wills or obtain a response from Temu, and did not rate the claim as definitively "False" — instead the story is categorised as news coverage of an unverified claim. The appropriate practical verdict is that there is no credible evidence the snake came from Temu's supply chain, and considerable biological evidence that it did not.

## The Wider Picture

The snake-in-Temu-package story connects to several larger currents in contemporary consumer culture and information ecology.

**Temu, supply chain anxiety, and the politics of e-commerce origin.** Temu — operated by the Chinese company PDD Holdings, parent of the Pinduoduo platform — launched in the US market in 2022 and grew with extraordinary speed, becoming the most downloaded app in the US within months (Chow, Andrew R., "The Truth About Temu, the Most Downloaded New App in America," *TIME*, 29 Dec. 2022). Its business model — ultra-low price points achieved through direct manufacturer-to-consumer fulfillment from China, bypassing traditional retail intermediaries — disrupted established e-commerce competitors. It also generated substantial controversy: concerns about data security (the spyware allegations Snopes investigated), product safety, suspected regulatory arbitrage around de minimis import thresholds, and the broader political anxiety around US-China trade relations.

This context is essential for understanding why Wills' post generated ten thousand shares rather than, say, two hundred. A story about finding a snake in a package from a US-based retailer would have been startling. A story about finding a snake in a Temu package tapped into a pre-existing reservoir of consumer anxiety and anti-Temu sentiment that amplified its reach far beyond what the underlying factual content warranted. The viral spread was in part a function of the claim's social meaning — what it confirmed about things people already believed — rather than of its factual accuracy.

**The ecology of invasive species transport.** The counterfactual scenario in Mackessy's account — the Taiwanese wicker furniture viper — represents the genuine mechanism by which small animals do occasionally cross international borders in commercial cargo. The United States Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (APHIS) and US Customs and Border Protection maintain active programs to intercept invasive species at ports of entry. The risks are real: invasive snake species have caused documented ecological harm in locations where they established themselves after accidental introduction. The brown tree snake (*Boiga irregularis*), accidentally transported to Guam in military cargo shortly after World War II, devastated Guam's native bird populations in one of the most dramatic documented cases of introduction-driven ecological collapse (Savidge, Julie A., "Extinction of an Island Forest Avifauna by an Introduced Snake," *Ecology*, vol. 68, no. 3, 1987, pp. 660-668, Ecological Society of America). The existence of genuine invasion pathways makes cargo stowaway stories plausible in the abstract, even when specific instances like Wills' claim are biologically implausible.

**Herpetophobia and public attitudes toward snakes.** The comment thread on Wills' post — the "kill it with fire" GIFs, the declarations of permanent Temu boycott — illustrates the disproportionate emotional response that snakes reliably generate. Ophidiophobia (fear of snakes) is one of the most common specific phobias, with prevalence estimates varying considerably across methodologies and populations. Öhman and Mineka's 2001 review article documents the evolutionary preparedness framework — the hypothesis that humans have an evolved predisposition to fear snakes and spiders that makes conditioning toward those stimuli faster and extinction slower than for arbitrary conditioned stimuli. Whether or not the full evolutionary account is correct, the behavioral data are robust: snakes generate intense fear responses in a substantial fraction of the human population, and information about snakes in proximity contexts (in homes, in packages, in beds) produces correspondingly intense responses.

Mackessy's observation — that the "burn it with fire" response reflects people being "fearful... when they are ignorant of the actual facts" — points to the public science communication dimension of this story. Juvenile coachwhips and racers are small, completely harmless, insectivorous snakes that perform genuine ecological services by controlling rodent and insect populations. Educating the public about the biodiversity value and behavioral repertoire of native snake species is a genuine challenge for herpetologists and conservation communicators, and viral moments like the Temu post — in which a clearly harmless juvenile snake is met with violent rhetoric — illustrate the stakes of that challenge.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Jordan Liles' Snopes investigation demonstrates both the strengths and limits of fact-checking claims that rest on unverifiable first-person accounts accompanied by limited photographic evidence.

**The value of expert consultation.** The decision to consult two herpetologists independently — Kleopfer and Mackessy — and to document the discussion within a specialist online community is the methodologically strongest element of the investigation. Expert consensus on species identification and biological plausibility provides the strongest available refutation of the claim short of Wills retracting it or providing additional evidence. The expert consultation methodology is appropriate for this type of claim, where the relevant knowledge is specialised and not accessible to general audience fact-checking.

**Documenting the unverified status.** Snopes chose to treat the story as a news investigation rather than a fact-check with a binary verdict, partly because definitive refutation was not possible. This is an appropriate choice. The "we could not reach the claimant and the claim is biologically implausible" conclusion is genuinely different from "the claim is false" — the former is honest about the limits of the available evidence, while the latter would overstate the certainty of the verdict. The transparency about failed contact attempts (Wills by phone and Messenger, Temu by email) is appropriate documentation practice.

**The publication timing and update commitment.** The article was published February 28, 2024, with a statement that it would be updated if Wills or Temu responded. As of the article's contents, no update had been published. This is a structural challenge for journalism on fast-moving viral claims: the story needs to be addressed promptly while the virality peak is occurring, but the investigation may not be complete at the time of publication. The Snopes approach — publish the available evidence with explicit acknowledgment that the investigation is ongoing — is a reasonable compromise.

**The broader Temu context.** The article includes a reference link to the prior Snopes investigation into Temu's alleged spyware capabilities, providing readers with relevant contextual background on Temu's standing in public discourse. This contextualization is helpful: it gives readers information about previous similar claims without pretending the current story exists in isolation. The implicit message — that Temu has been a target of claims of varying quality and that readers should evaluate Temu-related stories with awareness of this history — is a constructive epistemic contribution.
