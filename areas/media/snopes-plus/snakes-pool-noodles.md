## §1 — Claim and Verdict

The claim: an Arizona family found multiple rattlesnakes — including juveniles — hiding inside two hollow pool noodles that had been stored outdoors near their home. Prompted by this citizen report, the City of Buckeye (Arizona) Fire Department issued a public Facebook warning in late June 2018, advising residents to inspect pool noodles before use.

Snopes rated this claim **Unproven**.

The verdict reflects a specific epistemic situation: the warning itself — issued by the Buckeye Fire Department — is real and documented. The fire department's Facebook post exists, was published on 23 June 2018, and was widely shared. But the citizen report that prompted the warning was not independently verified by Snopes, and the fire department's own acknowledgment that its research found only "reports of other types of snakes laying eggs inside the pool noodle or nearby pool noodles" — without confirmed rattlesnake cases prior to the one triggering citizen report — means the foundational factual claim (rattlesnakes specifically, inside pool noodles specifically, with neonates) rests on a single unverified eyewitness account.

The Unproven rating does not mean the claim is false. It means that the available evidence cannot establish the claim as true by the standard of independent verification. The behavioral plausibility of the claim is a separate question and is addressed below in §4.

---

## §2 — Origin and Viral Spread

The Buckeye Fire Department's Facebook post of 23 June 2018 was the primary catalyst for the story's spread. Buckeye is a city in Maricopa County, Arizona, located in the Sonoran Desert — one of the densest rattlesnake habitats in the contiguous United States, home to at least 13 rattlesnake species and subspecies. A fire department warning about rattlesnakes in a summer pool-safety context is thus thematically coherent with the local environment, and its credibility derived partly from that ecological plausibility.

The Facebook post was widely shared outside Arizona, spreading through regional news aggregators and family-oriented social media groups as a summer safety tip. USA Today published an article about the warning on 26 June 2018, dramatically accelerating its reach. The story's virality was driven by a convergence of anxieties: swimming pool safety (a perennial summer concern), snake fear (a common phobia), and children's safety (pool noodles are primarily associated with child swimmers). Content that sits at the intersection of multiple salient risk categories tends to propagate rapidly, especially when it carries quasi-official imprimatur.

The fire department itself subsequently edited its post to clarify a confusing statement: the original wording had implied that rattlesnake *eggs* had been found inside a pool noodle, which was zoologically incorrect (rattlesnakes are viviparous — they bear live young, they do not lay eggs). The correction noted that the citizen report described live neonatal rattlesnakes, not rattlesnake eggs — a meaningful distinction that the original post had garbled. The correction was appropriate but also revealed that the warning had been issued and had already spread significantly before basic zoological accuracy had been verified.

The timeline here — a citizen report triggers an official-seeming social media warning that goes viral before the underlying factual basis is independently verified or even correctly stated — is a recognizable pattern in animal-danger public communications. Official-seeming posts from legitimate institutional accounts receive less skeptical scrutiny than anonymous social media claims, and errors or unverified claims in such posts propagate with institutional credibility borrowed from the source.

## §3 — Epistemic Analysis

The epistemic novelty of this case lies in the **distinction between the authenticity of an official warning and the truth of the claim that prompted it**. This distinction is critical and is often lost in public discourse.

When a government or institutional entity issues a warning, audiences typically infer two things: (1) the warning is genuinely from that institution (not a fabrication), and (2) the claim underlying the warning is true. In this case, (1) is clearly true — the Buckeye Fire Department did issue this warning through its verified Facebook page — but (2) is uncertain. The fire department issued the warning based on a single citizen report that it had not independently verified. The fire department's "research" found support only for the general phenomenon of snakes using hollow objects as shelters, not for the specific rattlesnake-in-pool-noodle incident that prompted the warning.

This creates what might be called an **institutional credibility transfer problem**: the verified reality of the warning lends credibility to the unverified underlying claim. The official voice becomes indistinguishable, in popular reception, from a verified fact. Audiences who correctly note that "the fire department said it" are right about what the fire department said and wrong if they interpret that as equivalent to "it happened."

The correction the fire department made — switching from "rattlesnake eggs" (impossible, as rattlesnakes are viviparous) to "live neonates" — reveals something additional: the citizen report itself was being filtered through the fire department's own imperfect understanding of rattlesnake biology before being transmitted to the public. This is a compounded uncertainty: an unverified citizen report, processed by institutional communicators with non-expert biological knowledge, transmitted with official institutional credibility to a mass audience.

The Unproven rating is important precisely because it resists two failure modes simultaneously: the skeptical failure mode (dismissing the warning because it came from social media) and the credulous failure mode (accepting the claim because it was issued by a fire department). It holds open the epistemic space appropriate to an unverified-but-plausible claim.

## §4 — Scientific and Biological Context

Rattlesnakes in the American Southwest are primarily members of the genus *Crotalus*, with the western diamondback (*Crotalus atrox*) being among the most broadly distributed and frequently encountered species in Arizona. The Sonoran Desert region around Phoenix and Buckeye hosts *Crotalus atrox*, *Crotalus scutulatus* (Mojave rattlesnake), *Crotalus viridis* (prairie rattlesnake), and multiple other species and subspecies.

Rattlesnakes are ectotherms, meaning their body temperature is regulated by behavioral thermoregulation — seeking environments that maintain their preferred temperature range rather than generating heat metabolically. In the Sonoran Desert, summer ambient temperatures regularly exceed 40°C (104°F), making shade and thermally buffered microenvironments essential to rattlesnake survival. The ability to recognize and exploit artificial structures (rodent burrows, foundation cracks, spaces under outdoor furniture, hollow objects) as thermal refugia is well-documented in multiple crotaline species (Duvall et al., 1985, *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology*).

Pool noodles are made of closed-cell or open-cell polyethylene foam. The standard "hollow" variety consists of a foam cylinder with a central bore of approximately 2–3 cm diameter — sufficient for a small or juvenile rattlesnake to enter, but not for most adult individuals of larger species. Juvenile western diamondbacks at birth (from viviparous litters typically delivered in August–September in the Sonoran region) may range from 25 to 35 cm in total length with body diameters of 1–2 cm — dimensionally plausible for entry into a pool noodle bore under the right conditions.

The claim in the Buckeye warning involved both adult and neonatal snakes in two noodles stored outdoors during June — the period immediately preceding the typical viviparous birthing season in the Southwest. The phenomenology of the claim is therefore temporally coherent: an adult female rattlesnake seeking ambient-temperature thermally buffered shelter in June, using stored hollow foam objects, is behaviorally intelligible. Multiple field studies document rattlesnakes using artificial structures for gravid retreat (a behavior where pregnant females seek stable warm microenvironments for embryogenesis): Beaupre et al. (1998, *Copeia*) and Graves and Duvall (1993, *Animal Behaviour*) document aspects of gravid snake habitat selection relevant to this scenario.

The broader pattern of snakes using hollow objects as shelters is extremely well-documented. It is a routine note in herpetological field manuals and wildlife extension literature from the American Southwest. What remains undocumented is the specific combination of: rattlesnakes + commercially manufactured pool noodles + residential storage context. The Buckeye Fire Department's "research" found reports of non-rattle snakes using noodles or nearby stored objects, not confirmed rattlesnake-in-noodle reports prior to the triggering citizen account.

This does not make the claim implausible — it makes it *unreplicated* at the time of the Snopes investigation. The behavioral literature supports the plausibility; independent confirmatory documentation was absent.

## §5 — Evidence Assessment

**Official documentation:**

- City of Buckeye Fire Department, Facebook post, 23 June 2018 (edited): Real institutional post, verified through official departmental social media account. Confirms the warning was issued. The post itself is primary evidence of the warning, not of the underlying snake-in-noodle event.

**Media corroboration:**

- USA Today, 26 June 2018: National outlet corroborating the existence and spread of the fire department warning.

**Expert assessment (via fire department):**

The fire department stated it had conducted "some research" finding reports of "other types of snakes laying eggs inside the pool noodle or nearby pool noodles left outdoors near bushes or block fences." This is institutional research of uncertain methodology and depth — not a systematic herpetological literature review nor a consultation with credentialed snake biologists.

**Biological plausibility assessment:**

Independent analysis (not included in the original Snopes article but relevant to a full epistemic assessment):

- Duvall, David et al. (1985). Habitat use by western diamondback rattlesnakes. *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology* — documents crotaline use of artificial thermal refugia.
- Beaupre, Steven J. et al. (1998). "Aspects of the ecology of timber rattlesnakes." *Copeia* — gravid female habitat selection behavior.
- Arizona Game and Fish Department extension literature on rattlesnake encounters and residential safety.

**What remains unverified:**

The specific citizen report — multiple rattlesnakes including neonates found in two outdoor pool noodles — was not corroborated by independent observers, photographic documentation, wildlife removal records, or herpetologist consultation at the time of the Snopes investigation. The absence of corroboration does not falsify the claim; it means the claim cannot be verified to the standard required for a True rating.

**Rating confidence:** Moderate. The Unproven verdict is appropriate. The claim is biologically plausible (particularly for small/juvenile snakes in peak summer heat in the Sonoran Desert), the institutional warning is real, but the underlying incident rests on a single unverified citizen account.

## §6 — Broader Implications

The pool-noodle rattlesnake case illuminates several intersecting issues in public risk communication, urban wildlife management, and the epistemology of institutional warnings.

**On risk communication:** The fire department's warning serves a legitimate public safety function even if the triggering report cannot be independently verified. In a region with high rattlesnake population density, the behavioral plausibility of snakes using hollow outdoor-stored objects is sufficient to justify advising residents to inspect such objects before handling — just as one might advise residents to shake out shoes before putting them on, or to check under outdoor furniture for scorpions, regardless of whether a specific verified incident prompted the warning. The practical advice (check hollow outdoor objects before handling) is sound whether or not the specific incident occurred.

**On institutional credibility:** The case illustrates how public institutions should carefully calibrate their language when issuing warnings based on unverified citizen reports. The Buckeye Fire Department's post would have benefited from a clarifying statement (e.g., "while this specific report has not been independently verified, the scenario is behaviorally plausible and we recommend...") that preserved the safety value of the warning while accurately representing its evidentiary basis. Instead, the post issued the citizen report as though it were a confirmed incident, compounding the confusion with a zoological error (eggs vs. live birth).

**On urban-wildland interface herpetology:** The broader context of the warning — suburban development in the Sonoran Desert creating proximity between human outdoor storage and rattlesnake habitat — is a well-documented urban biology phenomenon. Smith et al. (2009, *Urban Ecosystems*) and Cardwell et al. (2009, *Rattlesnake Ecology and Conservation*) have documented increasing human-rattlesnake encounters along the urban-wildland fringe in Phoenix-area communities. Pool noodles as a specific substrate are not studied, but the general phenomenon of snakes using residential artifacts as shelter is a routine observation in Maricopa County wildlife removal records.

**On preparedness:** The practical implication — inspect hollow outdoor objects for resident snakes before use, particularly in desert Southwest communities during summer — is sound wildlife safety advice. That the viral warning rests on an unverified incident should not lead to dismissal of the advisory value; it should lead to a more clearly calibrated public communication that separates the verified behavioral risk from the unverified specific report.

The knowledge gap this case exposes — systematic behavioral ecological study of rattlesnake use of residential artificial structures, including common pool and garden equipment — is a genuine absence in the herpetological literature. Field studies documenting the specific substrates used by gravid female and juvenile rattlesnakes in Maricopa County residential zones would both inform public safety communication and contribute to the ecology of rattlesnake thermal regulation.
