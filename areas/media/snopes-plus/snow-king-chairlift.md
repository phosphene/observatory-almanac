# Is This an Actual Photo of a 1960s Chairlift With No Safety Bar?

## 1. The Claim

A photograph that has circulated across social media platforms for years purports to show a ski chairlift in operation during the 1960s with no safety bar. The image depicts an adult sitting on a simple metal bench suspended from a cable, holding one arm in front of a child perched beside them. Behind the two passengers, a sign identifies the location as the Snow King Chairlift in Jackson, Wyoming, dated June 29, 1965. The image has been shared on X (formerly Twitter), Reddit, Imgur, TikTok, Facebook, and YouTube, typically accompanied by captions like "Safety standards in the 1960s" and expressions of shock that such a precarious arrangement was ever considered acceptable.

Snopes examined this claim in November 2023 and rated it "True," confirming that the photograph authentically depicts a 1960s chairlift without a safety bar. The Snopes article, written by Izz Scott LaMagdeleine, drew on reporting from Buckrail and the Cowboy State Daily, both local Wyoming news outlets, and placed the image in the broader context of Snow King Mountain's lift history. Snopes further noted that some chairlifts today still lack safety bars, citing a 2023 Outside Magazine article reporting that safety bar use is not mandatory at most North American ski areas.

At first glance, the claim appears straightforward: a photograph exists, and it is real. The Snopes verdict of "True" captures the factual accuracy of the image. But the deeper questions — what this photograph reveals about mid-century ski culture, the evolution of safety standards, the economics of mountain resort infrastructure, and the way viral images flatten complex histories — require a more textured examination. This entry engages with the Snopes article directly while extending the inquiry into territory the fact-check did not fully explore.

## 2. What's Actually True

The photograph is genuine. It was taken at the Snow King Chairlift in Jackson, Wyoming, on June 29, 1965, and shows the chairlift operating without a safety bar — a feature that would later become standard across the ski industry. Multiple independent sources have confirmed the authenticity of the image. Buckrail, a Jackson-based media outlet, included the photograph in a March 2021 article about the end of the Snow King Summit Lift's operational life, tracing the chairlift's history from a single chair to a double chair by the late 1950s, its replacement with a Summit Lift in 1981, and finally its replacement with an eight-passenger gondola in December 2021. The Cowboy State Daily also featured the photograph in a May 2023 article whose subheadline declared the images "100% real."

The Snow King chairlift was far from unique in its lack of safety bars. Throughout the 1960s, chairlifts across North America routinely operated without restraint bars, footrests, or any of the protective features that modern skiers and snowboarders take for granted. Skiing itself was a different sport in that era — helmets were rare, binding technology was rudimentary, and the concept of liability-driven safety engineering had not yet penetrated the industry. The photograph does not depict an anomaly; it depicts the norm.

Snopes accurately reported the key contextual facts: the chairlift had been replaced multiple times over the decades, and modern chairlifts sometimes still lack safety bars. The Outside Magazine article Snopes cited reported that safety bar use is not mandatory at most North American ski areas, underscoring the point that the 1960s were not an isolated period of lax safety standards. However, the article's primary finding — that some modern lifts lack bars — obscures important distinctions between the types of lifts that operate without bars today versus the Snow King chair of the 1960s. Modern lifts without safety bars tend to be slower, surface-level or beginner-focused lifts at small mountains, whereas the Snow King chair rose approximately 1,500 feet above the valley floor.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes deserves credit for several important elements of its fact-check. It correctly traced the provenance of the photograph through multiple local news sources, confirmed the chairlift's history through public records and journalism, and placed the image in a broader historical and contemporary context. The article also acknowledged that some modern lifts operate without safety bars, which complicates any narrative that frames the 1960s as a uniquely dangerous era from which the industry has since fully escaped.

However, the Snopes article, like many fact-checks of viral images, is constrained by its format. At approximately 600 words, the article focuses narrowly on establishing the photograph's authenticity and reporting basic contextual information. It does not explore several dimensions that would enrich public understanding.

First, the article does not interrogate the economic and cultural forces that shaped mid-century ski resort infrastructure. The Snow King chairlift was originally installed as a single chair in an era when Jackson Hole was a small ranching town, not the international destination it would become. The decision to operate without safety bars was not merely a question of standards but of cost, materials availability, and engineering priorities. A full history would examine how the ski industry transitioned from a culture of assumed personal responsibility to one of engineered safety, a shift driven in part by the rise of liability lawsuits in the 1970s and 1980s.

Second, the article does not address the way viral images like the Snow King photograph function as cultural artifacts. The image has circulated for years not merely because of its shock value but because it serves as a visual shorthand for a broader narrative about the decline of risk tolerance in American society. The photograph is deployed in debates about overparenting, the erosion of adventure, and the regulatory state. Snopes's fact-check confirms the image's authenticity but does not analyze why the image resonates so powerfully across political and cultural lines.

Third, the article's treatment of modern chairlifts without safety bars is accurate but incomplete. The Outside Magazine article Snopes cites makes clear that while some lifts lack bars, these are typically older, slower installations at smaller mountains. Major resorts have largely retrofitted or replaced bar-less lifts. The 1965 Snow King chair, rising hundreds of feet above the ground, would be unthinkable by modern standards at any major resort. Snopes's "True" rating, while technically correct, may leave readers with the impression that the photograph is merely a curiosity rather than a window into a fundamentally different era of industrial design and risk management.

Fourth, Snopes does not explore the question of who the people in the photograph are. The adult and child remain unidentified, and their relationship — parent and child, instructor and student, or casual acquaintance — is unknown. This anonymity contributes to the photograph's universality but also strips it of specific human context. Knowing who these people were, why they were on the mountain that day, and what they thought about riding without a safety bar would add a dimension that bare verification cannot supply.

## 4. Why This Matters

The Snow King chairlift photograph matters because it operates at the intersection of several important public conversations: the accuracy of viral history, the evolution of safety standards, and the way visual evidence shapes collective memory. Each time this image circulates, it reinforces a narrative — sometimes nostalgic, sometimes horrified — about how much has changed since the 1960s. The photograph is deployed as evidence in arguments about everything from parenting to workplace safety to the nanny state. Understanding what the image actually shows and does not show is essential to having those conversations in good faith.

The photograph also matters for what it reveals about the history of Jackson Hole and the American West. Snow King Mountain, known locally as the "Town Hill," has been a fixture of Jackson's community identity since the 1930s. The chairlift depicted in the photograph was part of a broader infrastructure that transformed Jackson from a remote ranching outpost into a world-class ski destination. The photograph is not just a safety curiosity; it is a document of Western economic development, the rise of the ski industry, and the environmental and social changes that accompanied it.

Furthermore, the photograph's viral life illustrates the dynamics of information spread in the social media age. The image has been shared millions of times across platforms, accumulating meaning with each share. It has been used to illustrate everything from the supposed toughness of earlier generations to the dangers of unregulated industry. By confirming the photograph's authenticity while providing limited context, Snopes's fact-check becomes part of the image's ongoing cultural life — a reference point that will be cited in future shares. The responsibility of fact-checkers in such cases extends beyond verifying the image to helping the public understand what the image signifies within the broader tapestry of American history.

Finally, the photograph touches on a theme that recurs throughout the Truth Vault project: the difficulty of holding two truths simultaneously. The photograph is real; the 1960s chairlift was genuinely bar-less. But the absence of a safety bar does not mean that 1960s skiers were recklessly indifferent to safety, just as the presence of safety bars on modern lifts does not mean that contemporary skiing is entirely safe. The photograph captures a moment in a long, uneven, still-unfolding process of industrial safety evolution. Reducing that process to a single image — or a single Snopes verdict — risks flattening the very history the image helps us glimpse.

Nor should the emotional impact of the photograph be dismissed as mere sensationalism. The visceral reaction most viewers have when seeing the image — a child perched on a bar-less lift hundreds of feet above the ground with only an adult's arm between them and disaster — is a legitimate response to a genuinely alarming scene. That alarm reflects how thoroughly safety norms have shifted in sixty years. Understanding the photograph means honoring that emotional response while also recognizing that the people of 1965 were not behaving irresponsibly by the standards of their time.

## 5. Research Notes

The primary sources for this entry include the Snopes article by Izz Scott LaMagdeleine (published November 27, 2023), the Buckrail article "End of an Era; Snow King Summit Lift Spins for Last Time" (March 2021), and the Cowboy State Daily article "Vintage Photos Of Wyoming's Snow King 'Chairlift Of Death' Has Internet Buzzing" (May 2023). The Outside Magazine article "Put the Chairlift Bar Down! Why I Always Use It" (April 2023), cited by Snopes, provides important contemporary context about the persistence of bar-less lifts in North American skiing.

Additional research into the history of Snow King Mountain reveals that the hill first opened for skiing in the 1930s with a rope tow, making it one of the earliest ski areas in the Rocky Mountain region. The original single chairlift, installed in the 1940s, was replaced by a double chair in the late 1950s — the lift depicted in the viral photograph. The Summit Lift that replaced it in 1981 operated until 2021, when it was supplanted by a gondola. This timeline illustrates the gradual pace of infrastructure investment at smaller, community-owned ski areas.

The broader context of ski lift safety standards in the United States is documented by the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) standard B77, which governs aerial tramways and lifts. The first version of B77 was published in 1959 but did not mandate safety bars. Subsequent revisions gradually introduced requirements for restraint systems. The evolution of the standard reflects not only improving engineering knowledge but also changing legal and cultural attitudes toward risk.

A useful parallel can be drawn with the evolution of automobile safety standards during the same period. Seatbelts were not standard equipment on most American cars until the mid-1960s, and it took another two decades for their use to become mandatory in most states. Airbags did not become widespread until the 1990s. In both the automotive and ski industries, the transition from assumed personal responsibility to engineered safety was gradual, uneven, and driven by a combination of technological innovation, liability pressure, and cultural change. The Snow King chairlift photograph, viewed in this light, is not an outlier but a representative artifact of a society in the process of renegotiating its relationship with risk.

The viral spread of the Snow King photograph can be traced through platform-specific search tools. The earliest identifiable instance of the image on social media appears on Imgur in 2020, though it likely circulated earlier on other platforms. The photograph's persistent virality suggests a deep cultural appetite for "then versus now" visual comparisons, particularly those that dramatize technological and social change.

## 6. Verdict

The Snow King chairlift photograph is authentic. It shows a 1960s chairlift in Jackson, Wyoming, operating without a safety bar, and it is a genuine document of mid-century ski infrastructure. Snopes correctly rated this claim "True." However, the full truth of the photograph extends beyond the verification of its authenticity. The image captures a moment in a broader history of industrial safety evolution, Western economic development, and the changing culture of risk in American life.

The photograph's value as a historical document is real, but its value as evidence in contemporary debates about safety, regulation, and cultural decline is limited. As with many viral images, the Snow King chairlift photograph tells a true story that is also, in important respects, incomplete. The men, women, and children who rode that lift in 1965 were not oblivious to danger; they were participants in a system with different assumptions about risk, liability, and personal responsibility. Understanding those assumptions — and how they have changed — requires more than a photograph and a fact-check. It requires the kind of sustained historical inquiry that the Truth Vault format exists to provide.

The 1965 Snow King chairlift was real. The people who rode it were not reckless. The safety bar that was absent then is now standard, but not universal. All of these statements are true, and together they compose a more complete picture than any single verdict can provide.
