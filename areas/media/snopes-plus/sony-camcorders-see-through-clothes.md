## The Claim

The story resurfaces in cycles on social media, and its longevity is instructive. In September 2025, a Facebook post by the page *The Frustrated Scientist* collected over 18,000 reactions with a version of the account that read, in summary: Sony accidentally released around 700,000 camcorders in 1998 equipped with a feature that could see through clothing under certain conditions. The device — the Sony Handycam CCD-TRV65 — used an infrared NightShot function designed for low-light recording. When used in daylight with an inexpensive infrared filter, it allegedly could "see through certain materials like swimsuits and thin fabrics, creating an effect that looked like X-ray vision." Sony, the story continued, quickly recalled the camcorders and discontinued the feature.

The same account circulates on Instagram, Threads, Reddit, X, and TikTok, each platform giving it fresh amplification as new users encounter it for the first time. Versions of the claim have been in circulation since at least 1998, when the story was genuine news, and persist because they combine several elements that make stories durable: they involve a major brand, they involve a taboo subject (voyeurism), they have the structure of an accidental scandal, and they offer the rhetorical pleasure of "technology gone wrong."

Snopes rated this claim **Mixture** in a September 2025 fact-check by Aleksandra Wrona. The verdict is correct: the claim contains a genuinely true technical core — there was a real infrared feature with real (though limited) clothing-penetration properties under specific conditions — wrapped in false or exaggerated packaging: the recall never happened, and the "X-ray vision" framing substantially misrepresents the actual optical effect.

## What's Actually True

The factual foundation of the story is real. In 1998, Sony's Handycam line included a NightShot feature — a near-infrared illumination and sensing system designed to allow recording in conditions of complete or near-complete darkness. The legitimate intended use cases were precisely those Sony's press materials described: recording sleeping infants in dark bedrooms, capturing nocturnal wildlife, and similar low-light domestic and documentary scenarios.

NightShot worked by emitting near-infrared light (in the 850–1000 nanometre wavelength range) from integrated LEDs and recording the reflected infrared signal on a CCD (charge-coupled device) sensor that was ordinarily blocked to infrared wavelengths by an infrared cut filter. In NightShot mode, this cut filter was shifted out of the optical path, allowing the sensor to respond to infrared wavelengths it would otherwise be insensitive to.

The clothing-penetration story emerged when the Japanese men's magazine *Takarajima* published a report in July 1998 demonstrating that, with an inexpensive infrared-transmitting filter placed over the camcorder's lens, and with the NightShot mode activated in daylight conditions, the camera could detect the infrared radiation naturally reflected or transmitted by thin fabrics. The effect, under those conditions, was that the infrared emissions from the human body beneath thin or lightly-woven garments were detectable to the modified camera, producing an image that suggested the outline or silhouette of the body beneath.

Sony confirmed, after investigation, that the effect could be replicated under very specific conditions. Sony spokesperson Dulcie Neiman confirmed to The Washington Post in August 1998 that engineers had attempted to replicate the Takarajima demonstration and found: "In some very special circumstances — depending on the daylight, the type of clothing, the texture, the colour, the thickness, how much clothing is worn, the distance of the person to the camera — that reported capability could be replicated." Sony subsequently halted shipment of remaining unsold units pending modification. The modification — implemented in subsequently manufactured cameras — prevented the NightShot function from activating in ambient light conditions sufficient to make the effect possible, by blocking the NightShot mode when light levels exceeded a threshold.

Phil Petescia, who served as marketing manager at Sony for the Handycam product line in 1998, confirmed in a 2024 email: "We did not recall the cameras. They could record through thin black clothing if the person was wearing white underwear (or had very light skin) under certain settings enhanced by an additional lens. I went to Macy's in Paramus and bought every black bathing suit they had and we tested it. We halted shipments while a rework was done, preventing that setting."

This is the core verified factual content. The NightShot infrared feature existed. The clothing-penetration effect existed. It was limited, conditional, and substantially less dramatic than "X-ray vision" language implies. Sony halted shipment — it did not recall distributed units. The widely-circulated figure of 700,000 recalled units is not supported by any source.

## Why People Believe This

The Sony NightShot story is a case study in how a genuinely remarkable piece of technology news transforms through twenty-five years of retelling into a simplified, amplified, and partially false legend. Understanding why the false version persists requires examining both the appeal of the story and the properties of its true kernel.

**The scandal structure.** The story has the narrative shape of a corporate blunder-turned-scandal: a company inadvertently releases a product with a voyeuristic capability, is embarrassed when users discover the unintended feature, and is forced into remedial action. This structure is compelling and memorable because it combines corporate vulnerability, inadvertence (which is easier to process morally than deliberate wrongdoing), and a subject — non-consensual observation of bodies — that touches on genuine violation anxieties. Stories with this structure circulate more readily and are retained longer than purely technical accounts.

**"X-ray vision" as a mythological frame.** The idea of seeing through clothing or skin has deep roots in popular culture, from the apocryphal Superman mythos to less-savoury novelty products. The "X-ray specs" — joke binocular-style glasses marketed in the back pages of comics for decades — promised this capability (fraudulently) to generations of purchasers. When a real technology appeared to offer a genuine, if limited, version of this capability, it triggered an existing cultural script about voyeurism and the transgressive power of technology. The fit between story and script ensured wide circulation.

**The limitation problem.** The actual effect was extraordinarily conditional. The conditions required for successful imaging included: the NightShot mode being activated, an additional infrared-pass filter being placed over the lens (a filter that was not sold as an accessory by Sony and had to be separately purchased from a photography supply store), bright daylight conditions, close subject proximity, and thin, lightly-woven fabrics — particularly wet bathing suits or sheer materials. Under less ideal conditions, the effect ranged from weak to non-existent. Steve Uhrig, president of SWS Security, a Maryland surveillance technology company, told The Washington Post that "infrared rays can penetrate through lightly woven clothing and reflect back, but not in real detail. You'll see something big and dense on the other side of the clothing. It's the same illusion as X-ray specs." Steve McFradden, a sales associate at B&H Photo, told CNN Money: "It was nothing. It was intended to see in the dark, not see through clothes."

The gap between the pop-science framing ("see through clothes") and the physical reality ("detect a vague body silhouette under very specific conditions through certain fabrics with an add-on filter in bright sunlight") is enormous. But the pop-science version circulates more efficiently, and each repetition of the story strips away more of the conditionality until the plain claim "the camera could see through clothes" becomes the dominant version.

**The recall myth.** The claim that 700,000 units were "recalled" reflects a common conflation of two different corporate actions: a recall (active retrieval of distributed products from end users, typically involving direct consumer notification and product return logistics) and a shipment halt (stopping the dispatch of unsold inventory from warehouses pending modification). Sony did the latter. It did not do the former. The distinction matters: a recall implies that users had the feature actively removed or received modified units, which did not happen. Users who purchased original NightShot cameras retained them, unmodified, indefinitely. Sony's response was limited to preventing further distribution of unmodified units.

The figure of 700,000 units circulates as though it were the number recalled, but contemporary reporting from 1998 cited different figures for units already shipped versus total units. The New York Times article on the controversy in December 1998 stated that "400,000 units had been sold," and CNN Money also cited approximately 400,000 units shipped to the U.S. market. The 700,000 figure in recent viral posts appears to be an inflation of the actual figure, though its origin is unclear.

**The 2025 amplification cycle.** The story's September 2025 resurgence coincides with no particular relevant event — it is instead a case of algorithmic content promotion feeding on the persistent curiosity value of the story. The Facebook post by *The Frustrated Scientist* was a repackaging of a well-established narrative for an audience that may not have seen previous versions of it. The engagement figures (18,000+ reactions) confirm that the story retains its viral properties more than two decades after the underlying events.

## Verdict

The claim as commonly stated is a **Mixture** of true and false elements. The specific truth is this: Sony released a camcorder with an infrared NightShot feature that, under a narrow and demanding set of circumstances, could reveal body outlines beneath certain thin fabrics. Sony halted shipment of unmodified units and modified subsequent production. The specific falsehood is this: Sony did not recall 700,000 units, or any units. The cameras were not taken back from purchasers. The "X-ray vision" description substantially overstates the actual optical capability, which was conditional, weak by the standards the language implies, and dependent on external accessories not provided by Sony.

The Snopes "Mixture" rating is precisely correct for this evidence. The story is not fabricated from nothing — it has a real event at its core — but the popular retelling distorts the factual record in ways that substantially change the meaning of the story.

## The Wider Picture

The Sony NightShot story is worth examining not just as a fact-check exercise but as a window into how optical physics, privacy law, corporate response strategy, and media amplification interact around consumer technology incidents.

**The physics of near-infrared in consumer optics.** The NightShot feature's unintended capability was a consequence of the physics of near-infrared light and its interaction with common textile fibres. Near-infrared radiation (roughly 750–1400 nm wavelength) behaves differently from visible light when it encounters organic materials. Many natural and synthetic textile fibres are partially transparent to near-infrared wavelengths that they are opaque to in the visible range — a property exploited commercially in remote sensing, quality control in textile manufacturing, and food safety inspection.

The CCD sensors used in consumer video cameras of the late 1990s were inherently sensitive to near-infrared light, which extended their responsivity well beyond the visible spectrum (approximately 400–700 nm). Standard camera design incorporated infrared cut filters — glass or coating layers that blocked wavelengths above approximately 700 nm — to prevent the near-infrared sensitivity of the CCD from producing colour distortion in normal daylight shooting. In NightShot mode, this cut filter was removed from the optical path (physically shifted, in many camera designs) to allow the sensor to use Sony's own near-infrared illumination LEDs for low-light recording.

The problem was that removing the infrared cut filter in daylight did not merely allow the camera to use its own IR LEDs — it allowed it to respond to ambient near-infrared radiation present in sunlight. Sunlight contains substantial near-infrared energy. When near-infrared sunlight illuminated a subject wearing near-infrared-transparent fabrics, the camera could detect the near-IR radiation transmitted through those fabrics from the body beneath, producing the body-silhouette effect.

The additional infrared-transmitting filter required to produce the effect served a different purpose: it blocked the visible light component entering the lens (which would otherwise overwhelm the sensor), leaving primarily the near-infrared signal. This is why the effect required an add-on filter not supplied by Sony.

**The privacy law context.** The NightShot controversy occurred at a moment when video camera technology was becoming widely accessible to consumers for the first time and privacy law had not yet adapted to address the legal implications of consumer video surveillance capabilities. In 1998, there was no U.S. federal law specifically criminalising the use of recording technology to capture images through clothing, and state laws varied widely. The controversy contributed to discussions in several states about voyeurism statutes and their applicability to emerging technology.

Subsequent camera designs — not just Sony's, but across the industry — incorporated safeguards preventing infrared modes from operating in conditions where they could be misused. This is an example of a product safety and privacy response that occurred through voluntary industry action rather than regulatory mandate, driven by reputational pressure and consumer concerns.

**Comparing to the TSA body scanner context.** A related but distinct privacy controversy arose years later around the Transportation Security Administration's deployment of full-body imaging scanners at U.S. airports. These millimetre-wave (Active Millimetre Wave, AMW) and backscatter X-ray scanners were designed to detect concealed objects — weapons, explosives — beneath clothing, raising privacy concerns because they depicted the human body beneath clothing to TSA operators. Unlike the NightShot feature, these scanners were intentional in their design, used in a specific security context with legal authority, and were subject to regulations about operator conduct and image handling. Snopes investigated these scanners in February 2024, finding that the original designs that produced detailed body images were replaced with generic avatar-based displays.

The contrast highlights that "seeing through clothing" as a technology capability exists on a spectrum from accidental and limited (NightShot) to intentional and regulated (TSA scanners) to intentional and unregulated (hypothetical consumer spyware cameras). The Sony case is the least alarming of these scenarios, which may be why Sony's response — a shipment halt rather than a recall — was proportionate rather than panic-driven.

**The corporate communication failure.** The Sony NightShot incident is also a case study in how corporate communications in response to product controversy can contribute to or mitigate false narratives. Sony's response in 1998 was cautious and somewhat ambiguous — while the company acknowledged that the effect could be replicated under "very special circumstances," its messaging did not clearly or emphatically distinguish between what the camera could and could not do, or between a shipment halt and a recall. This ambiguity created space for the more dramatic version of the story to fill in the gaps.

Phil Petescia's 2024 email clarification to Snopes — providing specific detail about Sony's internal testing, the limitations of the effect, and the absence of a recall — is the most concrete first-person confirmation of the factual record that has been publicly documented. That this clarification emerged nearly twenty-six years after the events reflects both the limitations of corporate communications at the time and the durability of journalistic follow-up by fact-checking organisations.

**The media amplification cycle.** The story's persistence over more than twenty-five years illustrates a pattern common to technology myths with genuine foundations: the true kernel is interesting and verifiable enough to give the myth credibility, while the exaggerated elements are memorable and shareable enough to ensure continued circulation. Each amplification cycle — 1998 news, internet-era retelling, Reddit/social media versions, 2025 viral posts — strips away some conditionality and adds some definitiveness, converting "under very specific circumstances, with an add-on filter, in bright daylight, with certain fabrics, you might see a silhouette" into "the camera could see through clothes." The process is not deliberate deception but rather the natural information compression that occurs when complex claims travel across social networks with limited context-carrying capacity.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Aleksandra Wrona's Snopes fact-check does several things well and reflects sound fact-checking methodology for a claim of this complexity.

**Primary source research in historical archives.** The fact-check draws on news articles from 1998 — The Washington Post, The New York Times, CNN Money, Wired, and Associated Press — which constitute the primary documentary record of the events as they occurred. This archival recovery work is important: the events are distant in time and the participants are not all contactable, so the historical record is the best available evidence. Where a primary source is available (Petescia's 2024 email), the fact-check includes it.

**The distinction between "halted shipment" and "recall."** The fact-check correctly identifies this distinction as central to the falsity claim. This is non-trivial: to a lay audience, both actions might seem equivalent (Sony stopped distributing cameras with this feature), but the distinction has real significance for understanding what actually happened and for evaluating the seriousness of Sony's response.

**The role of the conditionality.** The fact-check correctly emphasises the extraordinary conditionality of the effect — the multiple factors that all had to align simultaneously for any imaging to occur. This conditionality is essential to an accurate account but tends to erode in viral retelling.

**What could be richer.** The fact-check could offer more explicit explanation of the near-infrared physics involved, which would help readers understand not just that the effect had conditions but why those conditions existed. Similarly, a more systematic examination of the chain of exaggeration from original 1998 reporting to 2025 viral posts would give readers tools to recognise similar amplification patterns in future claims. But these are enhancements to an already sound core analysis — the Mixture verdict and its supporting evidence are well-reasoned and accurately sourced.

The Sony NightShot story is, in the end, a genuinely interesting intersection of optical physics, product design, privacy ethics, and media amplification patterns. The true version is less dramatic than the false one, but it is more useful: it is a picture of how unintended capabilities emerge from deliberate design decisions, how companies respond under reputational pressure, and how information about technology degrades as it travels. All three of those dynamics remain active and relevant in 2025 — the technology has changed, but the patterns have not.
