# The South American Larvae Breast Rash

## 1. The Claim

In the early 2000s, as email chain letters became one of the dominant vectors for distributing alarming, unverified, and often fabricated stories, few artifacts achieved the notoriety of a single photograph that purported to show a woman's breast covered in what appeared to be numerous small, open pores or holes, each seemingly containing a larval organism. The image was accompanied by a harrowing narrative that w together elements of adventure, medicine, and body horror into a tale that seemed tailor-made for viral propagation.

The story, as it was most commonly circulated, centered on an anthropologist named Susan McKinley. According to the chain letter, McKinley had returned from an expedition in South America and shortly thereafter noticed what she described as a "very strange rash" on her left breast. The narrative held that she initially dismissed the symptom — perhaps attributing it to heat, insect bites, or an allergic reaction — but that the condition worsened over the following days. She developed intense, unexplained pains in the affected area. When she finally consulted a physician, the doctor was reportedly unable to identify the condition. It was only after being referred to a dermatologist that the alarming truth was allegedly discovered: larva were "growing and squirming within the pores and sores of her breast." The chain letter went on to claim that the holes in the breast tissue were approximately five centimeters deep, that the larvae were feeding on fat, tissue, and milk ducts, and that surgical removal was required to extract the organisms.

The photograph itself was deeply unsettling. It showed a female breast whose surface was covered with numerous irregularly spaced openings, each appearing to contain a small, rounded, pale object that could plausibly be interpreted — by a viewer already primed by the accompanying text — as a larva or maggot nestled within a cavity in the flesh. The visual was graphic enough to provoke an immediate disgust response, and the narrative context provided just enough pseudomedical detail to give the story an air of credibility. The combination of visceral imagery and authoritative-sounding exposition was, for many recipients, sufficient to overcome skepticism.

What made the claim particularly potent was the way it evolved over time. The story was not static; it mutated as it passed from one chain letter to another, from one online forum to the next, accumulating new details and new contexts with each iteration. By 2005, later versions of the chain letter had attached a video clip showing maggot-like creatures being extracted from a breast, and by November of that year, the story had been reframed as a consequence of Hurricane Katrina's flooding in New Orleans — a coupling that exploited public anxiety about sanitation and disease in the storm's aftermath. A 2006 variant relocated the story to Zimbabwe and included instructions for women to iron their undergarments as a preventive measure. A 2007 version pivoted entirely, warning about flesh-eating bacteria in newly imported clothing shipments. Each of these iterations carried the same core image (or variations thereof) but draped it in a fresh explanatory narrative designed to tap into whatever anxieties were most current in the public consciousness.

The claim, in all its forms, was that the photograph documented a real medical condition — a parasitic infestation of human breast tissue by insect larvae — and that the specific circumstances varied depending on the version: a South American expedition, a hurricane, contaminated clothing, or an African outbreak. The underlying assertion was consistent: this was a real photograph of a real condition, and the viewer should be alarmed, informed, and prompted to share the warning with others. It was, in essence, a textbook example of what would later become recognized as a distinct genre of internet folklore: the medical hoax chain letter, which leverages disgust and fear to achieve viral spread, using fabricated narratives to give meaning to images that have been stripped of their original context.

## 2. What's Actually True

Stripping away the fabricated narratives and the chain-letter embellishments, what is actually true in this case is remarkably little — but what truth exists is worth identifying precisely, because the boundary between the real and the fabricated is where the most instructive lessons lie.

First and most importantly: the video clip that became attached to the story in 2005 was genuine. It did depict a real medical procedure. However, it had nothing whatsoever to do with South American larvae, Susan McKinley, or any of the other narrative frameworks that had been built around it. The video showed the medical treatment of a Nigerian patient whose breast lesions contained Tumbu fly larvae — the larvae of *Cordylobia anthropophaga*, a species of blowfly endemic to sub-Saharan Africa. Fourteen larvae were extracted from the patient. This condition, known as furuncular myiasis or sometimes specifically Tumbu fly myiasis, is a real parasitic infestation in which the larvae of the fly burrow into the skin — typically through clothing that has been hung outdoors to dry and upon which the fly has deposited its eggs. The larvae then penetrate the skin and develop within subcutaneous tissue, creating boil-like lesions.

The medical literature on this condition is well-established. Tumbu fly myiasis is endemic in parts of Africa and has been documented in numerous case reports and reviews. However, breast involvement is extremely rare. A 2004 medical journal article noted that only "one case has been reported in the English literature" of Tumbu fly larvae infesting breast tissue specifically. This rarity is important context: while the video was real, the idea that breast myiasis is a common or expected consequence of travel to tropical regions is not supported by the medical literature. The condition exists, but it is uncommon enough that a single case warrants a published case report.

Second: the general phenomenon of image circulation and narrative attachment that Snopes describes — in which an image is posted online without context, then accrues fabricated explanatory stories as it spreads — is a well-documented pattern in the study of internet folklore and viral content. This pattern has been observed across countless viral images and videos, from the mundane to the grotesque. It is a real sociological and psychological phenomenon, rooted in the human need to impose narrative order on ambiguous visual stimuli. When people encounter a disturbing image without context, they are not satisfied with uncertainty; they want an explanation, and if none is provided, they will construct or accept one. This is not a deficiency of human cognition but a feature of it — one that viral content exploits, whether intentionally or emergently.

Third: the additional photographs that became attached to the story in 2006, showing severe rashes, infections, and necrosis of the upper chest and breasts, were likely depictions of real medical conditions — but they were almost certainly showing advanced stages of breast cancer or other serious dermatological or oncological conditions, not parasitic infestation. These images, like the primary photograph, had been stripped of their original medical context and repurposed to serve the larvae narrative. They were real photographs of real suffering, but the explanation being attached to them was fabricated.

Fourth: there is no known medical condition that produces the specific visual effect shown in the primary photograph. This is a critical point. While myiasis (infestation by fly larvae) is a real condition, and while the Tumbu fly video was genuine, the primary photograph at the center of the claim does not depict any documented medical condition. The breast tissue shown in the image appears healthy in the areas surrounding the supposed infestation — there is no redness, no inflammation, no necrosis, no signs of the tissue destruction that would inevitably accompany a parasitic infestation of the severity depicted. The holes or pores in the image are too regular, too clean-edged, and too uniform in appearance to be consistent with parasitic lesions. This absence of medical plausibility is one of the strongest indicators that the image is not what it claims to be.

What is true, then, is this: parasitic myiasis is a real medical condition; Tumbu fly larvae can, in extremely rare cases, infest breast tissue; the video attached to the story in 2005 showed a genuine (but unrelated) case; and the pattern of contextless images accumulating fabricated narratives is a real and well-documented phenomenon in the circulation of online content. Everything else — the photograph itself, the Susan McKinley story, the South American expedition, the five-centimeter-deep holes, the feeding on fat and milk ducts — is fabrication.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes, in its fact-check published on April 5, 2003 (and updated subsequently as the story evolved), correctly identifies the core facts of the case and reaches the appropriate verdict: the claim is false. The photograph does not show a breast rash caused by South American larvae. The image is a digital manipulation. The accompanying narrative is fabricated. The video that was later attached, while genuine, documents an unrelated and geographically distinct medical condition. These are all accurate findings, and Snopes deserves credit for investigating the claim at a time when the infrastructure for digital verification was still in its infancy.

Where Snopes excels is in its reconstruction of the image's circulation history. The fact-check traces the photograph's first known online appearance to June 2003, on a website that hosted a collection of manipulated photographs — a context that immediately suggests the image's inauthenticity. Snopes then follows the image's trajectory through the succeeding months: initially carrying no explanatory text, then briefly attributed to "body modification" (a more plausible context given the visual similarity to extreme body modification imagery), and then, within a couple of months, picking up the Susan McKinley larvae story. This timeline is invaluable for understanding how viral narratives form. It demonstrates that the story was not the original context for the image but rather an accretion — a fabricated explanation that attached itself to an already-circulating, contextless picture. Snopes correctly characterizes this as a common online phenomenon: "someone makes an image available, the picture begins to circulate, the original attribution gets lost, people make up stories to explain the now-sourceless picture, and fabricated explanations become attached as it continues to circulate."

Snopes also correctly identifies the visual evidence of manipulation. The fact-check notes that the breast tissue around the supposed infestation appears too healthy — no redness, inflammation, or necrosis is visible — which would be medically implausible given the severity of the alleged condition. And Snopes identifies the likely source of the manipulation: the image appears to be a digital melding of a photograph of a woman's breast and a picture of something similar to a lotus seed pod. This is a perceptive and almost certainly correct assessment. Lotus seed pods (the receptacles of *Nelumbo nucifera*) have a distinctive appearance: a flat or slightly convex surface dotted with numerous small, round cavities, each containing a seed. When this texture is digitally overlaid onto a photograph of a breast, the result looks disturbingly like a surface infested with embedded organisms. The visual resemblance is striking, and the technique — compositing a natural texture onto a human body — is a common one in the creation of digital manipulation art and shock imagery.

The identification of the Tumbu fly video as genuine but unrelated is another area where Snopes demonstrates appropriate rigor. Rather than dismissing everything attached to the chain letter as fabricated, the fact-check carefully separates the real from the false: the video is real, it shows a real condition, but it has no connection to the South American larvae narrative. This kind of granular verification — distinguishing between the entirely fabricated and the genuinely-sourced-but-misattributed — is the difference between a thorough fact-check and a cursory dismissal.

However, there are areas where the Snopes entry could be strengthened, and where a more comprehensive treatment would add significant value.

First, the fact-check does not provide detailed information about the actual medical condition depicted in the video. While it identifies Tumbu fly larvae and notes the Nigerian patient and the extraction of fourteen larvae, it does not explain the life cycle of *Cordylobia anthropophaga*, the mechanisms of transmission (eggs deposited on soil or clothing, larvae penetrating skin upon contact), the geographic distribution (endemic to sub-Saharan Africa, not South America), or the treatment protocols (occlusion to force larvae to surface for extraction, or surgical removal). This information would help readers understand not only why the video is unrelated to the claimed story but also what the actual medical condition involves — knowledge that could be valuable for readers in endemic regions or for travelers.

Second, the fact-check does not deeply explore the cultural and psychological factors that made this particular claim so viral. The combination of disgust, bodily vulnerability, and exoticized threat is a potent formula for viral spread, and the way the story mutated to exploit different anxieties over time (Hurricane Katrina, African outbreaks, contaminated clothing) reveals something important about how viral content adapts to its environment. A more thorough analysis of these dynamics would elevate the fact-check from a simple true/false determination to a deeper understanding of why the claim spread and how similar claims might be countered in the future.

Third, while Snopes correctly identifies the image as a digital manipulation and suggests the lotus seed pod source, it does not provide a technical analysis of the manipulation. In an era when digital forensics tools are increasingly available — reverse image search, metadata analysis, error level analysis — a modern fact-check would benefit from at least describing what tools or methods were used to reach the manipulation conclusion. The 2003 publication date of the original fact-check partially explains this absence, but subsequent updates did not appear to add this layer of analysis.

Fourth, the fact-check treats the various mutations of the story (Katrina, Zimbabwe, flesh-eating bacteria) as essentially interchangeable variants of the same hoax. While this is true at a surface level, a more nuanced analysis would recognize that each variant reveals something specific about the anxieties of its moment. The Katrina variant exploited fears about public health in the aftermath of a natural disaster. The Zimbabwe variant played into stereotypes about African disease and poverty. The flesh-eating bacteria variant tapped into consumer anxiety about globalization and supply chain safety. Each of these deserves examination not just as a mutation of a hoax but as a reflection of how public fear is channeled and exploited in viral content.

Finally, the Snopes entry does not address the ethical dimension of the additional photographs that were attached to the story in 2006. If these images depicted real patients with real medical conditions — advanced breast cancer, necrotic infections, or other serious illnesses — then their appropriation into a viral hoax represents a significant violation of those patients' dignity and privacy. The fact that an image is circulating online without context does not mean it is free for reuse; it likely means it has already been stripped from its original context without consent. This ethical dimension is increasingly recognized as an important consideration in fact-checking and content moderation, and its absence from the Snopes analysis reflects the earlier era in which the fact-check was written.

## 4. Why This Matters

The South American larvae breast rash hoax is not merely a curiosity of early-2000s internet culture. It is a case study of enduring relevance, and its significance extends well beyond the specific question of whether a particular photograph depicts a real medical condition. The hoax matters for several interconnected reasons, each of which illuminates a broader truth about information, trust, and the dynamics of viral content in the digital age.

First, the case demonstrates the remarkable durability and adaptability of viral narratives. The core story persisted for at least four years (2003–2007) and underwent at least four major mutations, each adapting the same basic claim to a new context of public anxiety. This adaptability is a defining characteristic of successful viral content. Unlike a news story, which is tied to a specific event and becomes obsolete when the event recedes into the past, a viral hoax like this one is modular: the image provides the emotional payload, and the narrative provides the explanatory framework, but both are detachable and reconfigurable. The image can be paired with a new story; the story can be paired with a new image. This modularity means that a single piece of fabricated content can have an effective lifespan of years, continuously finding new audiences and exploiting new anxieties. Understanding this dynamic is essential for anyone involved in fact-checking, content moderation, or public health communication, because it means that debunking a specific version of a hoax is insufficient — the underlying image or narrative will simply recombine into a new variant.

Second, the case illustrates the specific mechanism by which contextless images accumulate fabricated narratives. Snopes describes this mechanism accurately: an image appears without context, circulates, loses any remaining attribution, and then has fabricated explanations attached to it. But this description, while accurate, understates the speed and efficiency of the process. In the case of the larvae photograph, the image appeared in June 2003 and had acquired the Susan McKinley story within approximately two months. This is a remarkably rapid accretion, and it suggests that the narrative was not gradually built up through a collective process of myth-making but was likely authored by a single individual or small group and then spread rapidly through the chain-letter ecosystem. The fact that the story was sophisticated enough to include specific details (the anthropologist's name, her field of study, the expedition location, the sequence of medical consultations, the depth of the holes, the specific tissues being consumed) suggests deliberate construction rather than emergent elaboration. This has implications for how such hoaxes are countered: if the narrative is deliberately authored rather than emergently constructed, then identifying and countering the author (or at least the originating source) may be more effective than attempting to debunk each individual variant.

Third, the case highlights the specific vulnerability of medical and health-related content to viral exploitation. Health information has unique characteristics that make it particularly susceptible to viral spread: it is inherently anxiety-provoking (because it concerns the body and its vulnerabilities), it is often difficult for laypeople to evaluate (because medical knowledge is specialized), and it carries an implicit moral imperative to share (because sharing health warnings is seen as protecting others). These three factors — anxiety, information asymmetry, and moral imperative — combine to create a near-perfect environment for viral propagation. The larvae hoax exploited all three: the image provoked visceral disgust and anxiety, the accompanying pseudomedical narrative was detailed enough to seem authoritative but could not be easily verified by most recipients, and the chain-letter format explicitly urged recipients to share the warning with others, particularly women. Any effort to counter health-related viral misinformation must address all three of these factors: providing accurate information to reduce anxiety, making that information accessible and verifiable to reduce information asymmetry, and reframing the moral imperative from "share this warning" to "verify before sharing."

Fourth, the case reveals the way viral hoaxes can cause harm even when they are debunked. The larvae hoax caused harm in multiple ways. It caused fear and anxiety in recipients who believed it, some of whom may have altered their behavior (avoiding travel, ironing undergarments, discarding new clothing) based on fabricated information. It diverted attention and resources from real health concerns, as recipients who were alarmed about phantom larvae were not attending to actual health risks. It exploited and reinforced stereotypes about tropical regions (South America, Africa) as sources of exotic and terrifying disease, contributing to a pattern of xenophobic health anxiety. And, as noted above, it appropriated images of real medical suffering — patients with breast cancer, patients with Tumbu fly myiasis — and stripped them of their context and dignity. The harm caused by a viral hoax is not limited to the specific falsehood it propagates; it extends to the secondary effects of anxiety, misallocation of attention, reinforcement of prejudice, and violation of dignity.

Fifth, the case demonstrates the importance of digital literacy — specifically, the ability to recognize digital manipulation. The primary photograph in this case was, as Snopes correctly identifies, a digital composite combining a photograph of a breast with an image resembling a lotus seed pod. This is a relatively simple manipulation by contemporary standards, yet it was convincing enough to deceive large numbers of people. The key tell was not the manipulation itself but the absence of medical plausibility: the surrounding tissue was too healthy, the lesions too regular, the overall presentation too inconsistent with any known condition. Teaching people to look for these kinds of inconsistencies — not just in images but in narratives — is a critical component of digital literacy. The question is not merely "is this image real?" but "does this image depict something that is medically, physically, and logically possible?"

Finally, the case matters because it is not an isolated incident but a representative example of a recurring pattern. The same mechanism — contextless image, fabricated narrative, viral spread, mutation over time — has been observed in countless subsequent cases, from the "body modification" genre of shock imagery to the modern era of AI-generated content and deepfakes. The specific technology has evolved (from Photoshop composites to generative AI), but the underlying dynamic remains the same: ambiguous visual content, stripped of context, acquires fabricated narratives, and spreads through networks of anxious, well-meaning people who share because they believe they are protecting others. Understanding this case from 2003 is not merely an exercise in historical curiosity; it is a study of a pattern that continues to shape the information environment today.

## 5. Research Notes

The research surrounding this case draws on several intersecting domains: medical parasitology, digital forensics, the sociology of viral content, and the history of internet folklore.

On the medical parasitology side, the key reference is the literature on *Cordylobia anthropophaga*, the Tumbu fly. This species, endemic to sub-Saharan Africa, is one of several fly species whose larvae can cause furuncular myiasis in humans. The adult female fly deposits eggs in soil or on clothing that has been laid out to dry; when the eggs hatch, the larvae penetrate the skin of a human host who comes into contact with the contaminated material. The larvae then develop within subcutaneous tissue, creating boil-like lesions with a central opening through which the larva breathes. Treatment typically involves occlusion of the opening (which forces the larva to surface for air, allowing extraction) or surgical removal. The condition is well-documented in the medical literature, with case reports from endemic regions as well as imported cases in travelers returning from Africa.

The rarity of breast involvement is a notable feature. While cutaneous myiasis in general is not uncommon in endemic regions, involvement of breast tissue specifically is sufficiently rare that a single case warranted publication in a medical journal. The 2004 article cited by Snopes noted that only one case had been reported in the English-language literature. This rarity is consistent with the general pattern of Tumbu fly myiasis, which typically affects areas of the body where larvae can easily penetrate skin that has been in contact with contaminated clothing — most commonly the trunk, buttocks, and thighs. Breast involvement would require specific circumstances (contaminated undergarments worn without washing, for example) and is therefore less common.

It is worth noting that the Tumbu fly is not found in South America. South America has its own myiasis-causing species, most notably *Dermatobia hominis* (the human botfly), which has a different life cycle and clinical presentation. The botfly deposits its eggs on a carrier insect (such as a mosquito), which then transfers the eggs to a human host when it bites. The larvae then penetrate the skin at the site of the bite. Botfly myiasis is a well-known hazard for travelers to tropical regions of Central and South America, but its presentation is typically a single furuncular lesion, not the widespread, multi-lesion pattern depicted in the hoax photograph. The mismatch between the claimed South American origin and the African species shown in the video is a further indicator of the fabrication.

On the digital forensics side, the identification of the primary photograph as a digital manipulation is supported by several lines of evidence. The absence of perilesional inflammation — the normal redness, swelling, and tissue reaction that would surround any wound or lesion of the apparent severity depicted — is a strong indicator of inauthenticity. In real parasitic infestations, the tissue surrounding each lesion would show signs of inflammation: erythema, edema, possible purulent discharge, and in advanced cases, necrosis. The photograph shows none of these. The tissue between the supposed lesions appears normal, even healthy, which would be medically impossible in a real case of this severity.

The likely source material — a lotus seed pod — is a perceptive identification. The seed pod of *Nelumbo nucifera* has a distinctive topology: a roughly hemispherical or flattened surface with numerous small, circular depressions, each containing a single seed. When photographed from an appropriate angle and digitally composited onto a photograph of a breast, the result mimics the appearance of multiple embedded objects in a way that is visually convincing but medically implausible. The technique is essentially a texture overlay: the tonal values and shading of the seed pod image are blended with the underlying breast photograph, creating the illusion of a textured surface. This is a relatively simple manipulation technique, achievable with early versions of image editing software, and it does not require sophisticated 3D modeling or rendering.

On the sociology of viral content, this case fits within a well-documented tradition of medical and body-horror chain letters. The early 2000s were a peak period for this genre, facilitated by the widespread adoption of email and the relative absence of fact-checking infrastructure. Other examples from the same era include the "brown recluse spider bite" photographs (which showed progressively worsening necrotic wounds attributed to spider bites but likely depicting other conditions), the "tapeworm diet" stories, and various purported disease warnings attached to unsettling but contextless images. The genre exploits a particular psychological profile: the combination of disgust (which commands attention), fear (which motivates action), and altruism (which motivates sharing). The chain-letter format is particularly effective because it includes an implicit social obligation to forward — the message is framed not merely as interesting but as a warning that the recipient has a duty to share with others.

The mutation pattern observed in this case — South American expedition (2003) → Hurricane Katrina (2005) → Zimbabwe (2006) → contaminated clothing (2007) — is consistent with the general principle that viral content adapts to exploit current anxieties. Each mutation aligned the core image with a fear that was salient at the time of its appearance: exotic travel (2003), natural disaster and displacement (2005), African disease (2006), and consumer safety (2007). This pattern suggests that the mutations were not random but were strategically (if not always consciously) selected to maximize viral spread by connecting to the most potent current anxieties. This is a dynamic that has been extensively documented in the study of rumor and misinformation, and it continues to be relevant in the era of social media, where the same principle operates at faster timescales and with greater reach.

For researchers interested in the primary sources, the Snopes fact-check provides the essential timeline and documentation. The original image-hosting website where the photograph first appeared is identified in the Snopes entry, though its longevity and accessibility cannot be guaranteed. The medical journal article on Tumbu fly breast myiasis, while cited by Snopes, would need to be located through medical literature databases for verification. The chain letters themselves, in their various iterations, are preserved in various internet folklore archives and fact-checking databases, including the Snopes entry itself.

## 6. Verdict

The claim that a photograph shows a breast rash caused by South American larvae is **false**.

The photograph at the center of this viral claim is a digital manipulation — almost certainly a composite of a photograph of a woman's breast and an image of a lotus seed pod or similar botanical structure. It does not depict any known medical condition. The surrounding breast tissue shows no signs of the inflammation, necrosis, or tissue destruction that would inevitably accompany a parasitic infestation of the apparent severity depicted. The image first appeared online in June 2003 in a collection of manipulated photographs, carrying no explanatory text, and the Susan McKinley narrative was attached approximately two months later as a fabricated explanation for the now-contextless image.

The video clip that became attached to the story in 2005 was genuine but entirely unrelated to the South American larvae claim. It documented the treatment of a Nigerian patient with Tumbu fly (*Cordylobia anthropophaga*) breast myiasis — a real but extremely rare condition. The Tumbu fly is endemic to sub-Saharan Africa, not South America, and the case had no connection to any anthropological expedition. The appropriation of this genuine medical footage into the fabricated narrative represents a particularly insidious form of misinformation, as it combines real evidence with false context, making the overall claim more convincing while violating the dignity of the real patient involved.

The additional photographs attached in 2006, showing severe rashes, infections, and necrosis of the upper chest and breasts, were likely depictions of real medical conditions — probably advanced breast cancer or other serious illnesses — that had been stripped of their original context and repurposed as part of the hoax. Their appropriation represents a significant ethical violation, regardless of the intentions of those who shared them.

The mutations of the story over time — from South American expedition to Hurricane Katrina to Zimbabwe to contaminated clothing — demonstrate the characteristic adaptability of viral content, which reconfigures itself to exploit whatever anxieties are most current in the public consciousness. Each variant was equally fabricated, but each was strategically aligned with a different fear: exotic travel, natural disaster, African disease, and consumer safety.

Snopes's verdict of False is correct and well-supported. The fact-check appropriately identifies the image as a digital manipulation, traces its circulation history, separates the genuine (but unrelated) video from the fabricated narrative, and characterizes the overall phenomenon as an example of the broader pattern in which contextless images accumulate fabricated explanations. The verdict of False is not merely a statement about the specific factual claim — it is a recognition that the entire apparatus of the claim, from the image to the narrative to the mutations, is a constructed artifact of viral culture rather than a representation of reality.

The case stands as an early and instructive example of the dynamics that continue to shape the online information environment: the power of visceral imagery to override critical thinking, the speed at which fabricated narratives can attach to contextless content, the adaptability of viral claims to exploit current anxieties, and the enduring challenge of countering misinformation that is designed — whether by individual authorship or emergent process — to exploit the fundamental mechanisms of human attention, fear, and altruism. The tools and platforms have evolved, but the pattern remains. Understanding it is the first line of defense.
