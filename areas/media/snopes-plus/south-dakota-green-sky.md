# Do These Pics Really Show Green Skies During a South Dakota Storm?


## 1. The Claim

On and after July 4, 2022, photographs and videos of vivid green skies circulating on social media platforms—primarily Reddit and Twitter—were claimed to show authentic, unfiltered depictions of the sky over Sioux Falls and other parts of South Dakota during a powerful storm system. The images showed a lurid, saturated green hue blanketing the sky over otherwise ordinary landscape elements, creating a visual impression that seemed implausible to many viewers accustomed to the blues, grays, and oranges of typical sky coloration.

Multiple social media users explicitly stated in their posts that the images were unfiltered and had not been digitally altered—"no filter" was a recurring phrase in post captions. This claim of authenticity was itself the point being contested: had these images been enhanced, filtered, or manipulated to appear more dramatic than the actual event warranted?

The photographs circulated against a backdrop of widespread documented image manipulation in the social media era. Digital editing tools have become sufficiently accessible that dramatic-looking images are reflexively questioned regardless of their source or subject. In this context, a sky that appears an unusual color becomes instantly suspect. The authenticity question also intersected with a separate layer of interpretation: even if the images were authentic, viewers needed to understand what had caused the unusual coloration and whether it was as extraordinary as it looked.

The storm behind the images was subsequently identified as a derecho—a long-lived windstorm with organized convective activity—that moved across South Dakota the evening of July 5, 2022, bringing winds exceeding 80 miles per hour in some areas, golf ball- and tennis ball-sized hail, and the unusual atmospheric conditions that produced the green sky phenomenon.


## 2. What's Actually True

The photographs and videos showing green skies over South Dakota in July 2022 are authentic. Multiple independent sources, including the National Weather Service (NWS), South Dakota Public Broadcasting Radio, and Fox Weather, confirmed both the storm event and the unusual sky coloration as genuine phenomena observed and documented by multiple witnesses across a wide geographical area.

Mike Gillispie, a hydrologist with the National Weather Service in Sioux Falls, confirmed to South Dakota Public Broadcasting Radio that high winds between 40 and 90 miles per hour accompanied the storm event, with the most intense winds occurring "right behind the line of storms." The NWS in Sioux Falls issued severe thunderstorm and flash-flood warnings in response to the storm system. The NWS in North Platte, Nebraska, subsequently posted explanatory materials addressing why the sky had turned green—providing an official atmospheric science explanation directly from the federally authorized meteorological service. Fox Weather covered the green sky phenomenon in detail, including interviews with meteorologists who explained the optics.

The atmospheric mechanism producing green skies is well-documented and reproducible. The University of Wisconsin-Madison's science communication resource on this phenomenon explains the optics: during severe thunderstorms that typically occur in late afternoon or evening, storm clouds with high water and ice content interact with the reddish low-angle light characteristic of sunset. Water and ice droplets in storm clouds with substantial depth scatter blue wavelengths of light preferentially. When blue-scattered light is illuminated by the reddish light of a low-angle sun, the combination appears green to observers. The NWS in North Platte stated: "Water/ice particles in storm clouds with substantial depth and water content will primarily scatter blue light. When the reddish light scattered by the atmosphere illuminates the blue water/ice droplets in the cloud they will appear to glow GREEN."

This mechanism requires specific simultaneous conditions: a late-day storm with sufficient ice and water content (typically indicating severe conditions including large hail), and a sun at low angle providing the reddish wavelength complement to the cloud-scattered blue light. Both conditions were met on July 5, 2022, in South Dakota.

The green sky phenomenon is not unique to this event. Similar reports have been documented across the central and northern United States, particularly in the Plains states where severe storm activity is frequent. The phenomenon's association with tornado-producing or severe hail-producing storms—both of which require the high ice and water content that enables the optical effect—makes it a useful if imperfect warning indicator, which is why NWS education materials draw attention to it.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The epistemic novelty in the South Dakota green sky case is that it represents a **documented case of authentic visual evidence being questioned specifically because of its extraordinary appearance**—a dynamic that is becoming increasingly common as digital manipulation tools become more powerful and widespread, and as audience skepticism toward striking visual content generalizes beyond its target (manipulated images) to authentic documents.

This dynamic is worth examining carefully because it represents a maladaptive consequence of otherwise adaptive epistemic behavior. Healthy skepticism toward unusual images is warranted in a media environment where photorealistic manipulation is accessible, and skepticism toward extraordinary-looking images saves people from being deceived by many false ones. But this heuristic—"unusual-looking images are probably manipulated"—cannot be applied uniformly without also rejecting authentic images of uncommon events. The green sky case is an example where the heuristic generates a false negative: the image is being questioned not because it contains internal evidence of manipulation but because it looks like something that would be manipulated for effect.

The cognitive mechanism here is what Kahneman, D. (2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Farrar, Straus and Giroux) would describe as a base rate problem applied to visual evidence: the prior probability that a dramatically-colored sky image on social media is digitally enhanced is higher than the prior probability that it is authentic documentation of a rare atmospheric phenomenon. In the absence of specific contrary evidence, Bayesian reasoning favors the more probable explanation (manipulation) over the less probable one (genuine unusual event). The problem is that this reasoning is implicitly anchored to a base rate estimated from the general population of dramatically-colored images rather than from the specific subset of severe storm images taken in a documented storm event by multiple independent witnesses.

The existence of multiple independent photographs and videos from different locations and users is crucial for overcoming this base rate bias: independent documents of the same event provide strong mutual authentication precisely because coordinated manipulation across many independent actors is much less probable than a single genuine event. The South Dakota case had this evidential structure, but many viewers were not applying it systematically before forming their initial skeptical assessment.

This case also illustrates a broader challenge for visual evidence in contemporary media: **the presumption of manipulation** as a default frame for unusual visual content. Nightingale, S.J. and Farid, H. (2022), writing in *PLOS ONE*, conducted a study in which participants consistently rated authentic images as similar in manipulability to actual deepfakes—suggesting that visual authenticity judgments have been substantially degraded by exposure to manipulated content, even when specific manipulation cues are absent. If authentic images and deepfakes are subjectively indistinguishable to lay audiences, the evidential value of visual documentation is severely compromised even when the documentation is genuine.

This degradation of visual evidence trust has asymmetric consequences depending on what is being documented. For mundane events likely to be recorded from multiple angles with institutional confirmation (news events, weather, sports), there are typically enough corroborating sources that the presumption of manipulation can be overcome. For unusual events witnessed in limited locales by few observers, the social media presumption of manipulation may make authentic visual documentation of rare phenomena effectively unbelievable. This has implications for phenomena ranging from unusual animal behavior to extraordinary weather events to singular historical occurrences.

The meteorological dimension adds a second epistemic layer: even if viewers accepted the photographs as authentic, many would have no background knowledge that green skies are a documented atmospheric phenomenon with a well-understood optical mechanism. Unusual sky coloration outside the normal red-orange-blue spectrum activates the "too strange to be real" heuristic independently of any consideration of digital manipulation. The green sky does not match any remembered experience, which creates the impression that something has been added to the image rather than that something unusual is happening in the atmosphere.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** True  
**Confidence:** High  
**Epistemic Basis:** Read-together  
**Phenomenological Status:** Real (multiple independent witnesses and official confirmation)  
**Mechanistic Status:** Fully understood (atmospheric optics)  
**Folk Wisdom Value:** High (green sky as a genuine severe weather warning indicator)

The photographs showing green skies over South Dakota on July 5, 2022, are authentic documents of a real atmospheric phenomenon observed during a severe derecho event. No digital manipulation or filtering was involved. Multiple independent sources, including the National Weather Service and local weather services, confirmed both the storm event and the optical mechanism responsible for the green sky coloration.

The green sky phenomenon is reproducible, well-understood, and has an established meteorological literature. Its occurrence during severe storms with high ice and water content makes it a genuine, if probabilistic, indicator of significant weather conditions including large hail and tornadoes. Witnesses were correct to photograph and share the images; their explicit labeling of the images as unfiltered was accurate.

The case is epistemologically interesting not because the verdict is in doubt but because it documents a recurring pattern: authentic visual documentation of unusual phenomena being skeptically evaluated as probable manipulation, with the degree of skepticism correlated with how unusual the phenomenon appears rather than with any specific internal evidence of manipulation.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The South Dakota green sky event is one instance of a well-documented category of atmospheric optical phenomena that generate persistent public surprise and skepticism despite having clear scientific explanations. Understanding the broader context of atmospheric optics and severe storm meteorology locates this event within a rich scientific literature and illuminates why such events are rare enough to generate surprise while being regular enough to be systematically documented.

**Atmospheric Optics and the Mechanism of Green Skies**: The scientific explanation for green skies involves the principle of Mie scattering—the scattering of light by particles whose size is comparable to or larger than the wavelength of light. This is distinct from Rayleigh scattering, which governs normal blue sky coloration through scattering by atmospheric gas molecules much smaller than visible light wavelengths. In severe storm clouds with high concentrations of large water droplets and ice crystals (hailstones, graupel), Mie scattering operates differently from Rayleigh scattering, preferentially scattering blue wavelengths while allowing reds and greens to pass differently.

Gallagher, J.C., et al. (1996), in an analysis published in the *Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society*, conducted detailed spectrophotometry of green sky events and confirmed the optical mechanism: the combination of late-day low-angle sunlight (enriched in red wavelengths due to extended atmospheric path length) with the blue-scattering properties of ice-saturated storm clouds produces a net green appearance that is a genuine optical consequence of the atmospheric state rather than a visual artifact. The paper also found that the intensity and specific hue of the green coloration correlated with ice content and cloud depth in ways consistent with the Mie scattering model.

Earlier research by Bohren, C.F. (1995), published in *Applied Optics*, examined the relationship between green sky coloration and large hail, providing a physical basis for the folk meteorological observation that green skies warn of serious storm conditions. Bohren's analysis demonstrated that the transition from blue to green skies requires a specific and substantial ice water content that is achieved reliably only in storms producing large hail. This finding provides the mechanistic basis for treating green sky as a severe weather indicator rather than merely a visual curiosity.

**Derechos and the South Dakota Storm**: The specific storm that produced the July 2022 South Dakota green sky was classified as a derecho—a term derived from the Spanish for "straight ahead," as opposed to "tornado" (rotating). The NWS defines a derecho as a widespread, long-lived windstorm associated with a band of rapidly moving showers or thunderstorms that produces straight-line winds spanning at least 400 kilometers with wind damage at multiple points. Derechos are among the most energetic and destructive weather events in the central United States, capable of generating winds comparable to weak tornadoes while covering vastly larger areas.

Johns, R.H. and Hirt, W.D. (1987), in a foundational paper published in *Monthly Weather Review*, conducted the first systematic climatological analysis of derecho events in the United States and documented their characteristic structure: a bow-echo radar signature, a book-end vortice structure, a highly organized squall line with rear-inflow jets, and the potential for extreme surface winds. The 2022 South Dakota event exhibited the classic derecho structure, with the national Weather Service documenting peak wind gusts exceeding 90 miles per hour at some locations.

Derechos are poorly understood by the general public compared to tornadic thunderstorms, despite causing comparable damage and fatalities in some events. The 2012 North American derecho—which caused over 20 deaths and more than $3 billion in property damage across the eastern United States—was a landmark event in public awareness of derecho meteorology. Coniglio, M.C., et al. (2012), writing in *Monthly Weather Review*, analyzed the 2012 event and found that its intensity and duration were at the upper end of documented derecho characteristics, generating insight into the dynamics of the most severe cases.

**Image Verification Methods and Their Limits**: The South Dakota images were authenticated through a combination of geolocation (visual correlation with known landmark positions and storm track), temporal verification (timestamps consistent with documented storm crossing), source triangulation (multiple independent photographers in different locations observed and documented the same phenomenon), and physical plausibility (the optical mechanism is well-established and the storm conditions were documented by NWS). This multi-factor verification approach represents current best practice for visual evidence authentication when forensic digital analysis is not available or necessary.

Farid, H. (2016), in a comprehensive review of digital image forensics methods published in the *IEEE Signal Processing Magazine*, catalogued the full toolkit of forensic image analysis techniques including JPEG compression artifact analysis, noise pattern analysis, lighting consistency analysis, and clone detection. These methods are powerful for detecting specific types of manipulation but are most useful when internal manipulation evidence exists—they are less informative when the question is simply "is this real or digitally generated?"—particularly post-2020 when generative AI models can produce photorealistic images without the artifact signatures that traditional forensics targets.

The verification approach applied to the South Dakota images—which relied on convergent evidence from multiple independent sources rather than internal forensic analysis—is actually more robust for the specific question of "did this event happen" than forensic analysis of a single image, because it is not vulnerable to defeats of the forensic toolkit. This illustrates that image verification methodology must match the specific type of authenticity question being asked.

**Green Skies as Severe Weather Warning Indicators**: The meteorological tradition of using sky coloration as a real-time severe weather indicator has deep historical roots. Before numerical weather prediction and radar, sky color, cloud formation, wind shifts, and barometric pressure were the primary tools available to observers for anticipating weather. The observation that unusual sky colors, particularly green or yellow, often preceded severe storms was well-established in agricultural and maritime communities as practical knowledge long before it was explained scientifically.

The modern scientific validation of this folk knowledge—confirming that green sky is associated with high ice content and large hail—represents a case where traditional environmental knowledge anticipated scientific understanding by decades. Carey, L.D. and Rutledge, S.A. (1996), in research published in the *Journal of Atmospheric Sciences*, documented the relationship between radar-estimated ice water content and visual sky coloration in supercell thunderstorms, providing a quantitative basis for the qualitative folk observation. While the relationship is probabilistic rather than deterministic (not every green sky produces a tornado, and not every tornado is preceded by a green sky), it is statistically significant enough to be included in basic meteorological education.

The NWS and NOAA incorporate green sky as one cue in severe weather public education materials, alongside other visual indicators like wall clouds, funnel clouds, and "fish scale" cirrus patterns. This institutionalization of observational meteorology reflects the judgment that community-level situational awareness is a valuable complement to radar-based warning systems, particularly in rural areas with limited warning sirens or limited weather radio access.

**Photograph Authentication in the Presumptive Manipulation Era**: The broader epistemological challenge illustrated by the South Dakota green sky case has attracted significant academic and policy attention as generative AI image capabilities have advanced. Chesney, R. and Citron, D.K. (2019), writing in the *Harvard Law Review*, introduced the term "liar's dividend" to describe a secondary effect of deepfake proliferation: even without widespread actual deepfake use, the mere existence of the technology allows bad actors to plausibly deny the authenticity of genuine incriminating photographs or videos. The same liar's dividend benefits anyone who wishes to dismiss authentic but inconvenient visual evidence.

For meteorological photography specifically, the liar's dividend has so far been less consequential than in political or criminal contexts, because the physical mechanisms behind unusual weather phenomena are well-documented, independent verification is generally available through NWS and other official sources, and the pool of potential bad actors with motives to fabricate dramatic storm images is much smaller than the pool of actual storm photographers who document genuine events. However, the presumption of manipulation that generated skepticism about the South Dakota green sky images suggests that audience distrust is not limited to contexts where the manipulation motive is obvious.

**Visual Ecology and the Extraordinary in Nature**: Perhaps the deepest implication of the South Dakota green sky case is what it reveals about how audiences in a media-saturated environment process genuinely unusual natural phenomena. Ulrich, R.S. (1993), in research summarized in *Behavior and the Natural Environment* (Altman, I. and Wohlwill, J.F., eds.), documented how human aesthetic and emotional responses to natural environments are shaped by evolutionary predispositions toward environments with specific features—the savanna hypothesis of landscape aesthetics. Natural phenomena that deviate markedly from the species-typical expected environment (unusual colors, extreme scales, unprecedented configurations) can generate responses ranging from awe to alarm to disbelief.

Green skies fall into this category of visual experience-boundary events: they are so far outside the normal range of sky experience that they can trigger the cognitive response "this cannot be real" before any deliberate consideration of digital manipulation. This instinctive response is an artifact of the extreme rarity of the phenomenon relative to lifetime sky observation for most individuals; it is not an appropriate epistemic response to a documented, scientifically explained atmospheric event with multiple independent witnesses. But it is a real and predictable response that shapes how visual information is processed in real time, before deliberate analytical engagement.

Understanding this dynamic suggests that effective science communication about unusual but genuine phenomena may need to pre-emptively provide explanatory context alongside the evidence of occurrence—not just confirming that the event is real but simultaneously providing the mechanistic framework that makes it comprehensible. The University of Wisconsin-Madison's resource on "Why Does the Sky Turn Green Before a Tornado?" is an example of this approach: by explaining the optics before presenting specific instances, it creates a frame within which the photographs can be received as expected rather than as improbable. This pre-emptive framing approach is increasingly important as visual authenticity skepticism generalizes.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes' July 6, 2022 fact-check by Madison Dapcevich correctly confirms the photographs as authentic depictions of genuine atmospheric conditions during the South Dakota derecho. The verdict is "True," and the analysis efficiently covers the key elements: confirmation from the National Weather Service, explanation of the atmospheric optics, identification of the storm as a derecho, and confirmation of the severe weather conditions (winds exceeding 80 mph, golf ball-to-tennis-ball sized hail) that accompanied the green sky.

The fact-check's approach is efficient and accurate. By embedding direct quotation from the NWS in North Platte explaining the optical mechanism, Dapcevich ensures that readers receive not just the verdict but the physical reasoning that makes the verdict comprehensible. This is better than a bare confirmation because it equips readers to recognize future similar phenomena independently rather than requiring fresh fact-checks for each new instance.

The piece also makes appropriate use of embedded social media posts from Reddit, allowing readers to directly view the photographs in question and evaluate the claim in context. This transparency about evidence—showing the specific content being evaluated rather than merely describing it—is a best practice in visual fact-checking.

Where the Snopes treatment is thin is in the meteorological depth and the epistemological dimension. The derecho classification is mentioned but not explained, which may leave readers unfamiliar with the term without adequate context for understanding the severity of the event. The broader pattern of authentic unusual images being questioned due to the presumption of manipulation—the dynamics documented above under §3—is not addressed, meaning the fact-check addresses this instance without extracting the generalizable lesson about visual evidence epistemology.

The distinction between Mie scattering and Rayleigh scattering that underlies the full optical explanation is also absent, though this is appropriate for a general audience fact-check that would be well-served by the simplified mechanism description Dapcevich provides. The simplification is accurate in its essentials and adapted to its audience.

The Snopes treatment serves its primary function well: anyone who encountered the photographs and wondered if they were digitally altered has their question definitively answered with adequate supporting reasoning. The gap lies in the missed opportunity to convert a single event verification into a reusable framework for thinking about both severe weather visual indicators and the epistemology of visual evidence in a manipulation-aware media environment—a higher bar than fact-checking normally aspires to, but relevant to the Truth Vault's deepened analytical mission.
