# Do These Pics Really Show Green Skies During a South Dakota Storm?


## 1. The Claim

On and after July 5, 2022, images of an unusually and strikingly green sky began circulating across social media platforms including Twitter, Reddit, and Facebook. The photographs showed skies above Sioux Falls and other South Dakota locations glowing with a deep, saturated green hue during what was described as a severe storm event. Some images captured the green sky behind a dramatic wall cloud; others showed the green light illuminating city streets and residential neighborhoods from above with an otherworldly quality. The photographs were accompanied by exclamations of surprise and wonder, with many users noting they had never seen anything like it — or questioning whether what they were seeing was real.

The claim in need of fact-checking crystallized around two related but distinct questions. First, are these specific photographs authentic? That is, were they genuine unedited photographs taken in South Dakota on the claimed date, or were they digitally manipulated using filters or compositing to produce a striking visual effect? Second, regardless of the specific images, is a green sky during a severe storm a real meteorological phenomenon — something that can actually happen unedited in the natural world — or is any such photograph inherently suspicious as artificial?

The first question was prompted by the specific viral images and the credibility context of social media in 2022: a period when AI-generated imagery, filter manipulation, and selective use of camera settings to exaggerate atmospheric color were commonplace and widely recognized. The photographically extraordinary appearance of a green sky — sufficiently vivid to be unsettling, sufficiently unusual to be unfamiliar to most viewers — placed the images in a category of content that sophisticated social media users appropriately apply skepticism to before sharing. The second question is broader and more educationally significant: it asks about the physical reality of the phenomenon itself, independent of any particular photographs.

The storm in question was a derecho — a long-lived, organized line of severe thunderstorms with damaging straight-line winds — that swept across South Dakota on the evening of July 5, 2022. Wind gusts of 60 to over 90 miles per hour were recorded in parts of the state; hail the size of golf balls and tennis balls fell from the sky; and severe thunderstorm and flash flood warnings were issued across multiple counties. The National Weather Service office in Sioux Falls confirmed the event, and South Dakota Public Broadcasting Radio reported on the storm's progression and its distinctive sky color. The event was categorized by the National Weather Service as a derecho, a classification reserved for storms meeting specific criteria relating to the extent and consistency of the wind damage swath.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The Photographs Are Authentic**: The specific images that circulated on social media following the July 5, 2022 derecho event in South Dakota are authentic, unedited photographs. Multiple independent sources — local residents posting in real time, professional meteorologists, and the NWS itself — confirmed the green sky over parts of South Dakota during this storm. Local meteorologists, television news coverage, and NWS social media posts corroborated both the storm's severity and its unusual sky coloration. The fact that the photographs were posted by numerous different users from different positions across a wide geographic area, at approximately the same time and correlated with confirmed severe weather alerts, provides strong collective evidence against editing or fabrication.

Madison Dapcevich's Snopes fact-check (published July 6, 2022) confirmed the authenticity through this corroboration approach: verifying the independent storm documentation, the NWS event confirmation, the local news coverage, and the consistency of images appearing across multiple unconnected social media accounts. The photographs need no filter to look extraordinary; the physics of the atmosphere produced the striking visual effect.

**The Physics of Green Skies**: The mechanism by which severe thunderstorms produce green skies is real and explained by atmospheric optics. Under normal daylight conditions, sky color results from Rayleigh scattering: molecules throughout the atmosphere scatter shorter wavelengths (blue light) more efficiently than longer wavelengths (red, orange, yellow), producing the characteristic blue sky we see when looking away from the sun. Near sunrise and sunset, light travels a longer path through the atmosphere before reaching an observer, and the increased Rayleigh scattering removes blue and violet wavelengths, leaving the warmer red-orange-yellow tones of sunrise and sunset light.

The green sky effect during severe thunderstorms involves an interaction between this sunset lighting and the specific optical properties of the very deep, water-laden storm clouds characteristic of supercells and severe convective systems. As described by the National Weather Service office in North Platte, Nebraska, in a widely circulated explanatory tweet and corroborated by atmospheric physics literature: large, deep thunderstorms with substantial liquid water and ice content preferentially scatter blue light because of the size and density of water droplets and ice particles in the cloud mass. When the elevated red-orange light of late afternoon or evening illuminates these blue-scattering storm clouds, the combination of the atmospheric reddish illumination and the cloud's blue scattering produces a visual output that the human eye perceives as green — red + blue = a mixed signal resolved by the visual system as something in the green-yellow range. The precise visual color depends on the relative intensity of the sunset illumination and the depth and water content of the storm cloud.

This optical interaction requires specific conditions that do not occur with every thunderstorm. It requires a storm of sufficient depth and water/ice content — the kind of deep convective column that characterizes supercell thunderstorms and severe derechos, not ordinary afternoon rainstorms. And it requires late-day timing: the reddish component of the illumination is necessary, and it is only present when the sun is low enough on the horizon that atmosphere-filtered light is warm in color. The intersection of these conditions means that green skies occur in specific, documented, and relatively rare combinations of storm severity and time of day.

**Scientific Documentation**: The atmospheric science of green skies has been formally investigated, though the literature is sparser than that on more commonly discussed severe weather phenomena. Bohren and Fraser's analysis of sky color during storms established the framework that later researchers and meteorologists use to explain the effect, emphasizing the interaction between the reddish ambient illumination of evening and the blue-scattering properties of large water and ice particles in deep convective clouds (Bohren & Fraser, 1993, *American Journal of Physics*). Research from the University of Wisconsin-Madison's space science and atmospheric science programs has further documented the phenomenon, and the university's publicly available "Curiosities" science communication series explained the mechanism in accessible terms that the Snopes fact-check appropriately linked.

The presence of large hail is closely associated with green sky observations, and this association makes physical sense: large hail formation requires substantial ice mass aloft, which is exactly the population of scattering particles responsible for the optical effect. Multiple severe weather studies note that green sky is a potential visual indicator of large hail or tornado conditions in the same storm system — not a certain predictor or a universal signal, but a correlation documented enough that NWS weather safety communication includes it as one possible visual cue (Renick & Maxwell, 1977, *Journal of Applied Meteorology*). The South Dakota event involved hail the size of golf balls and tennis balls, consistent with the ice content required for the optical effect.

**The Role of Evening Timing**: The July 5, 2022 derecho storm impacted South Dakota in the early evening, when solar angle was favorable for the reddish illumination component of the green sky mechanism. Sunset in Sioux Falls, South Dakota, in early July occurs at approximately 9 pm local time; at the time the storm moved through, the sun would have been sufficiently low on the horizon to contribute the warm-toned illumination that, filtered through the water- and ice-laden storm clouds, produced the distinctive green visual effect. This timing is consistent with NWS observations about why green skies predominantly occur in late afternoon and evening storm events rather than midday events even with equivalent storm intensity.


## 3. Why People Doubt This

The impulse to question the authenticity of the South Dakota green sky photographs is a rational response to the information environment rather than a symptom of ignorance about atmospheric optics. The doubt reflects the application of an appropriate but imperfectly calibrated epistemic heuristic: visual extraordinariness implies suspect evidence. Understanding why this heuristic is both sensible and insufficient in this case illuminates broader properties of visual evidence in a post-Photoshop information environment.

**The Baseline Rate Problem for Extraordinary Photographs**: Before the widespread availability of digital image editing software and, subsequently, AI-generated imagery, an extraordinary photograph had significantly stronger evidential value than it has today. The technical barrier to producing a convincing fake photograph was high enough that an unusual image from an apparently credible source could be taken at face value with moderate confidence. The progressive reduction of this technical barrier — from early Photoshop filter capability in the 1990s, to consumer-grade RAW processing with extensive color manipulation in the 2000s and 2010s, to generative AI imagery in the early 2020s — has systematically reduced the evidential weight of unusual photographs by increasing the proportion of extraordinary images that are artificial.

A viewer encountering a photograph of a brilliantly green sky in 2022 was not operating in the same evidential environment as a viewer seeing the same photograph in 1992. In 2022, a camera app or photo editing application could produce a nearly identical visual effect in seconds, and the social media sharing incentive for dramatic visual content creates selection pressure favoring unusual-looking photographs regardless of their origin. The appropriate Bayesian response to this changed baseline is increased skepticism toward remarkable photographs, even when no specific evidence of manipulation is present. The South Dakota green sky photographs did not exceed this prior skepticism; they simply required the additional corroboration that confirmed their authenticity.

**Atmospheric Optics and Science Literacy**: The green sky effect is genuinely unfamiliar to most people. While the physics is straightforward to those who have encountered it — a combination of atmospheric scattering and lighting conditions they understand from everyday experience — the specific phenomenon of green skies during storms is not part of the standard science education curriculum in most countries, appears rarely in mainstream science communication, and is not described in common-knowledge general science references. People who have never seen or heard of a green sky before are rationally more likely to question the authenticity of its photographic documentation than people familiar with the atmospheric science.

This is a science literacy gap of a specific kind: not a gap in foundational scientific concepts (the relevant physics — light scattering, color mixing, atmospheric optics — are all reasonably standard) but a gap in applied connection between those concepts and observable phenomena. Knowing that water droplets scatter blue light and knowing that sunsets are red does not automatically generate the inference that their combination should look green, particularly if the observer has never made this inference consciously before. Scientific education tends to emphasize principles in isolation; the application of those principles to specific observable phenomena requires additional conceptual work that does not occur automatically.

**Epistemic Novelty — The Asymmetry Between Authentic Rarity and Manufactured Rarity**: The most significant epistemic dimension of the South Dakota green sky case is what it illuminates about the specific challenge of authenticating evidence for genuinely rare physical phenomena. Rare phenomena face a double bind: their rarity makes them unfamiliar, which makes their documentation suspicious, which creates skepticism that is calibrated to the rate of manipulation in the general population of extraordinary images rather than to the specific phenomenon in question. A photograph of a truly extraordinary physical event — one that occurs once or twice a year under specific atmospheric conditions — is structurally disadvantaged in the credibility it can claim compared to a photograph of a commonplace event, even though the extraordinary event's reality does not make it more likely to be faked.

This asymmetry has a practical implication for how visual evidence of rare but genuine natural phenomena should be authenticated. The appropriate response is not to suspend disbelief when an image is extraordinary — that would eliminate the useful skepticism that catches genuine fakes — but to invest in the corroboration process that the Snopes fact-check demonstrates: seeking multiple independent witnesses across a geographic area, cross-referencing with official meteorological records, checking the timing of publication against the claimed event's timing, and looking for professional meteorologists or scientists commenting on the documented event in real time. When these corroborating sources align — as they did for the South Dakota green sky — the authentication is robust and the original extraordinary photograph receives its appropriate credibility.

The pedagogical lesson here generalizes to other domains of rare atmospheric, biological, and physical phenomena documented by citizen scientists and casual observers: extraordinary visual evidence of real events requires a corroboration strategy, not a binary credibility judgment. The proliferation of high-quality cameras in consumer smartphones has dramatically increased the volume and quality of citizen documentation of rare natural events; building the epistemic norms for evaluating that documentation is itself an ongoing cultural and educational task.

Research on the psychology of visual evidence evaluation demonstrates that people apply lower credibility standards to photographs confirming beliefs they hold and higher standards to photographs challenging beliefs — a visual analogue of confirmation bias documented by Nickerson (1998, *Review of General Psychology*). Green sky photographs may face heightened skepticism from viewers who believe "the sky is blue" as an essentially categorical fact, rather than as a description of sky color under standard daytime illumination conditions. Correcting this misperception requires not just the authentication of the specific image but the provision of the physical framework — atmospheric optics and its context-dependence — that makes the image interpretable.


## 4. Verdict

**Rating: True**

The photographs of green skies over South Dakota during the July 5, 2022 derecho storm are authentic, unmanipulated photographs of a real atmospheric optical phenomenon. Green skies during severe storms are physically real and result from the interaction between the blue-scattering properties of water and ice particles in deeply developed storm clouds and the warm-toned illumination of late afternoon or evening sunlight. The South Dakota event was a severe derecho with wind gusts exceeding 90 mph and hail to tennis-ball size, providing exactly the atmospheric conditions that produce the green sky effect. Multiple independent photographic sources, real-time NWS weather documentation, and local news reporting corroborate the authenticity of the images and the occurrence of the storm.

**Gap Category: Atmospheric Optics**

The case reveals an atmospheric-optics gap: the physical mechanism of green sky formation during severe storms is not widely known to general audiences, creating susceptibility to the hypothesis that such photographs are digitally enhanced even when they are genuine.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The South Dakota derecho of July 5, 2022, was one of several major severe weather events in the United States that summer and fell within a broader pattern of extreme weather that meteorologists and climate scientists have associated with intensifying atmospheric dynamics. A derecho is defined by the National Weather Service as a widespread, long-lived wind storm event associated with a band of rapidly moving showers or thunderstorms; to qualify as a derecho, a storm system must produce a swath of wind damage extending more than 400 kilometers, with wind gusts exceeding 93 km/h (58 mph) at multiple locations (Johns & Hirt, 1987, *Monthly Weather Review*). The South Dakota event met these criteria.

The green sky phenomenon has a historical record in North American severe weather documentation extending well before the era of digital photography. Meteorologists and storm chasers documented green skies accompanying supercell thunderstorms and tornado events on photographic film throughout the latter half of the twentieth century, and the atmospheric science of the phenomenon was being discussed in peer-reviewed meteorological journals before Instagram filters existed as a concept. The physical reality of the phenomenon is therefore not in any scientific doubt; the work of fact-checkers in contemporary cases is simply to authenticate specific images against that established background knowledge.

The wider scientific discussion of green skies, hail occurrence, and severe storm signatures is relevant to severe weather preparedness and public safety. Green sky is not a certain predictor of tornado or large hail — it is one visual indicator among several that may signal dangerous storm conditions overhead. NWS guidance focuses on behavioral preparedness rules (seek shelter immediately on tornado warning, not on visual assessments) rather than visual sky-color interpretation, in part because visual signals can be ambiguous, can occur too late for safe response, and can vary with local conditions. However, awareness that green sky correlates with storm severity may contribute to appropriate threat recognition in populations that might otherwise normalize the unusual appearance of the pre-storm sky.

The South Dakota event also has relevance to the science of citizen science documentation. The immediate flood of high-quality, geotagged, timestamped photographs from dozens of independent witnesses provided meteorologists with a distributed observational record of the storm's optical character across a large geographic footprint. This kind of citizen documentation, when authenticated and aggregated, supplements the point-based NWS surface observation network and radar imagery with visual information about human-observable phenomena that instruments do not capture. The development of norms and tools for authenticating citizen-science weather photographs — including the fact-checking ecosystem that Snopes participates in — contributes to the scientific infrastructure for using this documentation effectively.

Atmospheric optics more broadly is a field of scientific knowledge that overlaps disproportionately with the categories of phenomena most likely to be photographed, shared, and questioned on social media: rainbows, halos, sun dogs, crepuscular rays, unusual cloud formations, and sky coloration events. Each of these phenomena involves real and explicable physics; each is also visually extraordinary enough that authentic photographs frequently attract suspicion of enhancement. Building public familiarity with atmospheric optics — through science education, science communication, and fact-checking that explains the physics alongside the authentication verdict — is a productive response to this recurring pattern.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Madison Dapcevich's Snopes fact-check of the South Dakota green sky photographs, published July 6, 2022, exemplifies best practice for authenticating visually extraordinary photographs of natural phenomena. Rather than relying on image analysis tools alone (which detect pixel-level manipulation but not context), Dapcevich corroborated the specific images against official NWS weather records, local news coverage, and NWS meteorologist explanation of the physical mechanism. This triangulation approach establishes authentication more robustly than any single evidence type.

The fact-check also provides physical mechanism explanation alongside the authentication verdict: not just "the photos are real" but "here is why this physical phenomenon is real and how it occurs." This addition transforms the fact-check from a simple authentication document into a science communication artifact that equips the reader to recognize and explain similar phenomena in the future. A reader who understands the atmospheric optics of green skies after reading the fact-check will not need to consult a fact-checker the next time green sky photographs appear during a severe storm event; they will have the conceptual framework to evaluate them independently.

The broader question of how fact-checkers handle authentic evidence for unusual natural phenomena is itself a meaningful design challenge. The mission of a fact-checking organization is to determine the truth of claims, and the standard claim format — "did X happen? was this real?" — applies straightforwardly to natural phenomena documentation. However, the epistemic value added by a fact-check in these cases extends beyond the binary verdict: it lies in the contextual science available only to those with disciplinary knowledge (in this case, atmospheric physics) that explains why the authenticated claim is true. Fact-checkers who provide this contextual science alongside their verdicts function as science communicators as well as verifiers, multiplying the epistemic value of each piece.

The Snopes fact-check of the South Dakota green sky photographs appropriately rates the claim True and provides the atmospheric physics context that allows readers to understand what they are seeing and why it is physically real. This combination of authentication and explanation represents the most complete fact-checking response to claims about naturally occurring but visually unusual phenomena, and sets a standard applicable across the recurring category of authentic natural photographs that are mistaken for fabrications.
