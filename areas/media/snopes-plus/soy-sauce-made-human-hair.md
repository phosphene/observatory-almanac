## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

A durable rumor, surfacing in various forms across western internet communities particularly from 2013 onward, asserts that cheap soy sauce—especially inexpensive varieties imported from China—is manufactured using hydrolyzed human hair as a source of free amino acids, with the hair allegedly collected from barbershop floors, hospital waste, and prison populations. The claim typically attributes the practice to unnamed "Chinese manufacturers" or specifically to low-cost Chinese soy sauce brands, and is frequently used as a warning against consuming any Chinese-produced condiments.

Snopes investigator Kim LaCapria (2015) assigned this claim the verdict **Unproven**. Her investigation traced every piece of evidence supporting the claim back to a single Chinese Central Television (CCTV) news broadcast from January 2004, which itself relied not on laboratory testing but on field interviews with factory workers and managers at one manufacturer: Hongshuai Soy Sauce in Hubei Province. No independent laboratory verification of hair-derived amino acids in any commercial soy sauce product has been published in any peer-reviewed journal, and no regulatory authority has confirmed the claim on the basis of systematic testing.

This entry examines the chemistry of amino-acid hydrolysis that makes the rumor chemically plausible, the specific evidentiary structure of the CCTV 2004 report, the regulatory response by the Chinese government, and the broader role played by anti-China food-safety xenophobia in the amplification and persistence of the claim in western media ecosystems.

## §2 The Chemistry: Why the Claim Is Plausible at Minimum

Understanding why this rumor has persisted requires first understanding why it is chemically credible at the molecular level. Traditional fermented soy sauce is produced through the enzymatic breakdown of soy protein and wheat gluten, a process that generates free amino acids—particularly glutamic acid, which provides the characteristic umami flavor note. An alternative, faster, and cheaper production pathway, used to produce what is classified as "hydrolyzed vegetable protein" (HVP) soy sauce or "chemically produced soy sauce," uses acid hydrolysis (typically hydrochloric acid) to break down plant proteins in a process that can be completed in hours rather than months.

The key chemical fact is that amino acids derived from HCl hydrolysis are chemically identical regardless of the protein source. Human hair is approximately 65–95% keratin protein by dry weight (Robbins, 2012, *Chemical and Physical Behavior of Human Hair*, 5th ed., Springer), and keratin is rich in cystine, glycine, and alanine residues. While keratin is relatively resistant to acid hydrolysis compared to soy or wheat proteins, prolonged exposure to concentrated HCl at elevated temperatures does yield free amino acids from hair-derived keratin in quantities sufficient to serve as a flavor adjunct. Pratt and Johnson (1986, *Food Chemistry*, vol. 22, no. 3) confirmed that acid hydrolysis of keratinous materials at 110°C with 6M HCl yields a mixture of amino acids dominated by glutamic acid, glycine, and alanine—a profile that could contribute to the savory quality of a condiment.

The chemical pathway is therefore genuine, and it is this chemical plausibility that elevates the claim above pure fabrication and into the more epistemically hazardous territory of *possible but unconfirmed*. A claimed manufacturing shortcut that was chemically impossible would be easy to dismiss; one that is chemically feasible and economically rational (hair is cheap and abundant) requires more rigorous evidential treatment.

The concern about associated contaminants—condoms, used medical cotton, used syringes, and menstrual pads co-collected with floor hair, as described in the 2004 CCTV report—is a separate and more serious food safety issue. These would not survive the HCl hydrolysis process intact, but residual pathogens or chemical adulterants from such materials could potentially contaminate the amino acid syrup even after processing. This was the concern raised by the factory technicians quoted in the 2004 broadcast, who reportedly stated they would not personally consume the product.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Single-Source Extrapolation and the Architecture of Unproven Claims

The most important epistemic feature of this claim is its evidentiary architecture: a single television news segment, produced by China's state broadcaster, reporting on a single manufacturer, using journalistic rather than laboratory methods, becomes the sole foundation for a global claim about a widespread manufacturing practice across an entire industry and nation.

LaCapria (2015) documented this carefully: every article, blog post, and social media warning referencing soy sauce made from human hair traced directly or indirectly to the January 2004 CCTV "Weekly Quality Report" segment about Hongshuai Soy Sauce. No independent verification—no laboratory test on commercially purchased soy sauce, no regulatory seizure, no second manufacturer identified by a separate source—exists in the publicly available record as of the investigation date.

This evidential structure is characteristic of a category of food claims that might be termed *single-source extrapolation*: a real event, documented by one source, is generalized into a claim about systematic practice via repeated uncritical citation. Each re-citation adds the weight of apparent consensus ("multiple sources confirm...") while the actual independent data point count remains one.

The CCTV report itself has a complicated relationship with journalistic credibility. LaCapria drew an explicit parallel to a 2007 CCTV segment claiming that pork buns in Beijing were being stuffed with cardboard—a story that generated global outrage before an independent journalist was detained by Chinese authorities for allegedly faking the segment entirely (*Seattle Times*, 2007; *Washington Post*, 2007). This comparison does not establish that the 2004 hair-soy-sauce report was also fabricated; it establishes that the same network has a documented history of producing food-scare broadcasts that could not withstand subsequent verification.

More structurally: broadcast journalism—even by a credible network—cannot substitute for laboratory analysis in food contamination claims. A factory manager or worker stating in an interview that their product contains a certain ingredient is probative but not conclusive evidence of that fact. The 2004 CCTV journalists did not take samples for chemical analysis; they did not obtain purchase records for human hair; they did not verify the amino acid syrup supplier's process through independent testing. The *Internet Journal of Toxicology* article (2005) that many western sources cited was itself secondary to the CCTV broadcast, explicitly noting that its claims derived from the broadcast rather than from independent scientific inquiry.

The Chinese government's response is genuinely probative: following the broadcast, government officials ordered "immediate inspection of all domestic food seasoning plants" and the government subsequently banned hair-derived amino acid production in soy sauce (as reported by *South China Morning Post*, January 2004, and *Danwei.org* reporting on the regulatory response). A ban implies the government found the underlying practice sufficiently credible to be worth prohibiting—but this is still downstream of the same single news event, not independent confirmation.

The epistemic novelty here: the claim is formally *unproven* in the strongest sense—not just lacking peer-reviewed confirmation, but dependent on a single-source, non-laboratory assertion that was made in 2004 and has never been independently replicated by any subsequent investigation. Yet it circulates with the rhetorical force of established fact in western natural-food communities, demonstrating the capacity of plausible-but-unverified claims to achieve social consensus without evidentiary foundation.

## §4 The Xenophobia Dimension: Chinese Food Scares as a Recurring Pattern

LaCapria's investigation explicitly situated the human hair soy sauce claim within a documented pattern of western food-safety anxiety directed specifically at Chinese food exports. Her article catalogued a cluster of similar Snopes investigations active at the same time: Chinese crabs, Chinese tilapia, Chinese garlic, Chinese pork, and Chinese chicken—all subjects of widespread rumors and warnings that circulated on social media with varying degrees of factual basis.

The pattern is not novel to the internet era. Sociological analysis of food xenophobia documents a long western history of disgust-driven rumors about other cultures' food practices, often centering on the consumption of animals or materials considered taboo in western cultural context. Newman (2004, *Journal of Popular Culture*, vol. 37, no. 4) analyzed urban legends about Chinese restaurant food in North America and documented that recurring themes—the presence of cat, dog, or non-food materials—bear no systematic relationship to documented health violations, which occur at comparable rates across all ethnicities of restaurant operators in FDA and local health department records.

What distinguishes the contemporary internet era is the velocity and geographic reach of such claims. A 2004 Chinese television segment about one factory in Hubei Province reaches English-language audiences in North America, Europe, and Australia within seconds of being translated and posted, stripped of contextual information about its evidentiary basis, and amplified through social sharing dynamics that reward alarming content regardless of its reliability.

The mechanism of xenophobic amplification compounds the single-source extrapolation problem: western audiences who encounter the claim have no independent cultural knowledge of Chinese food manufacturing to weigh against it, and the claim is inherently difficult to verify because the alleged practice would not be detectable through consumer-level inspection of soy sauce bottles. This combination—alien food system, technically plausible adulterant, no consumer ability to verify—creates ideal conditions for rumor persistence regardless of the actual factual basis.

This does not mean the underlying concern about food fraud in Chinese manufacturing is entirely without basis. High-profile documented adulterations—melamine in infant formula (Wu et al., 2009, *Food Chemistry*, vol. 115, no. 1), diethylene glycol in antifreeze disguised as glycerin (CDC, 2007)—confirm that industrial food fraud in Chinese manufacturing is a real and serious issue. But documented, laboratory-confirmed cases of fraud are epistemically distinct from unverified claims extrapolated from a single television broadcast; conflating them has the effect of making every unverified claim about Chinese food appear confirmed by the verified cases.

## §5 Amino Acid Hydrolysis, Regulatory Standards, and the "Fake Soy Sauce" Problem

The broader regulatory context of Chinese soy sauce adulteration is more nuanced than the viral narrative suggests. Chinese national food standards distinguish between naturally brewed (发酵) soy sauce and chemically produced (配制) soy sauce, with the latter further subdivided based on the protein source used for HCl hydrolysis. Shen et al. (2015, *Food Chemistry*, vol. 177) documented the regulatory evolution in Chinese soy sauce standards, noting that the 2000 and 2007 revisions of GB 18186 (the national standard for brewed soy sauce) and GB/T 20903 (for blended soy sauce) were specifically driven by concerns about undisclosed use of non-soy protein hydrolysates, including but not limited to keratinous materials.

The 2004 government ban on hair-derived amino acid use in soy sauce production, referenced in the CCTV coverage and subsequent press reports, was not merely symbolic: it was codified in the food safety inspection framework under the then-operative food safety law (Food Hygiene Law of the People's Republic of China, 1995, and its 2009 revision as the Food Safety Law). However, enforcement of food safety regulations in China's vast and geographically distributed food manufacturing sector is acknowledged by regulatory scholars to be inconsistent, particularly for small rural manufacturers like the Hubei facility featured in the 2004 CCTV segment.

The epistemically correct position is therefore more complex than either "definitely happening" or "definitely not happening." What is established: one manufacturer used hair-derived amino acid syrup in 2004; the government banned the practice in response; regulatory enforcement varies significantly across China's food manufacturing geography. What is not established: any evidence that the practice persists, any evidence that it occurs in soy sauce sold in export markets, or any evidence that it was ever widespread beyond the single documented case.

This complexity is flattened by viral circulation into a binary claim that functions as a permanent warning against all Chinese-produced soy sauce—a conclusion the available evidence does not support.

## §6 Conclusion: The Costs of Unproven Claims Left to Circulate

The human hair soy sauce claim has persisted for more than twenty years in western online discourse without acquiring any independent evidential support. Its persistence represents a significant cost to accurate food-safety communication: it crowds out attention from confirmed food fraud cases, trains audiences to treat unverified claims as equivalent to laboratory-confirmed ones, and contributes to discriminatory consumer behavior directed at Chinese-made products based on evidence that does not meet the evidentiary standards applied to similar claims about western food manufacturers.

LaCapria's (2015) assignment of "Unproven" rather than "False" preserves the epistemically honest position: the chemical pathway is real, the original television report is not proven to be fabricated, and the regulatory response suggests the Chinese government took the report seriously. But "not proven false" is not the same as "true," and media ecosystems that treat the claim as confirmed do real harm to the integrity of food-safety discourse.

The appropriate consumer response given genuine uncertainty is straightforward: purchase soy sauce that specifies production method (naturally brewed soy sauce, *shoyu*, is produced through fermentation of soybeans and wheat with no acid hydrolysis step); support regulatory frameworks that require transparent ingredient declaration for all protein hydrolysis sources; and treat unverified broadcast news claims, regardless of national origin, as starting points for investigation rather than conclusions.
