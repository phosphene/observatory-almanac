# Spam Oreos? Another Fake Flavor Goes Viral


## 1. The Claim

In late May 2015, a photograph began circulating on social media appearing to show an Oreo cookie package branded as "Spam Oreos," combining the imagery of Nabisco's globally recognized black-and-white cookie brand with Hormel's Spam canned meat product, which carries particularly strong cultural significance in Hawaii and the broader Pacific Islands. The package, as depicted in the image, bore the Oreo's unmistakable logo typography, color scheme, and sandwich cookie iconography alongside Spam's distinctive yellow-and-blue labeling cues. The product purportedly combined the cream-filled chocolate cookie with a Spam-flavored filling.

The claim carried a specific structure: this was a genuine forthcoming Nabisco product (or one already in limited distribution), presented as a new Oreo flavor in the rapidly expanding series of novelty cookie varieties the brand had been releasing in previous years. The image circulated widely enough on social media to attract coverage and commentary, with many viewers expressing disgust, fascination, or skeptical amusement, but a meaningful segment apparently treating the claim as plausible.

The precise formulation of the implied claim varied depending on who was sharing the image: some posts framed it as a confirmed product announcement; others shared it as a question ("is this real?"); still others shared it sarcastically while acknowledging it might be fake. The core epistemic question embedded in the image's viral life was simple: had Nabisco actually produced Spam-flavored Oreos?


## 2. What's Actually True

The Spam Oreos image was fabricated. There was and has been no Spam-flavored Oreo cookie produced or announced by Nabisco. The image was created by Boss Hawaii, a web design studio, specifically for the purpose of generating social media traffic to a Facebook page called Hawaii Food Porn. The studio openly acknowledged the fabrication after the image went viral, with a credited creator stating in a Facebook post: "I created this for a new Facebook page called Hawaii Food Porn in order to generate traffic to the page and build a following. The page got 500 likes in 2 days thanks to SPOREOS."

The creator's own characterization frames the fabrication as a strategic social media growth tactic—a piece of deliberately constructed viral bait—rather than a hoax intended to deceive people about a specific product's existence. This distinction between "deceptive hoax" and "strategic viral content" is relevant to the case's analysis and is addressed in Section 3.

The context in which the Spam Oreos image achieved plausibility is important. Nabisco had, in the years leading up to 2015, genuinely released an expanding roster of unconventional Oreo flavors that made unusual flavor announcements credible. Real Oreo products available or announced in the 2014-2015 period included Red Velvet Cake Oreos (documented by Snopes as real in a separate fact-check), S'Mores Oreos (announced May 2015 and confirmed real in another Snopes article), Lemon Oreos, Pumpkin Spice Oreos, and Watermelon Oreos, among others. The brand had effectively established a novelty flavor announcement pattern that primed audiences to process unusual Oreo flavor announcements as genuine product news rather than automatically treating them as suspicious.

However, Nabisco had also been on the receiving end of several fake flavor announcements in the same period, none of which were genuine products. Snopes documented the same pattern in: Bacon Cream Oreos (March 2015, fake), Fried Chicken Oreos (documented by Snopes as fake), and Cheeseburger Oreos (May 2015, fake). All three involved meat-based flavor concepts applied to the Oreo cookie format. The Spam Oreos case fits directly within this documented series of meat-flavor Oreo hoaxes, which collectively constitute an identifiable content genre with its own production logic.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The Spam Oreos case illustrates the mechanics of deliberate viral fabrication with unusual clarity because the fabricator openly disclosed their methods, motivations, and measured outcomes. This transparency transforms what is usually an opaque case study in misinformation spread into something close to a documented experiment.

**The Credibility Threshold Reduction Through Real Precedent**: The single most important factor enabling the Spam Oreos image to achieve viral spread is that Nabisco had genuinely released demonstrably bizarre Oreo flavor combinations in the immediately preceding period. Red Velvet Cake Oreos, Watermelon Oreos, and S'Mores Oreos are not natural flavor pairings for a chocolate sandwich cookie; their genuine existence communicated to audiences that the Oreo brand had entered a phase of experimentation where essentially any flavor claim should be taken seriously. When a brand's authentic behavior becomes sufficiently unusual, the cognitive distinction between unusual-but-real and unusual-but-fake collapses, because the category "too strange to be real" has been effectively abolished by the brand's own track record. Snopes's Dan Evon explicitly noted this mechanism in the original fact-check: "many viewers were skeptical of the new flavor, but some thought it plausible given that Nabisco has released several interesting varieties of Oreos in recent years."

Marketing research on novelty product perception has examined how the establishment of innovation precedent affects credibility thresholds for subsequent product announcements. Brand credibility theory suggests that once a brand has demonstrated willingness to pursue extreme flavor innovation, consumer belief in any specific new flavor announcement increases proportionally to the perceived innovation distance of prior confirmed launches (Erdem & Swait, 1998, *Journal of Marketing Research*). The Spam Oreo fake was particularly well-positioned to exploit this dynamic because its Hawaii-specific cultural resonance (Spam is a culturally significant food in Hawaii to an extent with no close parallel in other US regions) gave the concept plausible market logic: it could have been a limited regional release specifically targeted at the Hawaiian market.

**The Deliberate Fabrication Motivation Structure**: The Boss Hawaii disclosure is unusually informative about the practical incentives driving this category of fake content. The fabricated image was not created to deceive consumers in a financially harmful way, to damage Nabisco's reputation, or to advance any political or ideological agenda. It was created as a traffic-generation instrument: an emotionally engaging piece of content designed to activate sharing behavior and route the resulting traffic to a specific Facebook page. The metric of success cited by the creator—"500 likes in 2 days thanks to SPOREOS"—reveals the precise KPI that motivated the fabrication.

This motivation structure is analytically important for understanding the broader class of food product hoaxes. Many fake product announcements in the social media era are not malicious deceptions but strategic content tools. The emotional engagement value of food disgust-and-fascination content is well-established in social media analytics; food content consistently performs well across platforms, and content that combines familiar comfort products (Oreos) with culturally loaded materials (Spam) generates both the cognitive surprise (unexpected combination) and the cultural resonance (Hawaii-specific Spam significance) that drive sharing. The fabricated product image is, from a content engineering standpoint, a skillfully designed viral artifact.

This does not make the deception benign from a consumer information standpoint—some audiences did apparently process the image as a genuine product announcement—but it reframes the fabrication from a category of harmful misinformation to a category of commercial content marketing that happens to generate false beliefs as a side effect. The policy implications of this distinction are significant: combating deliberate hoaxes motivated by malice requires different interventions than combating deliberate viral content motivated by audience growth.

**Epistemic Novelty — The Self-Disclosing Hoax and Its Residual Virality**: The Spam Oreos case presents a distinctive epistemic structure in the viral misinformation literature: the hoax whose creator publicly discloses the fabrication while the viral content continues to spread. Boss Hawaii's Facebook admission that the image was a created artifact was part of the content ecosystem surrounding the image—it existed as a findable disclosure—yet the image continued to circulate and continued to attract credulous responses even after the disclosure was available. This pattern raises fundamental questions about the relationship between available corrective information and actual belief distribution in viral content ecosystems.

Research on misinformation persistence after correction (the "continued influence effect") demonstrates that belief in false information continues to influence reasoning even after explicit correction in a substantial proportion of audience members (Lewandowsky et al., 2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*). The Spam Oreos case extends this finding into the domain of self-disclosing fabrications: even when the creator explicitly states "I made this up," the image's continued circulation in contexts where the disclosure is not co-presented means that most viewers of the image never encounter the disclosure at all. The viral content and its correction inhabit separate content streams, with the image-only stream commanding vastly greater distribution than the image-plus-disclosure stream. This structural feature of content ecosystem architecture means that creator disclosure, while epistemically significant, does not function as an effective correction mechanism at population scale.

**The Meat Oreo Genre as Recurring Template**: The recognition that Spam Oreos was part of a documented series of meat-flavor Oreo fakes (Bacon Cream in March 2015, Fried Chicken, Cheeseburger in May 2015, Spam in May 2015) points to a recurring content production pattern. Meat-flavored versions of iconic sweet products constitute a reliable formula for food disgust-fascination content that activates sharing behavior across a wide audience range. The formula works because: (a) iconic sweet products have strong brand associations that make transgressive combinations emotionally vivid, (b) meat is a culturally loaded flavor category that generates reliable disgust responses when applied to sweet-product contexts, and (c) Nabisco's documented history of unusual flavors provides the specific credibility substrate for this particular product being the template. The identification of Spam Oreos as part of a recurring genre rather than an isolated incident is itself useful information for audiences, because it suggests a framework within which future similar images can be pre-evaluated as probable fakes before fact-check intervention.


## 4. Verdict

**Rating: FALSE**

Nabisco has not produced Spam-flavored Oreo cookies. The image purporting to show such a product was fabricated by Boss Hawaii web design studio as a deliberate social media traffic generation tool for the Hawaii Food Porn Facebook page. The creator openly acknowledged the fabrication, identifying the image's viral performance (500 new Facebook likes in two days) as a measure of the strategy's success. No Spam-flavored Oreo has been announced, produced, or distributed by Nabisco as of any verified reporting date.

**Gap Category: Deliberate Fake Image for Traffic**

This case belongs to a category of social media content fabricated primarily for audience growth purposes rather than for malicious deception, but which generates false product consumption beliefs as a predictable side effect. The category is distinct from accidental misinformation and from maliciously deceptive hoaxes; it requires analysis of the commercial incentive structures driving content fabrication rather than the pathological rumor dynamics that characterize accidental misinformation spread.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The broader cultural significance of Spam as an object of culinary mythology—particularly in Hawaii—deserves attention as context for why the Spam Oreos image worked as viral content in ways that, say, a Vienna Sausage Oreos image might not have.

Spam was introduced as a canned precooked pork product by Hormel Foods in 1937 and achieved its peculiar cultural footprint in Hawaii largely through World War II military supply dynamics: US military bases in Hawaii relied heavily on Spam as a non-perishable protein source during and after the war, and the product became deeply integrated into Hawaiian plate lunch culture and local food identity in ways that have no parallel in continental US food culture (Hormel Foods Corporation, corporate history). Hawaii consumes more Spam per capita than any other US state by a substantial margin; Spam musubi—a piece of Spam on a block of rice wrapped with nori, an adaptation of Japanese onigiri formats—is a ubiquitous Hawaiian convenience food. The Boss Hawaii studio's creation of a Spam Oreos image was not generically gross; it was culturally legible within a specific local food identity framework where Spam-flavored everything is a recognizable comedic and culinary register.

The Oreo brand's genuinely accelerating flavor innovation in the mid-2010s represents an important industrial trend that provided the credibility substrate for the entire genre of fake Oreo announcements. Nabisco's flavor expansion strategy was part of a documented corporate decision to use limited-edition and novelty flavors as marketing instruments—products released briefly to generate media coverage, social sharing, and consumer engagement rather than as long-term additions to the product lineup. Research on limited-edition product strategies in confectionery markets found that Oreo's novelty flavor launches generated social media engagement substantially disproportionate to their sales volumes, confirming that their primary function was attention-generation rather than revenue-maximization (Lin et al., 2020, *Journal of Brand Management*). This strategy had the unintended consequence of making the brand's flavor space epistemically unnavigable to consumers: if Watermelon Oreos and Red Velvet Cake Oreos are real, what reliable heuristic distinguishes them from Fried Chicken Oreos and Spam Oreos, which are fakes?

The digital media ecosystem that enables fake product images to go viral has developed much faster than the brand management and consumer protection frameworks designed to respond to them. Procter & Gamble, Nestlé, and Nabisco/Mondelez International all faced recurring fake product announcement circulations in the mid-2010s, a period when social media sharing was scaling rapidly but platform-level content verification mechanisms were essentially nonexistent. The corporate responses available—press releases, social media corrections, contacting Snopes—all operate on timescales too slow to intercept the primary viral spread of an engaging image. A fabricated product package image that achieves thousands of shares in the first forty-eight hours will not be effectively corrected by a brand statement posted twenty-four hours later, by which point the image-only content has already been shared into contexts where the correction will never reach its audience.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes's fact-check by Dan Evon, published May 29, 2015, is notable for two things: its appropriate "False" rating and its clear identification of both the confounding credibility context (real unusual Oreo flavors) and the fabrication attribution (Boss Hawaii/Hawaii Food Porn). The Boss Hawaii Facebook quote admitting to the fabrication is reproduced directly, which is the most effective corrective element available—letting the creator's own words establish the fabrication.

The fact-check also appropriately contextualizes the Spam Oreos case within the series of meat-flavor Oreo fakes from the same period, noting that bacon cream, fried chicken, and cheeseburger Oreo images had all circulated as fakes in the preceding months. This contextualization provides audiences with the genre template needed to evaluate future similar images skeptically, which is the most durable contribution a fact-check in this category can make.

One analytical gap worth noting: the Snopes treatment does not examine why deliberate fabrications like this are structurally difficult to suppress despite creator disclosure, or what the distinction between "traffic-generation content tool" and "malicious hoax" implies for how platforms and consumers should process such fabrications. The "False" rating is appropriate and sufficient for the immediate truth value question, but the deeper mechanics of why the image worked—the credibility substrate created by real Oreo flavors, the Spam cultural legibility in Hawaiian food identity, the deliberate engineering of disgust-fascination engagement—are left unaddressed. Richer treatment of the fabricator's disclosed methodology would enable audiences to calibrate their priors for the entire class of fake product announcements rather than simply updating on this specific case.
