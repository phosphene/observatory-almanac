## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In April 2018, reports circulated online—and in many major news outlets—that a sperm whale had died after swallowing 64 pounds of plastic debris off the coast of Spain. The reports were accompanied by dramatic images of what appeared to be a beached whale with its decomposing innards visible, filled with plastic waste. The story generated widespread outrage about ocean plastic pollution and was shared hundreds of thousands of times across social media platforms.

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2018) assigned this claim the rating **Miscaptioned**. The underlying factual core is entirely accurate: authorities in the Murcia region of southern Spain did recover and autopsy a dead sperm whale in early 2018, and the postmortem examination did reveal more than 64 pounds (29 kilograms) of debris in its stomach and intestines, including plastic bags, pieces of fishing net, a plastic water container, and rope. The whale—a 33-foot-long male—died from peritonitis caused by the rupture of its digestive system, which had become so impacted with indigestible plastic material that the normal digestive process was fatally disrupted.

What was false was the image most commonly used to illustrate this story. The striking photograph shared by many outlets—including Forbes—did not show the Murcia whale or any real dead whale. It was an art installation created by Greenpeace Philippines in May 2017, displayed at the Sea Side Beach Resort in Naic, Cavite, Philippines. The installation was a deliberately constructed replica of a deceased whale made entirely from plastic debris collected from the ocean, created specifically to draw attention to the problem of marine plastic pollution. It was staged, photographed, and released for advocacy purposes—not as documentation of a real death event.

## §2 The Murcia, Spain Sperm Whale: What Actually Happened

The confirmed event at the core of this claim represents one of the clearest documented cases of marine mammal death attributable to plastic ingestion in the scientific and regulatory record. The animal, a male sperm whale (*Physeter macrocephalus*), was found dead on a beach at Cabo de Palos, near Murcia, on 27 February 2018. The whale was unusually thin, and its condition was described by experts at the El Valle Wildlife Recovery Center in Murcia as consistent with an animal whose digestive system had been functionally obstructed for an extended period.

The autopsy results, released on or around 5–11 April 2018, identified more than 29 kilograms (approximately 64 pounds) of debris in the animal's stomach and intestines. The itemized list of recovered material included plastic bags, pieces of fishing net, a plastic water container, rope, and other assorted plastic materials (Gabbatiss, 2018, *The Independent*; Ruiz, 2018, *La Verdad*). Veterinary pathologists from El Valle determined the official cause of death to be peritonitis—a potentially fatal infection of the peritoneal lining of the abdominal cavity—caused by perforation or rupture of the digestive system resulting from the impaction of undigestible material.

Sperm whales (*Physeter macrocephalus*) are deep-diving cetaceans and the world's largest toothed predators. Their primary prey is squid, including giant and colossal squid in deep ocean environments. They routinely dive to 1,000 meters and have been documented reaching depths of over 2,000 meters in search of prey (Watwood et al., 2006, *Journal of Animal Ecology*). The whale's foraging habitat is precisely the deep ocean where floating plastic debris—particularly flexible, neutrally-buoyant items like plastic bags—can be mistaken for prey or inadvertently ingested alongside prey. The whale's stomach did not contain any natural food, suggesting the animal had not been feeding normally for a significant period.

The revelation was not entirely unprecedented. In 2016, researchers conducting necropsies on thirteen sperm whales that stranded along the coasts of Germany, the Netherlands, France, and the United Kingdom found that multiple individuals had plastic debris in their stomachs (Kammann et al., 2017, *Marine Pollution Bulletin*). Material recovered included fishing nets, non-food plastic items, automotive parts, and a 13-meter-long piece of fishing line. The European strandings of 2016 received substantial coverage but were eventually attributed primarily to cardiovascular failure (the animals had become disoriented in the shallow southern North Sea), with plastic ingestion as a contributing stressor rather than the direct cause of death.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Greenpeace Philippines Art Installation and Strategic Misinformation Asymmetry

The most epistemically interesting dimension of this case is the role of strategic visual advocacy in producing a specific form of well-intentioned but technically misleading viral content. The Greenpeace Philippines installation at Naic, Cavite was not an attempt to deceive—it was intended as a transparent artistic statement. Created in May 2017, the installation was explicitly announced as an art project, documented extensively in Greenpeace Philippines communications, and accompanied by explanatory text about the advocacy goal.

The mechanism by which an explicit advocacy art installation became a factual illustration of a different, subsequent event illustrates what might be called *advocacy image displacement*: visually compelling material created to represent a category of harm (marine plastic death) becomes attached in online sharing chains to a specific, different instance of that harm—where it happens to be more visually dramatic than the actual documentation of the specific event.

This phenomenon has an important asymmetry. The actual Spanish authorities did release photographs of the Murcia whale (Evon, 2018)—images showing the thin, real carcass and the itemized plastic debris recovered from its stomach. These real images were available. They were less visually striking than the Greenpeace installation: a real dead whale is a decomposing gray-brown mass; the Greenpeace replica was designed to be maximally compelling as a visual argument, with plastic debris dramatically visible at the surface. Media outlets seeking a compelling illustration thus selected the more aesthetically dramatic image, even though that image was not documenting the specific event being described.

The broader pattern is documented in misinformation literature under the concept of "truth-adjacent misinformation"—claims that are technically false in a narrow sense but directionally accurate in a normatively significant sense. Berinsky (2017, *Political Psychology*) discusses how correcting truth-adjacent false claims can backfire by inadvertently undermining accurate background beliefs. In this case, the underlying claim (whales are dying because of plastic pollution) is robustly supported; the specific subclaim (this photograph shows the whale that died) is false. Correcting the photograph attachment without disrupting the accurate broader message requires a fine-grained correction that many retractions do not achieve.

The epistemic novelty here is structural: the visual evidence substitution occurred not because anyone was acting deceptively, but because the information ecosystem created an incentive gradient that rewarded visual impact over precise documentation. Greenpeace Philippines designed the installation to achieve maximum visual impact specifically because bland factual documentation rarely achieves behavioral change. The installation succeeded in its advocacy design goal—it was, by design, more compelling than real documentation—but this same design success made it disproportionately likely to be misappropriated as visual evidence for real events it did not depict.

## §4 Marine Plastic Pollution: The Scientific Record

The Murcia whale case did not occur in a vacuum. The scientific literature on plastic ingestion by marine megafauna—large marine vertebrates including cetaceans, sea turtles, and seabirds—had been accumulating for decades before the 2018 incident, providing clear context for why the story was both plausible and deeply concerning.

Laist (1997, in *Marine Debris: Sources, Impacts, and Solutions*, Marine Pollution Bulletin) documented over 260 species of marine organisms known to have ingested or become entangled in marine debris, including 26 of 28 cetacean species then known. Subsequent decades have seen this scope confirmed and extended. Gall and Thompson (2015, *Anthropocene*) conducted a meta-analysis of 340 original research reports and found that documented impacts of plastic ingestion spanned 693 marine species. Cetaceans appear in this literature with particular frequency in the case study literature, partly because stranded whales and dolphins are accessible for necropsies in a way that live deep-ocean animals are not.

The quantitative dimensions of ocean plastic pollution provide necessary context. By the late 2010s, the best available estimates placed cumulative ocean plastic loading at approximately 150 million metric tons, with an annual input rate of approximately 8 million metric tons (Jambeck et al., 2015, *Science*). The World Economic Forum's 2016 report on the new plastics economy projected that, on current trajectories, the mass of plastic in the ocean could exceed the mass of fish by 2050 (Ellen MacArthur Foundation & McKinsey Center for Business and Environment, 2016). These orders-of-magnitude are meaningful for interpreting the whale stranding data: with 150 million metric tons of plastic dispersed through the world's oceans, encounters between deep-diving marine mammals and persistent plastic material are not exceptional events but predictable consequences of the scale of contamination.

Sperm whales in particular inhabit productive deep-water environments where plastic debris tends to accumulate in convergence zones. Their foraging dives take them into layers of the water column where neutrally-buoyant plastic items concentrate at density interfaces. Thompson et al. (2004, *Science*) provided early evidence that microplastic particles—fragments smaller than 5mm—had already become pervasive through marine sediments and water columns globally, suggesting that even beyond macroscopic debris, the digestive systems of deep-diving predators are subject to contamination at scales not visible in necropsies focused on larger items.

## §5 Art Installations as Evidence: Advocacy Photography and Media Epistemics

The Greenpeace Philippines installation at Naic belongs to a recognized tradition of environmental art designed not merely to convey information but to produce emotional response sufficient to motivate behavioral or political change. This tradition includes works such as Chris Jordan's photographic studies of seabird carcasses with plastic stomach contents (the "Midway" project, 2009–2017), the "Giant Whale of Plastic" installation in Bilbao, and various large-scale sculpture projects using ocean-recovered debris.

The epistemic challenge posed by advocacy art is distinct from the challenge posed by deliberate misinformation. Advocacy art operates explicitly under a license to exaggerate, dramatize, and aestheticize in service of what its creators regard as an accurate and important message. Viewers encountering the Greenpeace Philippines installation in its original exhibition context understood they were viewing an art object, not a documentary photograph. The installation's construction from real ocean-recovered plastic was part of its claim to authenticity as advocacy—not a claim to factual accuracy as documentation.

When such images are stripped of their curatorial context and inserted into news coverage of specific real events, they enter an epistemically different register. Viewers of news articles expect accompanying images to document what the article describes. The psychological contract of news photography (Zelizer, 2010, *About to Die: How News Images Move the Public*, Oxford University Press) is that the image shows the event—or an event directly comparable to it. An art installation, however powerful, violates this contract when used as news illustration without clear labeling.

Harcup and O'Neill (2016, *Journalism Studies*) updated their landmark taxonomy of news values to include shareability and emotional impact as increasingly central criteria in digital-era editorial selection. By those criteria, the Greenpeace installation image dominated the actual Murcia whale photographs on every relevant dimension: it was larger, more visually dramatic, and explicitly designed to maximize emotional reaction to plastic pollution. In a digital media environment where visual impact is a primary selectable criterion, mislabeling of advocacy imagery as documentary is a predictable rather than exceptional outcome.

## §6 Contemporary Relevance and Knowledge Gaps

The EARC gap assigned here is **C (Causal)**: the factual elements of the claim (that the whale died, that it contained plastic, that the amount was approximately 64 pounds) are well-established. What remains uncertain—and where the causal attribution is incomplete—is the precise mechanism by which the plastic accumulation led to peritonitis. Specifically, the scientific literature on exactly how impacted plastic causes digestive perforation in large cetaceans is not extensive. The El Valle veterinary team attributed death to peritonitis from digestive system rupture (Gabbatiss, 2018), but the pathophysiology of plastic-induced peritonitis in sperm whales is not a well-characterized clinical entity; it is inferred from anatomical findings at necropsy rather than from a controlled understanding of the progressive failure sequence.

Additional knowledge gaps: What fraction of sperm whale mortality globally is attributable to plastic ingestion mortality versus entanglement mortality versus other stressors? How does plastic ingestion interact with other anthropogenic stressors (acoustic pollution from shipping and sonar, prey depletion from industrial fishing, climate-driven distributional shifts) in determining sperm whale fitness and survival? What threshold of ingested plastic trigger peritonitis, and is there individual variation?

The practical implication for media consumers: when a dramatic photograph accompanies a report about a specific environmental event, the default assumption should not be that the photograph documents that event. Given the production ecology of environmental advocacy imagery—imagery intentionally designed for maximum impact regardless of specific event documentation—cross-referencing the image source is due diligence, not pedantry. In the present case, the underlying story is entirely real; the photograph is the error.
