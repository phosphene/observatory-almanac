## The Claim

Eating spicy food causes stomach ulcers. The advice follows naturally: if you want to avoid getting an ulcer, avoid hot peppers, chili, curry, and other pungent foods. If you already have an ulcer, cutting out spice is non-negotiable. Generations of gastroenterologists warned patients about their diets. Generations of mothers told their children to slow down on the salsa. The claim felt so obvious that it barely needed defending — eat irritating food, irritate your stomach lining, get an ulcer.

This belief is one of the most durable pieces of medical folk wisdom in the world. It crossed cultural boundaries, survived decades of clinical medicine, and persisted long after the actual cause of ulcers had been identified. Understanding why it was wrong — and why it was also, in a limited sense, pointing at something real — requires tracing one of the stranger episodes in twentieth-century medical history.

---

## What's Actually True

**The actual cause of peptic ulcers is a bacterium called *Helicobacter pylori*.** Roughly 70 to 80 percent of gastric ulcers and more than 90 percent of duodenal ulcers are caused by *H. pylori* infection, making it the dominant etiological factor in ulcer disease worldwide. The second major cause is chronic use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) — aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen — which inhibit cyclooxygenase enzymes that normally protect the stomach lining by stimulating mucus production. Spicy food is not a significant independent cause of ulcers in either category.

This was not the consensus view for most of the twentieth century. The prevailing theory was that ulcers resulted from an imbalance between acid production and mucosal defense — too much acid, not enough protective mucus, leading to the stomach or duodenal lining effectively digesting itself. Stress, diet, and alcohol were all implicated as triggers of this acid-mucosal imbalance. The logic was internally coherent. Acid clearly played a role in ulcer pathology — antacids did provide symptomatic relief — and it was natural to assume that anything that increased gastric acid secretion was a risk factor. Spicy food, especially capsaicin, does stimulate gastric acid secretion. The inference seemed inevitable.

**The bacterial discovery came from Barry Marshall and Robin Warren in Perth, Australia.** Warren, a pathologist, had been observing curved bacteria in gastric biopsies since 1979, always associated with active gastritis. Marshall, a clinical gastroenterology registrar, became convinced the bacteria were causally relevant. In 1982, Marshall and Warren succeeded in culturing the organism — which would eventually be named *Helicobacter pylori* — after an accidental five-day incubation period over the Easter holiday. They published their initial findings in 1984 in *The Lancet*, and Marshall famously performed a self-experiment: he drank a broth containing cultured *H. pylori* and developed acute gastritis within days.

The medical establishment was deeply skeptical. The stomach was thought to be too acidic for bacterial colonization. The stress-acid theory of ulcers was entrenched. Pharmaceutical companies had enormous financial interests in antisecretory drugs like cimetidine and ranitidine. It took most of a decade before controlled trials confirmed that antibiotic eradication of *H. pylori* cured ulcers at dramatically higher rates than antacid therapy alone, and prevented recurrence — which antacids could not do. By the early 1990s, the evidence had accumulated to the point where the National Institutes of Health issued a consensus statement endorsing antibiotic therapy for ulcers. Marshall and Warren received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2005 "for their discovery of the bacterium *Helicobacter pylori* and its role in gastritis and peptic ulcer disease."

**Why does spicy food seem to matter at all?** The answer lies in the distinction between causing an ulcer and aggravating one that already exists. Research by Kumar and colleagues (1984) demonstrated that spicy food does increase gastric acid secretion in susceptible individuals. More importantly, capsaicin — the active compound in chili peppers — activates TRPV1 (transient receptor potential vanilloid 1) receptors throughout the gastrointestinal tract. Research by Yeomans and colleagues (2005) showed that TRPV1 stimulation affects gastric motility, acid secretion, and mucosal blood flow in ways that can aggravate existing gastric lesions. In patients who already have ulcers (most of which are caused by *H. pylori*), eating very spicy food can genuinely increase pain, disrupt the mucosal healing process, and worsen symptoms. The observation that spicy food causes gastric distress was phenomenologically real. The causal inference — that the food was creating the underlying pathology rather than aggravating an existing bacterial lesion — was the error.

There is also some evidence, counterintuitively, that capsaicin in moderate amounts may be gastroprotective. Studies published in the 1990s found that capsaicin stimulates gastric mucus production and blood flow in ways that actually help maintain the mucosal barrier. The relationship between capsaicin and gastric health is not simple damage — it is dose-dependent, context-dependent, and entangled with whether a person has active *H. pylori* infection, NSAID use, or other ulcer risk factors.

## Why People Believe This

The persistence of the spicy-food-causes-ulcers belief rests on a cluster of interlocking mechanisms, each of which is individually understandable.

**The symptom-observation trap.** Ulcer patients who ate spicy food reported worsened pain. This was a real, reproducible phenomenon — not imagined. Clinicians who observed the pattern had no reason to distinguish between "this food is making the symptoms worse" and "this food is causing the underlying disease." The distinction between exacerbation and etiology is conceptually simple but epistemically invisible when you only have access to symptom data. The gastroenterologist who advised ulcer patients to avoid spicy food was giving advice that was genuinely useful for symptom management, even though the causal theory behind it was wrong.

**The acid logic.** Gastric acid was known to be central to ulcer pathology since the nineteenth century. The phrase "no acid, no ulcer," coined by Karl Schwarz in 1910, organized ulcer medicine for most of the twentieth century. Any food that stimulated acid production was a plausible cause. Spicy food did stimulate acid production. The syllogism was tidy, and its conclusion did not seem to require further investigation.

**Sensory salience.** Capsaicin produces burning, heat, and pain through mechanisms that are directly perceptible. Eating a very hot pepper and then experiencing gastric discomfort hours later creates a vivid, memorable causal narrative. *H. pylori* infection is invisible, asymptomatic for years, and mediated by a mechanism that requires microscopy and culture to detect. The bacterium was an invisible cause; the spice was a visible one. Human cognition strongly favors visible, temporally proximate causes over invisible, temporally distant ones.

**Cultural reinforcement.** Dietary advice against spicy food for ulcer patients appeared in medical textbooks, popular health literature, and clinical guidelines for most of the twentieth century. Once it was established as authoritative advice, it became self-reinforcing. Patients who avoided spice and whose ulcers eventually healed naturally attributed the recovery, at least partly, to the dietary change. The false positive — getting better while avoiding spice — cemented the belief even in the absence of a true causal mechanism.

**Institutional resistance to bacterial theory.** The story of Marshall and Warren is partly a story about how entrenched beliefs resist new evidence even when the evidence is substantial. The stress-acid model of ulcer disease was embedded in medical education, clinical practice, pharmaceutical development, and patient communication. A bacterial explanation required not just accepting new data but abandoning an entire explanatory framework that had successfully organized clinical practice for decades. The resistance was not purely self-interested — many skeptics were genuinely applying reasonable scientific standards — but it did mean that the dietary advice persisted in clinical medicine long after the bacterial evidence began accumulating.

## Verdict

**The claim that spicy food causes stomach ulcers is false as a causal statement.** *Helicobacter pylori* infection and NSAID use are the dominant causes of peptic ulcer disease. Spicy food does not cause ulcers in people who do not have *H. pylori* infection or NSAID-related mucosal damage.

**The observation that spicy food worsens gastric symptoms is true** in patients with active ulcers, particularly because capsaicin's effects on gastric acid secretion, TRPV1 receptor activation, and mucosal irritation can aggravate existing lesions. This makes dietary management of spice relevant for symptom control in ulcer patients, even though it is not a treatment for the underlying disease.

The belief belongs to a class of medical folk errors in which a genuine symptom correlation was mistaken for a causal mechanism. The direction of the observation was correct; the explanation was wrong. This is not the same as saying the advice to avoid spicy food during an ulcer flare is useless — it may genuinely help with symptom management — but it is categorically different from saying spicy food causes the ulcers in the first place.

**EARC Mode: A — Accurate core observation, wrong causal mechanism.** The phenomenological observations that drove the belief (spicy food worsens gastric symptoms in ulcer patients) were real. The causal inference (spice creates ulcers) was incorrect. The belief was directionally grounded in experience but mechanistically wrong.

## The Wider Picture

The Marshall and Warren story has become a paradigm case in philosophy of science for how anomalous evidence eventually overturns entrenched paradigms. Thomas Kuhn's framework of scientific revolutions — where anomalies accumulate until a paradigm shift becomes unavoidable — maps onto the *H. pylori* episode with unusual clarity. The bacterial theory was anomalous not just in its content but in its methodology: Marshall was a clinician, not a microbiologist; Warren was a pathologist, not a gastroenterologist. The discovery came from two people working outside the discipline's core institutional structure, which contributed to the initial dismissal of their findings.

The episode also illustrates how financial interests can slow scientific revision without necessarily corrupting it outright. Antisecretory drugs — H2 blockers and proton pump inhibitors — were among the most profitable pharmaceutical products of the 1980s and 1990s. Companies that had invested in these drugs had no immediate incentive to promote antibiotic therapy, which was cheap and curative. This did not prevent the eventual acceptance of the bacterial theory, but it contributed to the lag between the evidence and the guidelines.

**The capsaicin biology is itself genuinely interesting.** TRPV1 receptors are part of a broader signaling system involved in pain, inflammation, and mucosal defense throughout the gastrointestinal tract. Research on capsaicin as a therapeutic agent — exploiting its ability to deplete substance P in sensory neurons — has led to topical pain treatments for conditions ranging from arthritis to postherpetic neuralgia. The gastric effects of capsaicin are not simply damage mechanisms; they are part of a complex regulatory system. This complexity is part of why the story of spice and ulcers is more interesting than the simple "it was wrong" verdict suggests.

**Geographic patterns add another layer.** Countries with very high consumption of spicy food — Mexico, India, parts of Southeast Asia — do not have dramatically higher rates of peptic ulcer disease than less spice-consuming populations. This epidemiological observation was available before the bacterial theory was confirmed, and it is one of the reasons some researchers were skeptical of the dietary hypothesis even before *H. pylori* was identified. However, *H. pylori* prevalence is also high in many of these populations, which complicated the analysis. The epidemiology was messy enough that dietary factors could not be cleanly excluded on geographical grounds alone.

**NSAIDs as the second major cause** represent a separate lesson about medication use. The widespread use of aspirin and ibuprofen for pain, fever, and cardiovascular protection creates a substantial population-level burden of NSAID-induced gastric damage. Proton pump inhibitors co-prescribed with NSAIDs reduce this risk but do not eliminate it. In an era of increasing NSAID use, particularly aspirin for cardiovascular prevention, NSAID-related ulcers have become proportionally more significant as *H. pylori* rates decline in developed countries due to improved sanitation and targeted antibiotic treatment.

The full picture of peptic ulcer disease, then, is: bacterial infection as the primary cause, NSAID use as the second, and dietary factors including spice as modifiers of symptoms and possibly of mucosal resilience, but not as independent causes. The clinical advice to avoid very spicy food during an ulcer flare is not wrong — it is sensible symptom management. The mistake was in treating that advice as an etiological claim rather than a palliative one.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The spicy-food-ulcer claim presents a particular challenge for standard fact-checking because the categorical verdict ("spicy food causes ulcers — False") is accurate but potentially misleading in ways that simple binary verdicts tend to obscure.

**The categorical response is: False.** Spicy food does not cause peptic ulcers. *H. pylori* and NSAIDs do. This is established medical consensus, supported by decades of controlled trials, Nobel Prize-winning research, and NIH guidelines. Fact-checkers who rate this claim False are correct.

**The complicating factor is the symptom reality.** A patient with an active ulcer who eats a very spicy meal will probably experience worsened pain. That is not false. Telling such a patient "don't worry about spicy food — it's a myth" is not helpful advice, even if it is technically aligned with the debunking literature. The distinction between "causes ulcers" and "worsens ulcer symptoms" is clinically important and fact-checkers ideally need to make it explicit.

**The better fact-check structure acknowledges three things:** (1) Spicy food does not cause ulcers; (2) *H. pylori* and NSAIDs do; (3) Spicy food can genuinely worsen symptoms in people who already have ulcers, so dietary management remains relevant for symptom control even though it is not treatment for the underlying disease.

Fact-checkers working with medical content generally benefit from consulting primary sources — in this case, the Marshall and Warren 1984 *Lancet* paper, the NIH 1994 consensus statement on *H. pylori* eradication therapy, and subsequent systematic reviews on NSAID gastropathy — rather than secondary sources that may not make the exacerbation/etiology distinction clearly.

**The broader lesson for fact-checkers** is about the category of beliefs that are directionally grounded in real phenomena but wrong about mechanism. These claims are harder to assess than pure fabrications because the evidence supporting them is real — just misinterpreted. The appropriate response is not "this is completely false" but "the observation is real, the explanation is wrong, and the practical implications depend on which one you're acting on." That is a more complex verdict, but it is a more accurate one.

The spicy-food-ulcer case also illustrates the importance of time-stamping claims in health fact-checking. Medical consensus on the causes of peptic ulcer disease changed decisively in the 1990s following the acceptance of the bacterial theory. Claims that were accurate medical advice before 1994 became misleading after it. Fact-checkers dealing with historical health advice should check whether their reference period predates a significant paradigm shift in the relevant field, and update accordingly. In this case: any source advising avoidance of spicy food as a primary ulcer-prevention strategy without mentioning *H. pylori* eradication therapy is either outdated or incomplete, regardless of when it was written.
