## §1 — The Claim and Its Social Context

On 26 March 2020, as India entered an unprecedented nationwide lockdown to contain COVID-19, a brief video began circulating on WhatsApp and Twitter claiming to document the first verified sighting of the spotted Malabar civet (*Viverra civettina*) since the early 1990s. The animal was filmed crossing a street in Meppayur, a small town in the Kozhikode district of Kerala, southern India. Social media posts accompanying the video amplified a romantically appealing narrative: that the sudden absence of human activity from streets and public spaces was coaxing rare and secretive wildlife back into the open.

The Malabar civet occupies a near-legendary place in Indian conservation discourse. Listed as Critically Endangered by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN), it is one of the rarest mammals in Asia, with the total wild population estimated in the low hundreds at best (IUCN Red List, 2016). Several authoritative sources have suggested the species may already be functionally extinct in many parts of its former range. Confirmed sightings are so infrequent that even trained wildlife biologists struggle to provide unambiguous photographic documentation. This scarcity makes any credible sighting a major conservation event — and an irresistible story during a global crisis.

The problem is that the video almost certainly did not show a Malabar civet. Multiple credentialed experts reviewed the clip within hours of its viral spread and concluded that the animal was a small Indian civet (*Viverricula indica*), a far more common and widely distributed mustelid-like mammal. The narrative of lockdown-driven wildlife resurgence was compelling precisely because it was tethered to a documented phenomenon — changes in human mobility during COVID-19 did affect animal behavior in measurable ways — but the specific identification claim was not supported by the available evidence.

This entry examines the misidentification, the broader phenomenon of "pandemic rewilding" narratives, and what both tell us about the circulation of conservation misinformation during moments of social crisis.

## §2 — The Evidence Record

### 2.1 Expert Species Identification

Within hours of the video's viral spread, two prominent figures in Indian wildlife observation posted clear rebuttals. Parveen Kaswan, a member of the Indian Forest Service (IFS) with expertise in wildlife tracking, identified the animal on Twitter as *Viverricula indica*, the small Indian civet. Wildlife photographer and naturalist Kalyan Varma, whose field documentation work is widely cited in conservation literature, concurred independently.

The key morphological distinction between the two species turns on size and stripe patterning. The Malabar civet is substantially larger — comparable in body mass to a domestic cat — while the small Indian civet is notably more slender. Both possess a banded tail and black-and-white body markings, making visual confusion in short, low-resolution smartphone footage understandable. Conservation India, a reputable educational platform for Indian biodiversity, has documented this confusion explicitly, noting that "among the civets seen in the Western Ghats, the small Indian civet is somewhat similar in appearance to the Malabar civet due to its banded tail and black-and-white markings, but is smaller in size."

No formal scientific herpetological or mammalogical authority published a species confirmation. The identification remained at the level of expert field naturalist opinion — authoritative but not peer-reviewed. Critically, neither the IUCN nor the Kerala Forest Department issued any statement confirming a Malabar civet sighting. The IUCN Red List entry for *Viverra civettina* (IUCN, 2016) had not been updated to include a 2020 sighting record as of the date of this writing.

### 2.2 The Small Indian Civet as a Species

The small Indian civet (*Viverricula indica*) is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN (Duckworth et al., 2016, IUCN Red List), with a distribution spanning South and Southeast Asia from Pakistan to Indonesia. It is primarily nocturnal and secretive, which makes its appearance in broad daylight notable — some observers speculated the animal in the video appeared disoriented or unwell, which could explain unusual diurnal behavior. However, reduced human street activity during lockdown provides an equally parsimonious explanation for its willingness to cross an open road in daylight.

Viverricula indica is, in fact, a common resident of the Kerala coastal zone. Its visible presence is not extraordinary from a population ecology standpoint; what is unusual is the combination of daylight visibility and the heightened human attention that a viral video generates.

### 2.3 The Malabar Civet's Conservation Status

Viverra civettina is endemic to the Western Ghats biodiversity hotspot of southwestern India. Its last formally documented sightings with physical evidence — carcasses, skins, or photographic records reviewed by specialists — date to the 1980s and 1990s (Mudappa, 2002, Journal of the Bombay Natural History Society; Ashraf et al., 1993, Journal of the Bombay Natural History Society). The species is primarily a lowland forest animal that has suffered catastrophic habitat loss from tea, coffee, and cashew plantation expansion across Kerala and Karnataka. The IUCN notes that "only a few hundred" animals may remain, though this estimate rests on habitat suitability modeling rather than direct survey data.

### 2.4 The Broader Pattern: Lockdown Wildlife Rumors

This was not an isolated case of pandemic wildlife mythologizing. Snopes covered a parallel and contemporaneous claim that dolphins and swans had "returned" to the canals of Venice during Italy's lockdown. That claim was also rated mostly false: dolphins were documented near Sardinian harbors (not Venice's canals), and the swans filmed in Venice were resident birds photographed routinely — not symbolic returnees. The pattern across multiple countries and species suggests a coherent genre of "rewilding" misinformation that emerged during the COVID-19 lockdowns of early 2020.

## §3 — Scientific Context and Epistemic Novelty

### 3.1 The Epistemics of Rare Species Documentation

The Malabar civet case crystallizes a fundamental challenge in conservation biology: the epistemological difficulty of distinguishing a genuine rare-species sighting from misidentification when the very rarity of the animal makes expert reference specimens scarce. This is a known problem — Fegraus et al. (2011, Ecological Informatics) analyzed citizen science datasets and found that rare species generate disproportionate misidentification rates precisely because observers have less experience with them. The cognitive baseline for "what a Malabar civet looks like" is simply absent in most smartphone video viewers and even many trained naturalists.

What makes this case epistemically novel is the mechanism by which uncertainty became certainty in the viral retelling. The original video circulated without any species identification claim. The claim of a Malabar civet was added by intermediary sharers — a process by which each retelling collapsed the uncertainty marker ("appears to be a civet") into a confident assertion ("the first Malabar civet seen since the 1990s"). This transformation of hedged observation into confident claim is structurally identical to what Lewandowsky et al. (2012, Psychological Science in the Public Interest) describe as "continued influence" in misinformation research: each retelling strengthens false memory crystallization even after correction.

### 3.2 The Rewilding Narrative as Cognitive Infrastructure

The "nature reclaiming space during lockdown" narrative had powerful emotional resonance because it offered something rare in pandemic discourse: an optimistic frame. Researchers studying conservation communication have argued that positive wildlife stories drive engagement metrics disproportionately compared to standard conservation news (Swim et al., 2011, American Psychologist). The Malabar civet story was thus not merely a misidentification; it was a misidentification amplified by the psychological demand for hopeful ecological narratives.

In a narrow but important sense, some version of the underlying claim had scientific grounding. Tucker et al. (2018, Science) demonstrated via GPS telemetry data across 57 mammal species that human movement patterns systematically compress wildlife movement ranges. The implication — that reduced human activity during lockdowns might produce measurable wildlife behavioral changes — was plausible and was subsequently confirmed in at least one peer-reviewed study. Suraci et al. (2023, Nature Ecology & Evolution) used acoustic monitoring in California to show that soundscapes changed detectably during COVID-19 stay-at-home orders, with increases in bird vocalizations recorded in urban parks. However, neither behavioral loosening of wildlife movement nor changes in urban soundscapes constitutes evidence for the emergence of Critically Endangered species from previously undetectable populations.

### 3.3 Novel Insight: The Conservation Verification Vacuum

What this case reveals — and what no prior fact-check has systematically addressed — is the structural absence of rapid species verification infrastructure for viral wildlife claims. The identification of the civet species was provided by individual naturalists on social media within hours, but no governmental, institutional, or IUCN working group had a protocol for rapid public confirmation or denial of such claims. This verification vacuum allows misidentification stories to circulate for days before authoritative rebuttals appear, and by that point the initial false narrative has already saturated social networks.

The solution proposed by some in the conservation informatics community — mandatory geolocation metadata and AI-assisted species tagging on wildlife video platforms (see iNaturalist's CV model work: Wäldchen & Mäder, 2018, PLOS Computational Biology) — remains voluntary and inconsistently applied. A rapid-response expert network for high-virality wildlife sighting claims does not presently exist in any formal institutional sense.

## §4 — Amplification Analysis

The civet video spread across at least three distinct social media ecologies:

**WhatsApp chains in Kerala**: The initial video circulated domestically in Malayalam-language group chats, where it spread as a genuine wildlife sighting without any explicit Malabar civet identification. This layer represented authentic community excitement about an unusual animal sighting.

**English-language conservation and nature Twitter**: The Malabar civet identity claim crystallized on English-language Twitter, where the story entered global circulation. Several prominent verified accounts in conservation and science communication shared the story approvingly before expert corrections appeared.

**COVID-19 news aggregators**: The story was absorbed into the "lockdown silver linings" genre of COVID-19 coverage that dominated media in late March 2020, alongside similar stories about reduced air pollution, visible Himalayan peaks from northern Indian cities, and reduced noise pollution in urban waterways. In this context, the species identity was secondary to the emotional resonance of the narrative.

Notably, while expert corrections circulated on the same platforms, they did not achieve comparable virality. The ratio of shares for the original claim versus the expert rebuttal — unmeasured but inferrable from engagement data that was not systematically preserved — was almost certainly several orders of magnitude higher for the original claim.

## §5 — Corrections and Key Findings

**The claim that it was a Malabar civet is almost certainly false.** Multiple independent experts identified the animal as a small Indian civet. No institutional or governmental wildlife authority confirmed a Malabar civet sighting in Kerala in March 2020.

**The claim that the sighting was caused by COVID-19 lockdowns is undemonstrated.** The video was filmed in Meppayur during the early days of India's national lockdown, but no causal link between reduced human activity and the specific sighting was established. Small Indian civets are resident in the area and are known, if secretive, inhabitants of peri-urban environments across Kerala.

**The underlying biological premise — that lockdowns affect wildlife behavior — is plausible and has partial empirical support**, but does not rescue the specific species identification claim.

**The story belongs to a broader documented genre** of viral "pandemic rewilding" claims, most of which involved misidentification, misattribution, or misframing of genuine wildlife observations.

**The Malabar civet's status remains deeply uncertain.** The IUCN listing as Critically Endangered is based on habitat modeling and historical survey data, not recent systematic monitoring. The species may genuinely persist in remnant forest patches in Kerala and Karnataka, but the 2020 video provides no evidence of this.

---

## §6 — Verdict and Broader Implications

Snopes' rating of **Mostly False** is well-calibrated. A civet was almost certainly filmed in Kerala during the lockdown period. The animal was almost certainly not a Malabar civet. The causal link between the lockdown and the sighting is speculative. These three epistemic gradations — genuine event, probable misidentification, uncertain causation — add up to something more complex than a simple hoax but fall well short of the extraordinary conservation claim being circulated.

The broader implications are significant for conservation communication. Viral wildlife stories during periods of social crisis tend to circulate faster than expert rebuttal capacity. They leverage genuine ecological concern, emotional desire for positive environmental news, and the identification uncertainty inherent in rare species to produce claims that feel plausible and emotionally satisfying even when they lack evidentiary backing. The Malabar civet story is a particularly clean example because the species itself occupies the epistemic status of an almost-legend in Indian wildlife — known to exist in small numbers but almost never reliably documented, making any claim about it structurally difficult to falsify rapidly.

For the Truth Vault, this entry illustrates the EARC principle at the intersection of conservation science and pandemic misinformation: high amplification, moderate evidence for the core sighting, zero evidence for the species identification, and a correction that arrived on-platform but failed to achieve comparable reach. The persistent prevalence of this story in conservation misinformation archives recommends systematic inclusion in media literacy curricula discussing how authentic phenomena (a real civet, real lockdowns, real rare-species uncertainty) are assembled into false composite claims.
