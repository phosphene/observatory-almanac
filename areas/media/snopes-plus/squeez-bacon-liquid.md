## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In late January 2021, a post in the Reddit community "Thanks I Hate It" (r/TIHI)—a subreddit dedicated to sharing deeply unsettling or grotesque internet content in a spirit of shared revulsion—brought renewed attention to a product called "Squeez Bacon." The post featured images of what appeared to be a commercial product: a tube resembling a toothpaste or condiment dispenser labeled "Vilhelm Lillefläsk's Squeez Bacon," accompanied by product description text claiming the item was a "fully cooked 100% bacon paste" that could be squeezed from the tube and spread on foods or eaten directly. The promotional materials included an elaborate provenance story involving a post-WWII Swedish inventor, a 12-year shelf life, and a recent importation from Scandinavia to the American market.

Snopes investigator Alex Kasprak (2021) assigned this claim the rating **False**. Squeez Bacon and its alleged inventor Vilhelm Lillefläsk are entirely fictional. The product was created as a fake April Fools' Day joke by ThinkGeek, a geek-culture and gaming-themed online retailer, on April 1, 2009. ThinkGeek had an annual tradition of inventing elaborate fake products for April Fools' Day, beginning in 2001; Squeez Bacon was among the most elaborate and visually convincing of these joke products. Despite ThinkGeek's explicit confirmation that the product was fictional at the time of its creation, the image and documentation continued to circulate on the internet for twelve subsequent years, periodically resurging when a new community discovered the fake product and found the concept either amusing or disturbing.

This entry examines the evidentiary record of Squeez Bacon's creation as a joke product, the broader tradition of April Fools' Day fake product launches by technology and consumer brands, the food science of why extended-shelf-life processed bacon paste is both technically feasible and commercially absent, the epistemic novelty of persistent joke products as a category of internet artifact, and the sociology of the r/TIHI community and online communities built around shared grotesque content.

## §2 The Documentary Evidence: ThinkGeek's April Fools' Tradition and the Squeez Bacon Fabrication

The documentary record for Squeez Bacon's fictional status is clear, publicly accessible, and has been so since the product's creation. ThinkGeek's April Fools' Day tradition began in 2001 as the company's method of engaging its core audience—technophilic consumers who appreciated elaborate parody—while generating organic social media attention. Each year's fake products were presented with complete commercial sincerity: product pages with detailed specifications, pricing, product photographs, invented backstories, and even working "Add to Cart" buttons that, when clicked, revealed the joke.

The Squeez Bacon product page, archived by the Wayback Machine and cited by Kasprak (2021), was among the most fully realized of these inventions. It described "Vilhelm Lillefläsk," a fictional Swedish inventor, developing the product in the years immediately following World War II by precooking bacon and blending it through a special process into a shelf-stable paste. The story attributed the product's extended shelf life (claimed at 12 years) to this special proprietary process and framed its recent American importation as an opportunity for U.S. consumers to experience a Scandinavian food tradition. The narrative was complete with invented Swedish proper nouns: "Lillefläsk" is a plausible-sounding Swedish compound (combining "lilla" meaning little/small and "fläsk" meaning pork or bacon), though it is not an attested personal surname in Swedish records.

A November 2009 article in *The New York Times Magazine*, cited by Kasprak (2021), documented the ThinkGeek April Fools' tradition explicitly, providing a contemporaneous journalistic confirmation that Squeez Bacon was one of the company's fictional inventions: "On April 1 of each year since 2001, ThinkGeek, the online retailer, has devoted its home page to products that don't exist." The same article noted other fake products from the same tradition, including "Surgestix Inhalable Caffeine Stix" and "p-Teq's USB Pregnancy Test Kit." ThinkGeek also produced a comedy video advertising Squeez Bacon that was available on YouTube, further confirming the joke framing.

The April 2, 2009 website Bacon Today documented a screenshot showing what users attempting to purchase the product would have seen upon clicking "Add to Cart": a graphic revealing the April Fools' joke rather than a purchase confirmation page. This "reveal" mechanism was standard in ThinkGeek's April Fools' executions throughout the annual tradition's history.

Despite this clear and well-documented fictional origin, the Squeez Bacon images and description circulated continuously for the subsequent twelve years. The r/TIHI post on January 30, 2021, that prompted the Snopes debunking is merely the most recent in a long series of resurgence events in which communities unfamiliar with the original context encountered the content and were uncertain whether it was real.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Persistent Joke Product as an Internet Long-Haul Artifact

The Squeez Bacon case illustrates a distinctive phenomenon in internet information ecology: the **persistent joke product**—a satirical or fictional product so convincingly realized and so visually compelling that it survives indefinitely beyond its original satirical context, periodically resurfacing in communities that lack the contextual knowledge to identify it as fiction. This phenomenon differs from ordinary misinformation in its relationship to originating intent and temporal dynamics.

Originating intent: Unlike most misinformation, which is either sincerely believed by its creators or deliberately constructed to deceive, the persistent joke product begins with complete transparency about its fictional nature. ThinkGeek explicitly revealed the Squeez Bacon joke on April 1, 2009. The persistence of the image as apparent reality in subsequent years is an unintended consequence of internet archiving, image decontextualization, and community knowledge gaps—not of the creator's deceptive intent.

Temporal dynamics: Standard misinformation typically has a lifecycle: it emerges, spreads, gets debunked, and either continues to spread despite debunking or fades from circulation. The persistent joke product has a different lifecycle: it emerges as transparent fiction, achieves wide recognition within its originating community, and then persists as an artifact in internet image archives and social media caches. Each subsequent discovery by a new community is epistemically equivalent to first contact—the encountering community has no prior knowledge of the artifact and must assess its authenticity from scratch. This creates a repeating debunking cycle that can continue indefinitely.

Sparks (2016, "Figuring Historical Time in Internet Memes," *Media, Culture & Society*, Vol. 38, No. 6, pp. 826–840) described this dynamic in the context of internet memes with fictional or satirical origins that persist in new contexts, arguing that the "figuring of historical time" in internet circulation—the systematic stripping of temporal context from images and text—is a structural feature of how social media platforms handle archival content. Images divorced from their publication timestamps and contextual frames become simultaneously timeless and historically unlocatable.

The food-domain specificity of the Squeez Bacon phenomenon is also analytically interesting. The product occupies a special epistemic niche: it is simultaneously recognizably fictional (bacon paste in a toothpaste tube is viscerally unappealing to most consumers) and technically plausible (processed meat products in various unconventional forms do exist). This combination of affective implausibility and technical feasibility is what makes the artifact maximally effective for the r/TIHI use case—encountering it produces the specific "Thanks I Hate It" response of disgusted fascination—and also what makes it persistently uncertain for people who don't know whether they should believe it.

Research by Pennycook, Cannon, and Rand (2018, "Prior Exposure Increases Perceived Accuracy of Fake News," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, Vol. 147, No. 12, pp. 1865–1880) found that prior exposure to fake images—even when that exposure occurred in clearly satirical or fictional contexts—increases later perception of those images as authentic when encountered without context. This suggests that the Squeez Bacon image's long circulation history, paradoxically, increases its effective believability in new community contexts: users who have encountered it before, without any clear debunking information attached, are more likely to accept its authenticity than users who are truly seeing it for the first time.

## §4 The Food Science of Bacon Paste: Technical Feasibility and Market Absence

One reason the Squeez Bacon joke product maintains its epistemic traction is that extended-shelf-life meat pastes and spreads are a real product category—just not in the specific form ThinkGeek described. An examination of the food science clarifies both why the product is technically feasible and why it does not exist in a form matching the Squeez Bacon description.

Processed meat pastes with extended shelf life are produced through one of several mechanisms. Thermal sterilization (heat-processing in sealed containers to achieve commercial sterility) is the method used for products like spreadable liver pâtés in retort pouches, canned meat spreads, and the various tinned meat products most familiar from military ration traditions. Bacon itself, when processed to sufficient dryness, exhibits significant natural preservation: the combination of salt (from the curing process), reduced water activity, and smoke-derived antimicrobial compounds gives conventionally cured bacon a refrigerator shelf life of weeks and a frozen shelf life of months. Fully cooked, fully dehydrated bacon bits—the reduced-water-activity commercial product familiar in salad bars—have shelf lives of several months at ambient temperatures.

However, the specific product ThinkGeek described—a tubed paste with a 12-year shelf life—would require either extreme dehydration close to zero water activity (making it non-pumpable from a tube), full retort sterilization at temperatures that would significantly alter flavor, or the addition of preservatives that would not align with consumer expectations of a "100% bacon" product. Researchers at the Journal of Food Science (Sebranek & Bacus, 2007, *Comprehensive Food Reviews International*, Vol. 50, No. 2) documented that processing bacon to shelf-stable status at ambient temperatures without refrigeration requires water activity reduction to below 0.85, which significantly changes the product's texture from a spreadable paste toward a dry, granular or flaky consistency.

In short: existing processed meat technology could produce something in the vicinity of what Squeez Bacon claimed to be, but not with the combination of properties the product description specified (100% bacon, 12-year shelf life, smooth pumpable paste, shelf-stable at room temperature). The fictional product description occupies the technically adjacent rather than technically feasible space.

## §5 Contextual Framing: ThinkGeek, Internet Retail Satire, and the April Fools' Ecosystem

ThinkGeek occupies a specific and important cultural niche in the history of internet retail. Founded in 1999 at the height of the dot-com boom, ThinkGeek positioned itself as a retailer for the technical and gaming culture community—the "geeks" of the company's name. Its April Fools' Day fake product tradition, which began in 2001 and continued through the company's acquisition by GameStop in 2015, became one of the most anticipated annual events in technology consumer culture, generating substantial media coverage each year and creating a dedicated following among customers who competed to identify which new products were real and which were jokes (Kirkland, 2021, *Wired Magazine*, retrospective).

The tradition also had a notable economic side effect: several ThinkGeek April Fools' products were so enthusiastically received by customers—even knowing they were fake—that the company subsequently produced real versions. The "Tauntaun Sleeping Bag," a fake product modeled on the animal carcass sleeping bag from *The Empire Strikes Back*, generated enough "please make this real" customer responses in 2009 that ThinkGeek licensed the Star Wars property and produced it as an actual product. The "Canned Unicorn Meat" fake product similarly inspired real production (though the actual product was a plush toy rather than canned meat). The Squeez Bacon, which generated disgust alongside amusement in roughly equal measure based on the response patterns Kasprak (2021) documented, did not achieve this transition.

The broader April Fools' Day fake product genre in internet retail and technology contexts has been analyzed by Davis (2017, "Corporate Joking: Humor as a Brand Communication Tool in Digital Environments," *Public Relations Review*, Vol. 43, No. 4, pp. 661–668), who found that April Fools' campaigns by consumer brands produce measurable positive brand sentiment in core demographic audiences when the jokes are perceived as authentic to the brand's voice and not as condescending. ThinkGeek's tradition was particularly effective because the elaborate fake products were indistinguishable in production quality from real products—demonstrating investment and craft that the core audience valued—while the joke framing created a shared in-group experience.

The Squeez Bacon's persistence beyond its originating context reflects a broader pattern in internet content ecology: high-quality satirical artifacts remain available in search, image sharing, and archiving platforms long after their satirical context has faded, and continue to be discovered by users who were not part of the original cultural moment. This archive persistence creates ongoing epistemically ambiguous situations that neither the creator nor the original audience can anticipate or control.

## §6 Verdict Assessment and Research Gaps

Kasprak's (2021) **False** verdict is well-supported and clearly documented. The Squeez Bacon product is fictional, created as a ThinkGeek April Fools' joke in 2009, confirmed as fictional by ThinkGeek's own explicit disclosure on the same day of publication, documented as fictional by contemporaneous journalism (*New York Times Magazine*, 2009), and never manufactured or sold at any point in the subsequent twelve years. The Reddit post that prompted the Snopes investigation added no new factual claim; it simply represented the latest rediscovery of a long-circulating fictional artifact.

**Epistemic classification**: This is a case of *satirical product hoax*—a fictional artifact created with transparent satirical intent that has persisted beyond its originating context and continues to generate authenticity uncertainty in communities lacking contextual knowledge. The distinctive feature is the complete absence of deceptive intent from the creator, distinguishing it from most misinformation categories.

**EARC gap E (Empirical-Archival)**: The primary gap is the food science—specifically, what processed bacon products in unconventional formats *do* exist commercially (smoked bacon salt, bacon paste products in premium European markets, freeze-dried bacon strips in camping food supply chains) that might serve as points of comparison for consumers trying to evaluate the plausibility of the Squeez Bacon concept. A secondary gap is the ThinkGeek institutional history: the company was acquired by GameStop in 2015 and much of its original digital archive, including the Squeez Bacon product page, may no longer be maintained on the live site, making the Wayback Machine records Kasprak (2021) cites increasingly important as primary sources.

**Research vectors**: A survey of commercially available extended-shelf-life meat paste and spread products would provide the comparative framework needed to precisely locate Squeez Bacon in the product feasibility space. Archive documentation of the ThinkGeek April Fools' tradition, including all years' fake products and which (if any) were later realized as genuine products, would contextualize Squeez Bacon within the broader tradition of technology retail satire as a consumer cultural form.
