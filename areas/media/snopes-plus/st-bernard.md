## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In 2001, a petition circulating widely via email claimed that St. Bernard dogs were being exported to China and other parts of Asia to be raised and slaughtered for food — sometimes, the petition alleged, after being subjected to torture designed to increase adrenaline levels in the belief that this improved the flavor or aphrodisiacal properties of the meat. The petition called on recipients to pressure the United Nations to intervene, citing the practice as an abhorrent violation of animal welfare standards.

Snopes investigator David Mikkelson (2001) rated this claim **True** — a verdict that requires careful unpacking, because "true" in this context does not mean the petition's most lurid claims were verified. Mikkelson rates the basic factual core as true: multiple European newspapers, including reporters physically based in Beijing, covered stories about the raising of St. Bernard dogs for food in China, quotations were gathered from Chinese breeders and restaurant operators, and a group in Switzerland called SOS St. Bernard Dogs had collected approximately 11,000 signatures on a petition to the Swiss government to lodge a formal protest with China. The verdict reflects the consensus of the available press reporting, offered with explicit epistemic caution: Mikkelson characterizes the assessment as "cautiously assuming the basic facts found in the reports are true, based on the sheer persistence of the reports."

The entry is notable not only for the unusual factual territory but for Mikkelson's (2001) extended ethical commentary on the cultural politics of dietary norms and the hypocrisy he identifies in Western reactions to dog consumption in China — a dimension of the fact-check that makes it an important document in the history of Snopes as an institution.

## §2 The Evidence Base: European Press Coverage of St. Bernard Farming in China

The evidential foundation of the True verdict rests almost entirely on press coverage from early 2001 in major European newspapers. As Mikkelson (2001) notes, news reports from China are notoriously difficult to verify, and any generalization about what "Chinese people do" must account for China's enormous diversity of population, diet, and regional practice. Nonetheless, Mikkelson treats the basic claims as credible "based on the sheer persistence of the reports," and that reporting is unusually consistent in its sourcing.

August (2001), writing in *The Times* of London, reported from Beijing on St. Bernard farming in the Datong region of northern China, where former coal miners — displaced by the collapse of the coal industry in that province — had taken up St. Bernard cross-breeding with local dog varieties as a more economically valuable alternative to conventional livestock. A breeder named Meng Li was quoted directly: "The meat is so tender and has a unique aroma." Song Shiyong, identified as head of the Shenyang Dog Meat Research Institute, was quoted claiming that St. Bernard breeding was "four times more profitable than pig or chicken farming." A brochure from the Shenyang Agriculture and Science Development Institute was reported to praise the breed's "high farrowing rate," "fine and tasty" meat, and disposability to "group breeding."

The Agence France-Presse (2001) and *The Scotsman* (Hall, 2001) and *The Daily Telegraph* (Rennie, 2001) covered the reaction from European animal advocacy groups. The International Fund for Animal Welfare (IFAW) and the Swiss lobby group SOS St. Bernard Dogs were central actors in the international protest. IFAW China director Grace Ge Gabriel was quoted in Reuters (Eckert, 2001) making a pointed rhetorical challenge: "If a Chinese cannot understand why Swiss people get so upset that they are eating St Bernards, I would ask that same question: If Swiss people eat China's panda, how would Chinese feel?"

Mikkelson (2001) himself reported attending the "Dog Meat King" restaurant in Beijing and sampling what he was assured was St. Bernard meat — or at least, the best available dog meat, which the restaurant claimed meant St. Bernard. His tasting notes are drily honest: "it tasted a bit like Irish beef stew but at the time it seemed more like a wet, crumbling digestive biscuit."

Even with all this reporting, Mikkelson flagged two significant evidentiary gaps. First, the most disturbing claim — that the dogs were subjected to deliberate torture to raise adrenaline levels before slaughter, in the belief this improved flavor — was "more difficult to verify." Second, the claim that intact purebred St. Bernards were being imported into China in large numbers did not make economic sense: importing expensive European dogs when local breeding was cheaper and more accessible seemed implausible. More likely, the "St. Bernard" farming being described involved cross-breeds or local dogs of the type sold under that label in China, rather than genuine imports at scale.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Persistence-Based Credibility Heuristic and Its Limits

The Snopes assessment of this claim relies on an epistemological heuristic that deserves explicit examination: the idea that the persistence of consistent press reporting from multiple independent sources is itself evidence of factual grounding. Mikkelson (2001) makes this reasoning explicit — he is "cautiously assuming the basic facts found in the reports are true, based on the sheer persistence of the reports."

This is a reasonable and commonly used heuristic in journalistic epistemology, but it carries specific failure modes that are worth identifying. The heuristic functions well when reports are genuinely independent — when journalists in different cities at different news organizations are arriving at the same story via different sources. It fails when reports are syndicated, reprinted, or derivative of a single original source that all subsequent reporting merely amplifies.

In the case of China-based international reporting in 2001, there is genuine reason to worry about the second scenario. As Mikkelson (2001) himself notes, "newspapers often pick up stories from each other without verifying the information (and thus all report the same apocryphal story as true, as recently happened with the [Working Stiff] legend)." The China coverage was unusually consistent across European sources, but several of the pieces described by Mikkelson appear to have been filing from the same Beijing-based pool of sources — the Shenyang Dog Meat Research Institute, the same restaurant, the same agricultural brochures. The *reporters* were in different cities; the *sources* they were quoting may have had a much smaller independent footprint.

This does not mean the reports were false. It means the epistemic confidence warranted by "persistence" is somewhat lower than the number of independent stories would suggest if those stories are drawing on a common primary source. The True verdict is defensible but represents a judgment call at the edge of the available evidence.

There is also an interesting structural irony. The fact-check was explicitly prompted by a viral email petition that contained several dubious or unverifiable elements (the torture claim, the scale of imports), yet the core finding — that St. Bernard farming for food existed in China — was considered verified. This creates an odd epistemic situation: the petition was simultaneously circulating demonstrably exaggerated claims and pointing toward a genuine phenomenon. The appropriate response is the layered one Mikkelson gives: verify the core, flag the unverifiable, and note what the available evidence does and does not support.

## §4 The Ethics of the Verdict: Cultural Relativism, Animal Welfare, and Hypocrisy

Mikkelson's (2001) extended ethical commentary in this entry is remarkable both as journalism and as ethics. The primary fact-checking task is completed by the second major section; the substantial subsequent material is philosophical argument, and it is unusually pointed by Snopes standards.

Mikkelson argues that Western outrage at the Chinese practice of eating dogs — and specifically at the eating of St. Bernards — reflects cultural double standards rather than principled animal welfare positions. The core argument: even if eating dogs is wrong, it is no more wrong than eating lambs, pigs, or cattle. If Americans would reject with contempt a Hindu petition demanding that the United States stop the "abhorrent practice" of eating cows, then American or European outrage at Chinese dog-eating is hypocritical unless it is grounded in some principled distinction between dogs and other animals — a distinction that most people invoking it cannot actually articulate.

The rhetorical force of this argument is substantial. The IFAW's Grace Ge Gabriel acknowledges the symmetry implicitly in her panda comparison, though she uses it to appeal to Chinese cultural sensitivity rather than to contest the ethical frame. Mikkelson (2001) extends the argument further: "our standards don't count, only ours do. Certainly most of us prefer that any animals used for food be raised and killed humanely, but we don't have to look to China to find exceptions to this standard — there are plenty of cows, chickens, pigs, and other food animals being raised and slaughtered in appalling fashion right here in our own backyard."

The argument is a version of what philosophers call the "consistency challenge" in applied ethics. It does not specifically defend the Chinese practice but rather targets the selective application of welfare concern: critics who are moved by dogs but not by the equivalent suffering of factory-farmed pigs are applying a category distinction based on familiarity and cultural symbolism, not on morally relevant principles about suffering, sentience, or capacity for pain — which are equivalent across dog, pig, lamb, and cow.

This extended commentary is part of what makes the Snopes entry on the St. Bernard petition historically significant. It was not the mandate of a fact-checking organization to adjudicate the ethics of international dietary norms, and Mikkelson's decision to do so placed Snopes in an explicitly evaluative position that goes beyond the binary True/False function. Whether or not one agrees with the ethical position — and there are coherent objections, particularly from the standpoint of animal welfare advocates who *do* oppose factory farming and dog-eating equally — the fact that the entry contains it reveals something important about the institutional culture of early Snopes: a willingness to use verified facts as a platform for broader normative argument.

## §5 Dog Meat Consumption: Historical and Anthropological Context

The cultural controversy surrounding the St. Bernard claim benefits from contextual grounding in the anthropology and history of dog meat consumption, a practice with considerably more global spread than the Western reaction would suggest.

Anthropological surveys of food cultures — including foundational work by Marvin Harris (1985, *Good to Eat: Riddles of Food and Culture*, Simon and Schuster) on the cultural logic of food taboos — have consistently found that the line between "food animal" and "non-food animal" is culturally constructed rather than biologically given. Dogs occupy the "food animal" category in parts of China, South Korea, Vietnam, the Philippines, Switzerland (historically; dog sausage was legal in Switzerland until recent years), and various Indigenous communities across the Americas. The same dogs are "pet animals" — and thus categorically inedible — in the dominant food cultures of Western Europe and North America.

Harris (1985) argues that food taboos generally track ecological and economic logic: animals become taboo as food when they are more valuable alive than dead (cattle in India, which serve vital plowing and dairy functions in subsistence agriculture), and they enter the food system when they are economically competitive with other protein sources. Dog meat consumption in China and Southeast Asia is highest in regions and periods where protein sources are limited and dog populations are sufficient to support culling.

The specific interest in St. Bernards for Chinese dog meat farming reported in 2001 reflects a further economic logic: large European breeds, which grow rapidly to weights of 100–200 pounds, produce significantly more meat per animal than typical small Asian dog breeds, and the cross-breeding programs described in the press reports were designed to capture this size advantage while producing animals adapted to local conditions. From the perspective of protein economics, St. Bernard farming is a rational optimization strategy — a fact that makes the Western reaction even more stark in its selectivity, since the same large-breed logic governs the selection of cattle, pigs, and poultry breeds in Western industrial farming.

## §6 Conclusions: Verified with Caution, Ethically Complex

The St. Bernard petition fact-check is a case study in the interaction between factual verification and cultural values in journalism. The core factual claim — that St. Bernard dogs were being raised for food in China, that a Swiss advocacy campaign existed to protest this, and that the story had genuine coverage from credible sources — is affirmed by the True verdict.

But the fact-check is as interesting for what it does not claim as for what it asserts. It does not verify the torture claims. It does not confirm that large-scale importation of Swiss-bred St. Bernards was occurring. It is honest about the limitations of press-based verification for China-based claims. And it turns the reader's attention not toward an international condemnation but toward a domestic mirror: if dog welfare in China is an outrage worth mobilizing petition campaigns, what does consistent silence about equivalent suffering of pigs and chickens in American factory farms reveal about the moral structure of that outrage?

The EARC gap classification of **C** (ethical/cultural) reflects the genuine ethical complexity of the entry, which raises questions that have no straightforward resolution in factual terms. The question "Is it true that Chinese people are eating St. Bernards?" is answerable. The question "Is this wrong?" is not answered by factual investigation, and Mikkelson's (2001) commentary makes clear that even his ethical position — one of cultural pluralism with consistent welfare standards — is contestable.

What the entry contributes to the Observatory's epistemic record is a demonstration that fact-checking at its best is not morally neutral. Choosing to include extended ethical commentary alongside a straightforward True verdict is itself a statement about what the fact-checker thinks the audience needs to understand — not just the facts, but their moral context.
