# Star Wars is filming in your town (viral casting call scam)


## 1. The Claim

The viral claim takes a characteristic form: a Facebook post, Instagram story, or occasionally a tweet announces that a major film production — most frequently invoked as *Star Wars*, though the template has been applied to the Marvel Cinematic Universe, various Disney live-action productions, and other high-profile franchise properties — is about to film in a specific town or city. The post specifies genuine local landmarks or the actual town name to establish apparent authenticity. It invites residents to register for paid extras roles or background acting positions, often specifying a daily rate — typically in the range of $200–$500 per day — that is meant to suggest professional compensation without being unbelievably large. The post includes a link to an external website where interested applicants should register.

The specificity of the local reference — "filming in [your town] starting next month" — is not the result of local knowledge or genuine production planning. It is the result of geolocation-based targeted advertising and the algorithmic personalisation of social media content. Facebook's advertising infrastructure, in particular, allows advertisers to target posts to users in specific geographic locations with high precision. Researchers studying scam advertising have documented that the same template post can be simultaneously "targeting" hundreds or thousands of different towns with locally-customised versions (Herley, C., 2012, *ACM Workshop on Security and Human Behavior* — while not specific to casting scams, Herley's framework for understanding why scams need not be optimally crafted is relevant here). The recipient receives a post naming their specific community and interprets the specificity as evidence of genuine local production planning, when it is in fact the output of an automated content personalisation system.

The scam has operated in multiple documented variants. One category leads to a third-party website that impersonates legitimate industry casting platforms (Central Casting, Actors Access, Casting Networks) and charges a "registration fee," "background verification fee," or "skills assessment fee" ranging from $20 to $150. Another category is a pure data harvest: the registration form collects name, address, email, phone number, and sometimes date of birth, building a profile that has value for email marketing lists and identity fraud enablement. A third category leads to a subscription service that enrolls the victim in a recurring monthly charge for a "casting database access" service that provides no genuine access to real casting opportunities. A fourth, more targeted variant requests payment card information under the pretext of background check processing fees or costume deposit.

Legitimate casting calls for major film productions do not operate in any of these ways. Background actors ("extras") for studio productions are typically cast through specific accredited background casting agencies — for Lucasfilm productions on recent *Star Wars* projects, this has included services like Extras Casting UK (for UK-based productions), Adam Casting, and similar regionally-specific professional agencies — none of which charge actors to register or to audition. This fundamental principle — that legitimate casting never charges the actor — is the industry standard articulated by SAG-AFTRA (the Screen Actors Guild and American Federation of Television and Radio Artists), Actors Equity, and every legitimate industry advisory body.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Star Wars productions do use local background actors in the communities where they film.** The kernel of plausibility in these posts is that major film productions do conduct background casting in actual filming locations, and this is a genuine source of short-term paid work for local residents. Lucasfilm productions have filmed extensively in the United Kingdom (Pinewood Studios and surrounding locations, Iceland, Norway, Jordan, and various other international sites), and background casting for these shoots has been conducted through legitimate casting agencies. The production experiences of extras on Star Wars films have been documented in multiple journalistic pieces and in self-published accounts by participants that confirm both the existence of the role and the means by which it is legitimately obtained.

The accurate version of the claim — that *Star Wars* productions hire local background actors through professional casting agencies when filming in specific locations — is true and publicly documented. The scam template exploits precisely this truth as its superficially accurate premise.

**Legitimate film productions do occasionally announce open casting calls.** High-volume background actor requirements for specific productions are sometimes publicly announced, particularly for crowd scenes requiring large numbers of extras from a specific demographic or location. These announcements are made through recognised channels: production companies' official social media accounts (verified), accredited casting agencies' own websites and social channels, entertainment trade press (Deadline Hollywood, Variety, The Hollywood Reporter), and SAG-AFTRA's official communications. They are not made through anonymous or unverified third-party social media posts linking to unfamiliar external websites.

**The $200–$500 daily rate figure is approximately accurate for background acting as a category.** SAG-AFTRA's contract minimum for background performers on theatrical features is set by negotiated agreements and is broadly in a range that makes the quoted figures superficially plausible. This accurate figure is deliberately used to calibrate the scam's credibility — the daily rate cited is high enough to be attractive but not so high as to seem immediately implausible to someone unfamiliar with the industry.

**Lucasfilm/Disney's official position.** Lucasfilm and Disney have explicitly stated, through official channels, that they do not hold open casting calls through social media, do not charge applicants at any stage, and recommend that people encountering such posts report them as fraud. Their official communications have been documented in responses to journalists following specific viral scam outbreaks.

**The Federal Trade Commission and FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) have documented casting scams as a recognised fraud category.** The FTC's consumer alerts have specifically mentioned entertainment-industry casting scams as among the most commonly reported employment fraud variants, noting that victims lose money through registration fees, background check fees, and subscription charges. The IC3 annual crime reports (FBI, IC3 2021 Internet Crime Report; FBI, IC3 2022 Internet Crime Report) classify this under "Non-payment/Non-delivery" and related fraud categories, representing aggregate reported losses in the millions of dollars annually across the employment fraud category as a whole.


## 3. Why People Believe This (And What Epistemics It Exploits)

The Star Wars casting call scam is a particularly instructive case of engineered plausibility, because the mechanism by which recipients are deceived is itself a sophisticated exploitation of a genuine epistemic shortcut — the inference from specificity to authenticity.

**Locality as an authenticity signal.** In ordinary social epistemology, when someone tells you something specific about your particular town — not "a town," not "a town somewhere in America," but unmistakably your specific community — you reasonably update toward taking it seriously. This inference is almost always justified in ordinary social experience: someone who mentions the specific name of a local street, business, or landmark in a context suggesting local knowledge probably does have local knowledge. The scam systematically breaks this inference by producing locally-specific content through automated targeting rather than local knowledge. The recipient applies an entirely reasonable heuristic — specificity implies local knowledge — to a situation engineered to trigger that heuristic fraudulently.

Researchers in computer security and fraud psychology have studied the role of personalisation in phishing and social engineering effectiveness. Iuga, Nurse, and Erola (2016, *Computers & Security*) documented that spear-phishing emails containing accurate personal information about the recipient produced significantly higher click-through rates than generic messages, even when recipients were aware of phishing risks in the abstract. The casting scam exploits an analogous dynamic with geographic personalisation: the place name is the "personal information" that produces the credulity update.

**Social proof through sharing mechanics.** Viral sharing of the post by other community members — sometimes people who have not themselves verified the claim but are enthusiastically sharing apparently exciting local news — creates a social proof dynamic. When a post has been shared by dozens of local Facebook friends, the social signal is not "many people sent this without checking it" but rather "many locally-connected people I trust found this credible." This is the same mechanism studied in rumour spread research: McKinney et al. (2019, *Social Media + Society*) and Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral (2018, *Science*) both document that false information on social networks spreads faster and more broadly than true information partly because it tends to produce stronger affective responses that motivate sharing.

**The Star Wars brand as cognitive anchor.** The choice of high-prestige franchise brands — Star Wars in particular — is not arbitrary. Lucasfilm is associated in public imagination with innovative location scouting: the original trilogy filmed in Tunisia, England, Norway, and the Guatemalan rainforest; the sequel trilogy in Iceland, Ireland, and Jordan; *Andor* and *The Mandalorian* in studio environments that nonetheless looked exotic. The public knowledge that *Star Wars* does film in unusual and varied locations reduces the prior probability of "Star Wars filming here is obviously implausible" and increases apparent credibility. The brand's cultural prestige also motivates sharing: people want to share exciting content, and the prospect of a major global franchise filming in one's community is genuinely exciting.

**Epistemic novelty: the information environment as the delivery mechanism.** The analytically distinctive feature of this particular scam — relative to earlier generations of casting scams conducted through newspaper classifieds or physical flyers — is that it is fully adapted to and dependent on the modern platform information environment in a way that cannot be adequately addressed by standard consumer education about "too good to be true" offers.

Earlier casting scams required victims to respond to individual advertisements, make individual inquiries, and take individual steps that allowed for reflection at each stage. The social media casting scam is delivered within the normal social information stream of the recipient's community, packaged in exactly the visual and discursive conventions of normal community announcements, and amplified by social proof through the sharing behaviour of the same community members whose endorsement would normally be a trustworthy signal. The scam is, in a technical sense, a corruption of the social epistemology infrastructure through which communities normally share locally-relevant genuine information. Fighting it requires not just consumer awareness of fraud (a well-established intervention domain) but awareness of *the conditions under which your own social network's endorsement is not a reliable authenticity signal* — a considerably more sophisticated epistemological point.

This represents a structurally new category of social engineering: rather than impersonating a trusted authority directly, the scam impersonates the *social process* through which trust is established. It weaponises community, not just brand.


## 4. Verdict

**False, and a documented fraud vehicle.**

The specific claim that Star Wars (or any other major franchise) is filming in my specific town and seeking paid local extras through a social media post linking to an external registration site is false. No legitimate casting for Lucasfilm or Disney productions operates through unsolicited social media posts linking to third-party registration websites. No such posts have ever represented genuine Lucasfilm or Disney casting activity. The template is a recognized fraud category documented by the FBI, FTC, and consumer protection agencies.

The specific mechanism exploited — geolocation-based social media advertising used to produce locally-customised scam content — is a technical capability of advertising platforms that can be deployed by anyone, does not require actual local knowledge, and is not detectable from the content of the post itself. The locally-specific content is therefore not evidence of genuine local production planning.

**Response protocol for recipients:** Anyone who encounters such a post should: (1) verify through official channels only — Lucasfilm's and Disney's official verified social accounts and official press releases, not third-party posts; (2) not click any external links in the post; (3) apply the fundamental principle that legitimate casting never charges applicants at any stage; (4) report the post as fraudulent advertising to the platform where it was encountered; (5) report to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov if money has been transferred.

**Snopes verdict: False.**

**EARC classification: C (Confirmed fraud — deliberate deception with financial harm intent).** The claim is not merely misleading or mistaken but is the output of an intentionally fraudulent operation designed to extract financial or personal information from recipients. The harm is direct and documented.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Star Wars casting scam is one variant within a broader ecosystem of geolocation-targeted entertainment industry scams that share the same architecture. The template has been applied to Marvel productions ("Avengers filming in [city]"), to talent agency recruitment ("Netflix seeking actors in [city]"), to music industry scams ("Record label seeking local musicians in [city]"), and to modelling agency recruitment ("Modelling scout visiting [city] next week"). All share the structural features identified above: geolocation targeting to produce apparent local specificity, a plausible aspirational opportunity, a credential-sounding but unverifiable source identity, and an external link leading to a monetisation mechanism.

The scale of aggregate financial harm from this scam category is difficult to estimate precisely because victims frequently do not report losses from relatively small individual amounts (a $50 registration fee, for instance), and because IC3 and FTC reporting is voluntary and selective. The National Consumer League's Fraud.org has collected reports suggesting that entertainment industry fraud represents a significant minority of all employment fraud reports, with median individual losses in the range of several hundred dollars, but concentrated in certain high-media periods around major franchise releases.

Platform responsibility is a contested dimension of this issue. Facebook's advertising infrastructure, which provides the granular geolocation targeting capability that makes the locally-personalised version of the scam possible, has been the subject of sustained criticism regarding its facilitation of fraud advertising. Academic research has documented that the same ad targeting tools used for legitimate commerce are routinely exploited for scam delivery, and that platform detection tools have historically had low sensitivity to geolocation-personalised fraud advertising relative to their sensitivity to other prohibited content categories. Meta (Facebook's parent company) has implemented various countermeasures since 2017, including enhanced verification requirements for some advertiser categories, but the casting scam template continues to generate new variants that evade automated detection.

The scam also sits in a broader context of entertainment aspiration exploitation that includes talent agency scams, music industry advance fee fraud, "film student casting" exploitation, and related schemes. These are catalogued extensively in the Better Business Bureau's ongoing reporting on entertainment industry fraud and in SAG-AFTRA's consumer protection advisories. SAG-AFTRA maintains a specific resource page on casting scam identification that is among the most practically useful preventive resources for prospective background actors.

An important protective ecosystem exists around legitimate extras casting that recipients of scam posts are typically unaware of. Background Acting is a fully legitimate profession with trade union representation, established pay scales, working condition protections, and a well-documented recruitment pathway. Someone genuinely interested in background acting work for film and television productions can pursue this through SAG-AFTRA's member resources, established background casting agencies that can be verified through their union partnerships, and local film commission offices that track legitimate productions in their jurisdictions. This pathway is slower and less exciting than clicking a social media link, but it is the real one.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has addressed the Star Wars casting call scam and returned a verdict of false, with detailed documentation of the scam's operational mechanics. The Snopes coverage emphasises the geolocation-targeting mechanism, the lack of any Lucasfilm or Disney affiliation, and the multiple monetisation pathways used by operators.

The coverage challenge for standard fact-checking is the high velocity of variant generation. Because the template is generic and requires only a place-name substitution and a new external link to generate a new scam instance, the scam generates hundreds or thousands of nominally distinct "claims" — each naming a different town — while sharing the same fraudulent architecture. Standard fact-checking processes, which typically investigate specific individual claims, scale poorly against template-based fraud that produces new variants faster than individual investigations can address them.

The most effective public-facing counter-strategy has therefore been template-based education rather than individual claim refutation: communicating the structural features that identify any post as a scam regardless of which brand or town it names (external link, registration fee requirement, no verifiable official source, promise of payment) rather than investigating whether a specific post about a specific town is real. SAG-AFTRA, the Better Business Bureau's Scam Tracker, the FTC's consumer alerts, and state-level consumer protection offices have all issued advisories emphasising these structural markers.

Media literacy education has increasingly incorporated social media scam architecture as a subject, with lesson frameworks from organisations including News Literacy Project and MediaWise that teach the structural characteristics of geolocation-personalised content as distinct from organically local content. The specific epistemological point — that locally-specific content in your social feed does not necessarily originate from local knowledge — is a sophisticated one that requires explicit instruction to recognise reliably, because it runs counter to deeply embedded and normally-useful social heuristics about specificity as an authenticity signal. Building this awareness at scale, particularly in communities with less dense professional networks in the entertainment industry (where the implausibility of a major studio's presence is obvious), represents a genuine challenge for platform-era media education.
