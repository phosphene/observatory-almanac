# The 'Starbucks Cup-Size Scam' Video Is an Old Internet Prank

## §1 — Claim and Verdict Summary

A video that went viral on Facebook in May 2022 purported to show a person pouring coffee from one Starbucks cup into a larger cup, with the surprising result that both cups filled to the brim — implying that customers ordering the larger (and more expensive) drink size were receiving the same amount of liquid as customers buying the smaller size. The claim attached to this video was that Starbucks' tiered cup-size pricing system is a "scam" because all cup sizes hold the same amount of liquid.

Snopes, in a fact-check published May 31, 2022 and authored by Dan Evon, rated the claim **False**. Different Starbucks cup sizes demonstrably hold different amounts of liquid: a "tall" is 12 ounces, a "grande" is 16 ounces, and a "venti" is 20 ounces. These differences are real and verifiable. The video deployed a deceptively edited visual trick — most likely a false bottom in the larger cup — to create the illusion that the smaller cup's contents filled the larger cup to the brim.

Crucially, this is not a new trick. Snopes identified iterations of the same prank targeting McDonald's cups going back to at least 2016, with Snopes itself having first debunked a McDonald's version of the prank in 2020 following a TikTok viral cycle, and a Jack in the Box version of the same prank circulating in 2017. The 2022 Starbucks version is a re-instantiation of a recurring internet hoax format that has been used against multiple fast-food chains over at least six years.

## §2 — The Claim in Context: Starbucks Cup Sizes and Public Perception

Starbucks operates a cup-size naming system that has been the subject of considerable public commentary since the company popularized it in the 1990s and early 2000s: beverages are designated as "tall" (12 oz), "grande" (16 oz), and "venti" (20 oz), using Italian- and French-derived terminology rather than the more conventional small/medium/large. A "trenta" size also exists for certain cold beverages at 30 ounces.

This naming system has generated sustained popular discussion and mild frustration, often framed in comedic or satirical terms, because the names do not correspond to their intuitive English meanings — "tall" is the smallest available size, while "grande" (Italian/Spanish for "big") is a medium. The gap between the terminology and conventional English size-descriptor intuitions has made the Starbucks size system a recurring cultural touchpoint for jokes about corporate pretension and consumer manipulation (Holt, Douglas B., 2004, *How Brands Become Icons: The Principles of Cultural Branding*, Harvard Business School Press).

This preexisting cultural skepticism about Starbucks' relationship with its customers around cup sizes creates an unusual receptive context for the cup-scam video. Audiences already primed to see Starbucks' sizing system as somewhat absurd or manipulative are more likely to find a video purporting to expose the *actual* scam hidden beneath the surface to be compelling and share-worthy. The video exploits ambient brand skepticism rather than generating suspicion from scratch.

Starbucks' trademark paper cups have also been in news coverage independent of this claim: as reported by NPR (Kamenetz, Anya, "Starbucks Plans to Phase Out Paper Cups in the U.S. and Canada," *NPR*, March 16, 2022), the coffee chain announced plans in early 2022 to phase out single-use paper cups — creating a period of heightened attention on Starbucks' cups specifically in the months immediately prior to the May 2022 viral video.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: The Recurring False-Bottom Cup Prank as a Media Ecosystem Pattern

The most analytically interesting dimension of this fact-check is what the *recurrence* of this specific prank type tells us about internet hoax dynamics, content replication cycles, and the selective memory of fact-checking ecosystems.

The Starbucks cup-scam video is not simply a false claim — it is one instantiation of an identifiable *prank format* that has cycled through multiple corporate targets over multiple years:

- **2016**: A YouTube channel "As Seen on Social Media" published a debunking of the cup-scam prank targeting McDonald's cups.
- **2017**: A version targeting Jack in the Box circulated; *Business Insider* reported on it (Arda, Yasmin, *Business Insider*, November 2017).
- **2019**: A version targeting the Starbucks cold drinks cup circulated, covered by the Malaysian publication SAYS (Jayne, Tamara, "A Starbucks Venti And A Grande Are The Same Amount," *SAYS*, August 30, 2019).
- **2020**: A viral TikTok claimed that McDonald's small, medium, and large cups contained identical amounts of liquid; Snopes debunked this (Snopes first cup-scam debunk, 2020, https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/mcdonalds-tiktok-drink-video/).
- **2022**: The Facebook video targeting Starbucks' tall/grande/venti sizes.

This pattern illustrates a well-documented dynamic in internet hoax ecology: effective deceptive techniques are templates, not single-use devices. A visual trick that generates millions of engagements when applied to McDonald's in 2020 will be rediscovered and reapplied to a different brand on a different platform two years later. The new audience for the 2022 Facebook version may have never encountered the 2020 TikTok version, making the old trick feel novel.

Research on internet hoax recycling and the "zombie rumor" phenomenon — the tendency for debunked claims to re-emerge in new contexts — is well-documented. Kate Starbird and colleagues (Starbird, Kate, Jim Maddock, Mania Orand, Peg Achterman, and Robert Mason, 2014, "Rumors, False Flags, and Digital Vigilantes: Misinformation on Twitter after the 2013 Boston Marathon Bombing," *iConference 2014 Proceedings*, pp. 654–662) documented how crisis-context misinformation re-circulates with each new crisis. A variant of the same dynamic applies to branded consumer prank content: each new platform generation (YouTube → TikTok → Facebook) provides a new audience cohort that has not previously encountered the same template.

The mechanism of the trick itself is worth documenting precisely. Dan Evon's analysis in the Snopes fact-check identifies the most likely technique as a cup with a false bottom — a hidden interior floor that reduces the apparent interior volume of the "larger" cup, making it fill to the brim when poured into from the smaller cup. This is a classic stage magic technique (Lamont, Peter, and Richard Wiseman, 1999, *Magic in Theory: An Introduction to the Theoretical and Psychological Elements of Conjuring*, University of Hertfordshire Press) applied to a consumer context. It exploits the fact that cups are opaque and have circular cross-sections that make volume estimation difficult by visual inspection alone.

Alternative suspected techniques include pouring from a cup that is not actually full, using deceptive camera cuts when the cup is moved out of frame to switch cups, or similar short-con techniques that take advantage of the lack of continuous visual tracking in a short video clip. Crucially, the trick does not require sophisticated post-production: it is a physical real-world deception that happens to be captured on video, not a digital manipulation of the video itself.

This distinction matters for fact-checking epistemics: detecting digital video manipulation requires computational forensic techniques (analysis of compression artifacts, frame inconsistencies, and similar). Detecting a physical stage magic trick in a video requires understanding of conjuring techniques and experimental replication — the most effective debunking is simply to *measure* the cups, which multiple YouTube creators did in May 2022 and confirmed that Starbucks cups hold different amounts as advertised.

## §4 — Evidence Review: What the Cups Actually Hold

The claim that Starbucks cups all hold the same amount of liquid is straightforwardly falsifiable through direct measurement. Multiple independent verification efforts confirmed the obvious:

- Starbucks' "tall" paper cup holds 12 fluid ounces.
- Starbucks' "grande" paper cup holds 16 fluid ounces (4 ounces more).
- Starbucks' "venti" paper cup holds 20 fluid ounces (8 ounces more than tall; 4 ounces more than grande).

In May 2022, following the viral spread of the Facebook video, "another set of YouTubers took it upon themselves to investigate," per Snopes' reporting. Their findings, consistent with every prior independent measurement, confirmed that different cup sizes held different amounts of liquid. This is perhaps the least surprising empirically verifiable fact in the history of consumer fact-checking: Starbucks is a publicly traded company subject to state and federal consumer protection standards, including truth-in-advertising and net-weight labeling requirements that apply to beverage containers.

The deceptive mechanism in the video is further underscored by a 2018 YouTube investigation using Starbucks' plastic cups for cold beverages (cited in the Snopes article), which demonstrated that the cold-drink cup sizes also hold different amounts as labeled. The pattern of independent replication across multiple years and multiple cup types — all finding that the cups hold different amounts — makes the falsity of the "scam" claim extremely robust.

Regarding the specific deception technique: physical false-bottom cups are detectable by weighing the cups (a cup with a false bottom would weigh more than a genuine empty cup) or by other measurement techniques. The Snopes investigation did not definitively prove which specific technique the 2022 video used but was confident enough to state: "While we're not entirely sure how the video above was created — our best guess is that the larger cup (B) had a false bottom to take up some volume space — we can say for certain that this video does not accurately represent the amount of liquid in two different sized Starbucks cups."

## §5 — Broader Significance: Brand Trust, Consumer Skepticism, and the Social Function of Exposure Hoaxes

The cup-scam prank belongs to a broader genre of content that could be called *exposure hoaxes* — fabricated demonstrations of corporate malfeasance that circulate because they confirm preexisting suspicions about major brands. Exposure hoaxes differ from ordinary disinformation in a key respect: they don't merely assert a false factual claim but *perform* a fake revelation, using the theatrical structure of an exposé to generate outrage.

The cultural power of exposure hoaxes derives from the intersection of two factors. First, many large corporations have genuine histories of consumer-unfriendly practices, creating justified ambient suspicion that makes exposure-style claims plausible. Second, the emotional experience of discovering you have been cheated — or nearly cheated — is a high-engagement emotional state that generates strong social media sharing behavior. The exposure hoax exploits the second factor to deliver the emotional experience of the first, independent of whether the specific claim is true.

Research on consumer brand trust and perceived fairness consistently finds that perceptions of being cheated — even through means more subtle than this video alleged — are particularly damaging to brand relationships (Aggarwal, Pankaj, 2004, "The Effects of Brand Relationship Norms on Consumer Attitudes and Behavior," *Journal of Consumer Research*, vol. 31, no. 1, pp. 87–101). The cup-scam video deploys this dynamic as a weapon against Starbucks' brand relationship with consumers: by presenting an apparently visible, demonstrable proof of consumer fraud, it attempts to trigger a fundamental breach-of-trust response.

The fact that the "proof" is fabricated does not necessarily mean audiences will cognitively update back to neutral once debunked. The continued influence effect — cited in the USDA egg price entry — applies here too: consumers who watched the cup-scam video and shared it may have formed a more activated suspicion of Starbucks' integrity that persists even after they learn the video used a false bottom trick.

This creates an asymmetric information environment in which viral exposure hoaxes can cause lasting damage to brand trust even when comprehensively debunked, because the emotional encoding of "Starbucks is scamming you" is more vivid and memorable than the correction "the video used a fake cup." Media literacy researchers (Lewandowsky, Stephan, Ullrich K. H. Ecker, Colleen M. Seifert, Norbert Schwarz, and John Cook, 2012, "Misinformation and Its Correction: Continued Influence and Successful Debiasing," *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, vol. 13, no. 3, pp. 106–131) identify this asymmetry as one of the core challenges for factual correction campaigns in the misinformation ecosystem.

The EARC assignment for this entry is **R** (Refuted). The cups hold different amounts; this is verified beyond reasonable doubt. The video is deceptively edited using a known stage magic technique.

## §6 — Conclusion and Epistemic Assessment

The "Starbucks cup-size scam" video does not demonstrate what it claims to demonstrate. Key findings:

1. Starbucks tall (12 oz), grande (16 oz), and venti (20 oz) cups hold different amounts of liquid as labeled — this is verified by direct measurement and is required by consumer protection standards.
2. The viral May 2022 Facebook video used a deceptive technique — most likely a false-bottom cup — to create the illusion that the same amount of liquid filled cups of different sizes.
3. This identical prank format has targeted McDonald's (2016, 2020), Jack in the Box (2017), and Starbucks' cold drinks (2019) before re-emerging targeting Starbucks hot drinks in 2022.
4. Independent verificaton by multiple YouTube creators in May 2022 confirmed normal cup-size volume differences.
5. The appeal of the hoax derives from ambient brand skepticism around Starbucks' unconventional cup-size nomenclature and preexisting consumer suspicions about corporate pricing practices.

For the Truth Vault, this entry models the class of **exposure hoaxes** — content that performs a dramatic revelation of corporate malfeasance — and illustrates the pattern of recurring prank formats that cycle across different brand targets and platform cohorts over multi-year timescales. The debunking of the 2022 Facebook version is the fourth documented fact-check of the same fundamental trick targeting different fast-food chains.
