# Starve a Fever, Feed a Cold

## 1. The Claim

"Feed a cold, starve a fever." Or: "Starve a fever, feed a cold." The proverb circulates in both orderings—a small irony for a piece of folk wisdom that claims to offer precise medical guidance. The claim is that the appropriate nutritional response to illness depends on its type: when you have a common cold, you should eat; when you have a fever, you should withhold food.

Sometimes this is extended to a mechanism: food generates metabolic heat (which you want for a cold, to warm the body against the chill; which you don't want for a fever, to avoid overheating). Sometimes the mechanism is immune-specific: fasting redirects resources from digestion to immune function. Sometimes no mechanism is offered at all—it is simply presented as received wisdom, the kind of thing grandmothers know.

The claim occupies unusual territory in the landscape of health myths. It is old enough to be venerable, simple enough to be memorable, and touches on mechanisms that modern immunology has begun to find scientifically interesting—if not clinically validated in the way the proverb implies.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

**The clinical consensus: eat and drink for both**

The standard guidance from authoritative medical institutions is consistent and clear. The Mayo Clinic recommends adequate nutrition and—critically—hydration for both colds and fevers. The NHS advises eating as much as you feel able and emphasizes fluid intake, especially for fever. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, in its guidance on managing cold and flu symptoms, does not recommend caloric restriction for fever.

The rationale is straightforward: illness increases metabolic demands. Fever drives up basal metabolic rate—roughly 7–13% per degree Celsius of temperature elevation. Fighting infection, mounting an inflammatory response, and producing antibodies all require energy. Depriving the body of calories during illness imposes a nutritional deficit at precisely the time nutritional resources are most needed.

For practical management of both cold and fever, the evidence-based priorities are hydration, rest, fever management with antipyretics when appropriate, and adequate nutrition. The distinction between "starve" and "feed" based on illness type does not feature in contemporary clinical guidance.

**The emerging mechanistic science: where it gets complicated**

In 2002, a study published in *Clinical and Diagnostic Laboratory Immunology* by van den Brink et al. attracted attention by finding something that looked, at a first glance, like a scientific validation of the old proverb. The study examined how metabolic state influenced the type of immune response mounted by healthy volunteers.

The finding: eating appeared to stimulate cytokine profiles associated with Th2 immunity (sometimes loosely characterized as type 2, or antibacterial/antiparasitic responses), while fasting appeared to shift the profile toward Th1 immunity (sometimes characterized as type 1, or antiviral/intracellular responses).

If correct and generalizable, this would suggest that fasting might favor antiviral immune responses—relevant for colds, which are viral—while eating might favor responses relevant to bacterial infections, some of which manifest with fever. The proverb would be backwards, on this reading—but at least it would point toward a real mechanism.

The van den Brink study was widely cited in popular science discussions of the proverb. Its interpretation, however, requires considerable caution:

- The study was conducted in healthy volunteers, not in people with colds or fevers. Extrapolation to sick individuals is not straightforward.
- The cytokine findings reflect population-level trends in a small sample. The effect sizes and clinical significance are not established.
- The Th1/Th2 distinction is a simplification. The actual immune response to any infection involves complex, overlapping, and context-dependent pathways that do not map cleanly onto "antiviral" vs. "antibacterial."
- Subsequent research has not produced a robust clinical trial demonstrating that fasting improves outcomes for viral infections or that eating improves outcomes for fever-associated bacterial infections.

The van den Brink study opened a door to interesting questions about the relationship between nutritional state and immune function. It did not close the case for the proverb.

**Intermittent fasting and immunity: a related but distinct literature**

A separate body of research on intermittent fasting has examined effects on inflammation and immune function. Some studies suggest that time-restricted eating or caloric restriction has anti-inflammatory effects, reduces certain markers of immune activation, and may benefit autophagy—the cellular cleanup process. Animal models have produced interesting results suggesting fasting before some types of immune challenge may improve outcomes.

This literature is genuinely emerging and genuinely contested. It does not cleanly support the specific proverb (feed colds, starve fevers), but it does suggest that the relationship between nutritional state and immune function is more complex than "always eat as much as possible when sick." The research is at an early stage and has not yet produced clinical recommendations that would alter the existing guidance.

**Appetite regulation during illness: the body's own signal**

One piece of the picture that clinical guidelines acknowledge is that illness naturally suppresses appetite. Fever produces anorexia—reduced appetite—through cytokine signaling (particularly IL-1β and TNF-α acting on hypothalamic appetite centers). This is not coincidental. It may be adaptive: reducing food intake during infection could redirect metabolic resources toward immune function, reduce feeding-associated pathogen exposure, or serve other functions.

The body's natural tendency to eat less during fever is real. Whether this signal should be overridden, followed, or modulated depends on circumstances—the severity of illness, the person's baseline nutritional status, the ability to tolerate food, and the presence of dehydration risk. Clinical guidance generally recommends eating what the sick person can tolerate rather than forcing intake or imposing restriction.

## 3. Why People Believe This

**Antiquity and authority**

The proverb dates to at least 1574, where a version appears in John Withals' English-Latin dictionary: "Fasting is a great remedie of feuer." A 1592 version more closely resembling the modern form appears in print. By the 18th century, the saying was well established in English folk medicine.

This antiquity carries implicit authority. Folk wisdom that has persisted for centuries is assumed to have survived because it works. This is not an unreasonable heuristic—practices that are actively harmful tend to disappear from folk medicine over time. But the heuristic is imperfect: folk wisdom also persists because it is memorable, socially transmitted, and reinforced by the availability heuristic (people remember cases where the rule seemed to work and discount cases where it didn't).

**Felt experience**

When you have a fever, you typically don't feel like eating. The proverb's "starve a fever" aligns with how sick people feel. When you have a cold—especially in the early runny-nose stage, before full-body malaise sets in—you may have normal or slightly reduced appetite. The proverb aligns with a reasonable approximation of illness-related appetite patterns.

The problem is that a rule derived from "eat what your body wants" does not need to be encoded as "starve a fever, feed a cold." The proverb implies an active prescription—you should deliberately restrict food during fever—rather than a description of natural appetite suppression.

**The van den Brink legitimization**

As noted above, the 2002 study gave the proverb a veneer of scientific support that has circulated widely in popular health media. Many articles on the topic cite it as evidence that "science confirms grandmother's wisdom." This narrative—folk wisdom vindicated by modern research—is appealing, and its wide circulation has given the proverb renewed cultural credibility in the early 21st century, even though the research does not support the clinical practice the proverb recommends.

## 4. Verdict

**Contested — not simply false, but not clinically supported.**

This is a EARC-C case: the science is genuinely contested at the mechanistic level, even while the clinical consensus is clear.

**What is established:**
- Adequate nutrition and hydration are recommended for both colds and fevers by all major medical authorities.
- Illness increases metabolic demands; caloric restriction during acute illness imposes a nutritional cost.
- No clinical trial has demonstrated improved outcomes from deliberate fasting during fever or deliberate increased eating during colds.

**What is genuinely uncertain:**
- The relationship between metabolic state and immune response type is a live research area.
- The adaptive significance of illness-induced appetite suppression is not fully resolved.
- The long-term effects of nutritional state on immune function are complex and context-dependent.

The proverb as a literal clinical prescription—actively starve fever, actively force-feed colds—is not supported and contradicts mainstream guidance. The proverb as a rough pointer toward an interesting area of immunology-nutrition research gets slightly more credit than a simple false verdict warrants.

**Practical guidance:** Eat what you can tolerate. Prioritize fluids, especially during fever. Don't force large meals if severely nauseated, but don't deliberately restrict calories during illness.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The "starve a fever" proverb occupies an important category in health mythology: claims that are neither clearly true nor clearly false, where the emerging science is being actively recruited by both those who want to vindicate folk wisdom and those who want to dismiss it. This category is intellectually more interesting than simple myths and more practically difficult to communicate.

The proverb's persistence reflects something worth taking seriously about folk medicine traditions. These traditions encode observational knowledge accumulated over centuries without the benefit of controlled trials. Some of that knowledge is wrong. Some of it is approximately right for reasons that are only now becoming clear. And some of it gestures toward real phenomena with mechanisms that were not available to earlier observers to articulate.

The relationship between nutrition and immunity is one of the most active areas of contemporary biomedical research. Intermittent fasting, the gut microbiome, nutritional modulation of inflammation, the metabolic demands of different immune response types—these are all legitimate research programs with genuine findings. The proverb did not anticipate these mechanisms, but it circulates in an environment where they are being discovered, and it will be selectively cited by those who want to claim scientific support.

This creates a communication challenge that is different from the one posed by simple false myths. With "blind as a bat" or "bulls charge red," the correction is straightforward: here is the false claim, here is the contrary evidence, case closed. With "starve a fever, feed a cold," the response requires more nuance: the clinical recommendation is clear, but the underlying physiology is actively contested, and the relationship between nutritional state and immune function is genuinely not settled.

Health communicators operating in contested territory like this must resist two temptations: the temptation to oversimplify toward "it's obviously wrong" when the science is live, and the temptation to treat emerging mechanistic research as clinical validation when it isn't. The honest answer is "we don't know the full mechanism, but we do know what clinical guidance says, and it doesn't support the proverb as a practice."

The proverb also illustrates the role of reversibility in folk wisdom transmission. "Starve a fever, feed a cold" and "Feed a cold, starve a fever" circulate simultaneously and are frequently confused. This reversal is telling: if the rule were operationally important and clinically consequential, the correct ordering would be more firmly fixed. The fact that people are uncertain which way it goes suggests that the proverb functions more as a cultural artifact that signals attention to the fever/cold distinction than as an actually-followed clinical practice.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The "starve a fever" proverb has been addressed by a range of health fact-checkers and science communicators. The consensus in mainstream health media—WebMD, Healthline, NHS patient information, Mayo Clinic—is that the proverb does not reflect current clinical guidance and that adequate nutrition and hydration are appropriate for both conditions.

Snopes has addressed related folk medicine claims. The general approach in the fact-checking community is to rate the proverb as "mostly false" or "unproven" rather than simply "false," acknowledging the interesting mechanistic research while declining to endorse the clinical practice.

The van den Brink 2002 study presents a persistent challenge for communicators. It is widely cited in popular science articles as evidence for the proverb, and many readers take it as a conclusive validation. Careful fact-checking needs to explain both what the study found and what it didn't find—that metabolic state affects cytokine profiles in healthy volunteers does not translate to "sick people should fast when they have a fever." This requires more than a binary true/false verdict; it requires contextualizing the study within the broader evidence landscape.

The contested-consensus designation is appropriate here because authoritative clinical bodies have not incorporated the mechanistic research into their guidance, and the research itself has not reached the stage of clinical trial validation. The proverb is not supported as a practice, but the science it accidentally gestures toward is not settled. Fact-checkers operating in this space need to hold both simultaneously: the clinical answer is clear, and the scientific questions are open. Conflating these two registers—either by dismissing the science because the proverb is wrong, or by endorsing the proverb because the science is interesting—produces worse public health communication than treating them separately.

The most intellectually honest verdict: the proverb as a clinical prescription is unsupported and should not be followed. The proverb as a folk intuition that nutrition and immunity are related is pointing at a real question that science is actively investigating. The two can both be true.

### What the Immunology Actually Says: A Closer Look

The van den Brink 2002 finding is worth examining in more detail, because it has been substantially oversimplified in popular discussions. The study gave healthy volunteers either a standard breakfast or nothing, then measured cytokine levels and immune markers. The key finding: the fed group showed higher levels of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) relative to interleukin-4 (IL-4), and the fasted group showed the opposite ratio.

IFN-γ is associated with Th1 immune responses—classically important for fighting intracellular pathogens including viruses. IL-4 is associated with Th2 responses—important for extracellular pathogens, including some bacteria and parasites. If eating suppresses Th1 and promotes Th2, the proverb's reasoning might seem to run: fever is often bacterial (Th2-relevant), so eat; colds are viral (Th1-relevant), so fast.

But this reading involves several layers of simplification that each introduce significant error:

First, the Th1/Th2 framework is itself a simplification that has been substantially revised since 2002. Modern immunology recognizes Th17, Treg, and numerous other T-helper phenotypes that complicate the simple Th1/Th2 binary. Infections involve complex, sequential immune programs that don't map cleanly onto either arm.

Second, fever is not reliably bacterial. Many fevers are viral. The common cold causes fever in some presentations. Influenza is viral and typically produces high fever. COVID-19 is viral and produces fever. The mapping of "fever = bacterial" is too imprecise to serve as a dietary guidance rule.

Third, the van den Brink study was in healthy people and measured cytokines at rest, not during an actual infection. The immune dynamics of active viral or bacterial infection involve a cascade of events—pathogen recognition, innate response, adaptive response, resolution—that differ substantially from baseline cytokine variation in healthy volunteers.

Fourth, even if the Th1/Th2 ratio is responsive to nutritional state in healthy people, this does not mean that manipulating nutritional state during illness will steer the immune response toward better outcomes. Correlation between metabolic state and cytokine ratios does not establish that dietary restriction improves antiviral immunity in sick people.

### The Role of Fever Itself

Fever is not simply a symptom to be managed; it is an evolved physiological response with immune-modulating functions that complicate the "starve the fever" instruction.

Elevated body temperature directly impairs the replication of several common pathogens. Influenza virus, rhinoviruses (a major cause of the common cold), and SARS-CoV-2 all show temperature-sensitive replication kinetics, with reduced replication rates at temperatures above 37°C (98.6°F). Fever also enhances certain immune responses: T-cell proliferation, neutrophil function, and interferon signaling are all potentiated at temperatures in the 38–40°C range.

The decision to reduce fever with antipyretics (aspirin, acetaminophen, ibuprofen) is complicated by this understanding. Aggressive early antipyretic use may actually slightly prolong some infections by removing the thermal environment in which the immune system operates optimally. This is an active area of clinical debate—particularly for mild-to-moderate fever in otherwise healthy adults.

For the proverb, this matters because fever management intersects with nutritional management. The metabolic cost of maintaining an elevated temperature is real and substantial. Reducing fever through antipyretics and reducing caloric intake simultaneously may produce additive metabolic strain that neither intervention would produce alone. There is no clinical trial evidence addressing this specific question, but it illustrates the complexity of giving a simple nutritional rule for a physiologically complex state.

### History of the Proverb in Medical Literature

The 1574 John Withals dictionary entry gives the earliest traceable print citation, but similar ideas appear in earlier European medical traditions drawing on humoral theory. Galenic medicine, which dominated European medical practice from late antiquity through the 16th century, taught that fever was caused by excess heat in the body and that food generated heat through the process of digestion. "Cooking" the humors through digestion produced caloric heat, and starving the fever—withholding the food that generated heat—was therefore rational within the humoral framework.

The humoral framework was wrong, but it was internally consistent. The proverb made mechanistic sense within a model of disease that was coherent and authoritative for its time. When that model was replaced by germ theory in the 19th century, many folk medical practices derived from humoral theory should have been retired. The persistence of "starve a fever" through the germ-theory revolution and into the era of immunology illustrates the durability of medicinal folk wisdom that becomes culturally embedded before the theoretical framework that generated it is overturned.

By the late 19th and early 20th centuries, nutritional science began to develop the concept of caloric requirement during illness. Hospital protocols from this era show increasing emphasis on caloric support for febrile patients—the clinical tradition running directly counter to the folk wisdom. The tension between "starve a fever" as folk practice and "feed the sick" as clinical protocol has been present for over a century. The folk wisdom has outlasted repeated clinical corrections.

### Practical Guidance Synthesized from the Evidence

For readers wanting actionable guidance rather than theoretical discussion:

**For colds**: Eat as normal. If appetite is reduced (common with moderate colds), eat what is comfortable. Focus especially on hydration—nasal congestion and postnasal drip increase fluid needs. Hot liquids (soup, tea) provide comfort and steam, and warm broth provides sodium that supports fluid retention. There is no reason to force increased intake beyond normal.

**For fever**: Eat as normal, or as much as the sick person can tolerate. Do not deliberately restrict food. Fever increases metabolic rate significantly; the body needs calories. Focus heavily on hydration—fever increases insensible fluid loss through respiration and sweating. Cool water, electrolyte solutions, and dilute juice are appropriate. Force fluids actively in young children and elderly people, who are most vulnerable to dehydration.

**For both**: If the sick person genuinely cannot eat due to nausea or severe illness, do not force intake. But deliberate restriction—consciously withholding food because "starving a fever" is the rule—is not supported by evidence and may be counterproductive.
