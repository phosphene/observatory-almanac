# 'Stop Cuddling With Your Cat,' Misleading Ad Claims

**Snopes Verdict:** FALSE  
**Original URL:** https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/stop-cuddling-with-your-cat/  
**Snopes Author:** Jordan Liles  
**Snopes Published:** December 9, 2021

---

## §1 — Overview and Claim

In the autumn and winter of 2021, internet users encountered a peculiar and emotionally provocative online advertisement that carried a stark directive: "Stop Cuddling With Your Cat, Here's Why." The advertisement promised a revelation — an urgent, practical reason why the millions of people worldwide who sleep with, snuggle, or otherwise physically embrace their domestic feline companions should reconsider that behavior. The emotional hook was carefully calibrated. Cat owners, particularly those whose cats are sources of comfort and emotional bonding, experienced both a frisson of concern and a pressing curiosity: *what* could possibly justify such advice, delivered with such bluntness?

The advertisement appeared on the Taboola advertising network, a widely-deployed content discovery platform that embeds recommendation widgets at the bottom of mainstream news websites and popular content platforms. As of at least November 2021, the ad circulated across multiple websites and was algorithmically surfaced to users based on engagement-optimization signals. Taboola's system incentivizes content that generates click-through behavior, and the "stop cuddling with your cat" phrasing is a textbook example of what digital media researchers describe as curiosity-gap advertising — a format that withholds enough information to compel a click but promises a meaningful payoff on the other side of the link.

The claim Snopes chose to evaluate was straightforward: did the advertised article actually reveal any reason why cat owners should stop cuddling with their cats? The investigation, led by Senior Reporter Jordan Liles who has been with Snopes since 2016, required clicking through up to 60 pages of slideshow-formatted content to reach a conclusive answer. The answer was definitively no.

This Truth Vault entry analyzes the full epistemic architecture of the advertisement, assesses the underlying scientific landscape concerning human-cat physical contact, situates the incident within the broader advertising arbitrage ecosystem, and evaluates what this type of deceptive advertising infrastructure reveals about the mechanisms by which false or misleading claims propagate in modern digital environments.

---

## §2 — Snopes Investigation and Verdict

Jordan Liles investigated the advertisement and traced its destination to two websites: **Post Fun** and **Money Cougar**. Both sites hosted lengthy slideshow articles — a format in which each individual "next" click by the reader registers as a new page view and thereby generates additional advertising revenue. The Post Fun article ran to at least 60 pages before Liles and his team reached its conclusion; neither the first page, the last page, nor any intermediate page contained any explanation of why cat owners should stop cuddling with their cats.

The headline on the Post Fun website read: "Your Cat's Oddest Behaviors, Explained." The opening text did not reference any health threat, behavioral concern, or medical warning associated with cuddling. It described, in general terms, why cats head-butt people, why they push objects off counters, and why they race around at 3 a.m. — a compendium of familiar feline behavior explainers with no connection to the advertisement that directed readers there.

The Money Cougar version was, if anything, even more diametrically opposed to the advertised premise. Its headline read: "Here's What It Really Means When Your Cat Curls Up With You For Cuddles." The opening paragraph explicitly challenged the stereotype that cats are aloof and indifferent, arguing that cats genuinely enjoy cuddling with humans and that the behavior reflects authentic affection. This, too, was documented independently: the r/savedyouaclick subreddit, a Reddit community dedicated to exposing clickbait advertisements by summarizing what they actually contain, had already investigated and documented the Money Cougar article, confirming that it contained zero advice to stop cuddling with cats.

Snopes rated the overall claim **FALSE**. The advertisement made an implicit factual commitment — that a reason to stop cuddling with cats existed and would be disclosed — and the destination content categorically failed to fulfill that commitment.

The article explicitly identifies the mechanism: advertising arbitrage. The advertiser on Taboola's platform paid to have the "stop cuddling" ad displayed, attracting clicks from curious or concerned cat owners. Those clicks directed users to pages that served additional third-party advertisements on each of their 60 slides. If the revenue generated per pageview from those ads exceeded the per-click cost of running the initial Taboola ad, the entire operation generates profit. The content itself is economically incidental — it need only be long enough to hold the visitor while the ads load, and emotionally neutral enough to avoid triggering content moderation.

## §3 — Epistemic Analysis

The "stop cuddling with your cat" incident represents a category of deception that standard fact-checking frameworks are poorly equipped to classify, and this constitutes the **epistemic novelty** of this case.

The traditional model of misinformation involves a propositional claim that is factually incorrect — for instance, "vaccines cause autism" makes an assertion about causal relationships that can be evaluated against empirical evidence and declared false. Debunking that claim follows a clear logic: identify the claim, assess the evidence, render a verdict. The "stop cuddling" advertisement, however, does not assert a specific false proposition. It makes a **performative promise** — the promise that a reason will be revealed — and fails to deliver on that promise. The advertisement's falsity is not propositional but architectural: it is deceptive at the level of its delivery mechanism rather than its content.

This distinction matters epistemically because it reveals a gap in public media literacy frameworks. Standard advice about evaluating claims — "check the source," "look for primary evidence," "consult experts" — is designed for false assertions. It does not help a reader identify advertisements that are false in their structure rather than their propositions. A reader could, in principle, successfully navigate to the end of the 60-page slideshow, correctly identify that no cuddling advice was provided, and still lack a conceptual framework to describe *why* the advertisement was misleading. They might even suspect that they missed a page, or that the advice was there but oblique.

This structural deception has been analyzed by researchers studying native advertising and sponsored content. Wojdynski and Evans (2016, *Journal of Advertising*) demonstrated that native advertising — paid content designed to resemble editorial content — reduces readers' critical evaluation of claims, even when disclosure labels are present. The curiosity-gap structure compounds this effect: by embedding urgency ("stop"), command framing, and a withheld payoff, the ad generates emotional investment before the reader can exercise critical judgment.

The Taboola advertising network, which has hosted numerous misleading advertisements of this type, operates as what Snopes calls an "arbitrage ad scam" infrastructure. Taboola is a legitimate company with policies against misleading advertisements, but the incentive structure of the platform creates conditions in which false promises are profitable and enforcement is reactive rather than systematic. The "stop cuddling" ad is not an isolated anomaly but a representative specimen of a category.

From an epistemics-of-deception standpoint, the case also raises questions about the threshold for harm. No single viewer reading the Post Fun article about cats knocking things off counters is meaningfully harmed by that reading experience (beyond the annoyance and time waste of clicking through 60 pages). Yet at aggregate scale, the cumulative erosion of trust in online information environments — where promises are routinely unfulfilled and urgent framings are routinely disconnected from any urgent content — constitutes a real epistemic cost to society. Wardle and Derakhshan (2017, *Council of Europe*) describe this as "information disorder," which encompasses not just false content but content that creates a toxic epistemic environment through structural manipulation.

## §4 — Scientific and Empirical Context

The implicit premise of the advertisement — that there might be genuine medical or behavioral reasons for cat owners to stop cuddling their pets — is worth examining on its own merits, independent of the advertisement's fraudulence.

The scientific literature on human-animal physical contact, specifically with domestic cats (*Felis catus*), is largely positive about its health and psychological effects. Allen et al. (2002, *Psychosomatic Medicine*) found that pet ownership, including cat ownership, was associated with lower cardiovascular reactivity to stress in controlled experimental settings. Beetz et al. (2012, *Frontiers in Psychology*) reviewed 69 studies on human-animal interaction and found consistent evidence that animal-assisted interactions reduce cortisol levels and self-reported anxiety.

There are, however, legitimate concerns associated with close human-cat contact that scientific and veterinary literature does document. Toxoplasmosis, caused by the parasite *Toxoplasma gondii*, is transmissible from cats to humans via fecal contamination, though not through direct cuddling unless the human subsequently makes hand-to-mouth contact after touching contaminated surfaces (Hill and Dubey, 2002, *Veterinary Parasitology*). Cat scratch disease, caused by *Bartonella henselae*, can be transmitted through scratches or bites, not through routine cuddling (Kordick and Breitschwerdt, 1995, *Journal of Clinical Microbiology*). Cat allergens (Fel d 1) are a documented respiratory concern for sensitized individuals, and close contact increases allergen exposure (Ingram et al., 1995, *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology*).

None of these represent a blanket case for stopping physical affection with cats. They are context-dependent health considerations that would warrant discussion in a genuine educational article. The fact that Post Fun and Money Cougar addressed none of this material underscores that the sites had no interest in the medical reality of human-feline relationships; they were interest-harvesting vehicles with no educational mandate.

There is also domestic behavioral research to consider. A study by Turner and Bateson (2014, *The Domestic Cat: The Biology of Its Behaviour*) documents that cats vary considerably in their attachment behaviors and tolerance for physical handling, and that cats who initiate contact show greater behavioral contentment than those held without invitation. This is useful nuance for cat owners, but again, it is not advice to "stop" cuddling — it is context for understanding it.

## §5 — Misinformation Patterns and EARC/Gap Classification

**EARC Classification: Rhetorical Gap (R)**

This case is classified under the **Rhetorical Gap** category of the EARC framework. The deception does not arise from faulty empirical data, contested causal claims, or false authority citations. It arises from the rhetorical structure of the advertisement itself — the gap between what the advertisement promises and what the destination content delivers. The falsity is a function of framing and promise-architecture, not of empirically defective content.

The broader misinformation pattern here is **advertising arbitrage clickbait**, a well-documented phenomenon in media ecology. Its characteristic features are: (a) an emotionally activating headline using urgency, taboo, or threat framing; (b) a content destination that is irrelevant to the headline's promise; (c) monetization via per-page-view advertising revenue; and (d) dependency on large traffic volume rather than per-user engagement.

Snopes has documented dozens of such advertisements, and the organization explicitly invites readers to submit ad screenshots and destination URLs for investigation. This case is particularly useful as a pedagogical example because the advertised topic — cuddling with pet cats — is low-stakes enough that the deceptive mechanism can be analyzed without the confounding emotional valence that surrounds politically or medically consequential clickbait. It serves as a clean illustration of structural deception at its most transparent.

The gap in public knowledge this case exposes is the **structural literacy gap** — the difference between propositional fact-checking skills and the skills needed to recognize promise-deception, native-advertising intent, and arbitrage-driven content production. This gap is not addressed by most media literacy curricula, which focus heavily on evaluating claims rather than evaluating content architectures.
