# Strange radio signals detected from space are probably extraterrestrial


## 1. The Claim

Reports about anomalous radio signals detected from space periodically engage popular attention with headlines suggesting that such signals are either definitively or probably extraterrestrial in origin. These headlines appear in a range of media types, from tabloid science journalism to more reputable popular science publications, and they attach to a recurring cast of phenomena: Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs), unidentified repeating radio sources, signals that match SETI search criteria, and historical enigmas such as the 1977 "Wow! signal" detected at the Ohio State University Big Ear radio telescope.

The claim takes various forms. In its strongest version: "scientists have detected a signal that may be from aliens." In its more hedged version: "astronomers cannot explain this strange signal from space." In its implication-laden version: "the signal has characteristics consistent with artificial origin." What all these formulations share is a framing that positions the most dramatic possible explanation — intelligent extraterrestrial civilisation — as either the leading hypothesis or a serious contender for unexplained astronomical observations. Each new detection event generates a cycle of media coverage that functions on the same template regardless of the specific signal in question.

The claim pattern has notable examples in recent decades. In 2015, the "Wow! signal" received renewed attention following the announcement of the Breakthrough Listen SETI initiative funded by Yuri Milner. In 2016, the cometary explanation for the Wow! signal proposed by Paris et al. generated both scientific discussion and media coverage reframing the 1977 signal as newly mysterious. FRBs have been identified as possible alien transmission candidates by media outlets since the first detection in 2007, despite the astrophysics community developing steadily more robust natural explanations. In 2019, the Breakthrough Listen project detected a narrowband radio signal at 982 MHz appearing to originate from the direction of Proxima Centauri. The signal, designated BLC1, generated substantial media coverage suggesting a possible alien origin before being identified in 2021 as likely terrestrial radio frequency interference (TRFI) from human-made sources. In each of these cases, the pattern is consistent: anomaly detected, "extraterrestrial" hypothesis enters media framing, natural or terrestrial explanations develop over subsequent investigation, popular interest largely moves on without completing the explanatory arc.

This entry is concerned not primarily with any single signal but with the recurrent claim pattern: the framing of unexplained astronomical radio phenomena as "probably" or "possibly" extraterrestrial in the sense of involving intelligent alien origin.


## 2. What's Actually True

**All radio signals from space are extraterrestrial in the most literal meaning.** The most important ambiguity in the claim concerns the word "extraterrestrial." In the strictest technical sense, any signal originating from outside Earth's atmosphere is extraterrestrial. This includes signals from the Sun, signals from Jupiter's magnetosphere, signals from pulsars and magnetars, and signals from galaxies billions of light-years away. Everything detected by radio telescopes pointed away from Earth is extraterrestrial in this sense. None of this implies any connection to intelligent alien life. The popular use of "extraterrestrial" in this context, however, strongly implies the second meaning: not merely extra-Earth in origin, but originating from an intelligent technological civilisation beyond Earth. The claim that strange signals are "probably extraterrestrial" slides between these two meanings systematically, and the slide creates the misleading impression.

**Fast Radio Bursts: genuine astrophysical mystery with advancing natural explanations.** FRBs are among the most intensely studied phenomena in contemporary astrophysics. They are genuinely mysterious in important senses: the first one was discovered serendipitously in 2007 in archival data from the Parkes radio telescope by Lorimer and Bailes (Lorimer, D. R., Bailes, M., McLaughlin, M. A., Narkevic, D. J., & Crawford, F., 2007, "A Bright Millisecond Radio Transient," *Science*, 318(5851), 777–780), and their origins were debated intensely for years. They are extraordinarily energetic — a typical FRB releases roughly as much energy in milliseconds as the Sun emits over several days — and they originate from cosmological distances, with some detected from sources over 8 billion light-years away.

However, the scientific community's investigation of FRBs has progressively confirmed natural astrophysical explanations. The single most important development was the April 2020 detection of an FRB from within the Milky Way galaxy, originating from a magnetar — a type of neutron star with an exceptionally strong magnetic field — designated SGR 1935+2154 (CHIME/FRB Collaboration, Andersen, B. C., Bandura, K. M., Bhardwaj, M. et al., 2020, "A bright millisecond-duration radio transient from a Galactic magnetar," *Nature*, 587, 54–58). This detection, from a source whose nature was already independently identified, provided direct evidence that at least some FRBs — and by implication likely the majority — are generated by magnetar activity. The magnetar flare mechanism provides a physically coherent account of FRB energetics and, in the case of repeating FRBs, the pattern of burst recurrence. The leading alternative models — neutron star mergers for non-repeating FRBs, and the "blitzar" model involving rotationally-supported supramassive neutron stars collapsing to black holes — also involve known and well-characterised astrophysical processes. No professional astrophysical analysis of FRBs has concluded that the evidence favours an artificial origin; the scientific consensus is that natural explanations are both sufficient and much better supported than any artificial alternative.

**The Wow! signal: genuinely unexplained but not evidence of aliens.** The Wow! signal, detected on August 15, 1977, at the Ohio State University Big Ear radio telescope during a SETI survey, is the most famous candidate anomalous radio signal and the canonical case for the claim pattern. Astronomer Jerry Ehman circled the signal on the computer printout and wrote "Wow!" beside it. The signal was strong, narrowband (appearing at approximately 1420 MHz, the hydrogen line frequency that SETI research considers a candidate for intentional interstellar communication), and lasted for the full 72 seconds the Big Ear's rotating beam could observe a single sky position. It has never been detected again despite substantial follow-up observation.

The Wow! signal remains genuinely unexplained. It does not have a confirmed astrophysical source identification. However, "unexplained" is not the same as "probably alien." Two serious natural hypotheses have been advanced. In 2016, astronomer Antonio Paris proposed that the signal originated from a hydrogen cloud associated with one of two comets — 266P/Christensen and P/2008 Y2 (Gibbs) — that were in the relevant sky region in 1977 (Paris, A. B., 2016, "Hydrogen Line Observations of Cometary Neutrals," *Journal of the Washington Academy of Sciences*, 102, 1–10). Paris conducted follow-up observations during a subsequent passage of comet 266P/Christensen and reported detecting similar emission. This hypothesis has been contested on the grounds that cometary hydrogen clouds were not well-characterised as strong narrowband radio emitters at the time and that the Big Ear's beam geometry may not have been scanning the right sky position for either comet. In 2024, a preprint by Abel Méndez and colleagues proposed a "superradiance" mechanism, in which a transient high-energy astrophysical event such as a magnetar flare induces stimulated emission from a neutral hydrogen cloud, producing a bright narrowband pulse (Méndez, A. et al., 2024, *arXiv:2408.08513*). This model would naturally produce a signal with the characteristics of the Wow! signal and explain its non-recurrence.

Neither explanation is definitively confirmed. What the accumulated analysis demonstrates is that the most scientifically credible investigators in both the SETI community and the broader astrophysics community assess natural explanations as substantially more probable than intelligent alien origin, for multiple independent reasons. SETI itself considers the Wow! signal unexplained but does not assert it is of artificial origin; the SETI Institute's own position, stated on their website, is "The lack of repetition means we cannot make any definitive claims about its origin."

**Pulsars: the definitive historical case.** The history of pulsar discovery is the definitive refutation of the inference from "strange and regular signal from space" to "probably alien." Jocelyn Bell Burnell, working as a graduate student with radio astronomer Antony Hewish in 1967, detected an astonishingly regular repeating radio pulse from a specific sky direction (Hewish, A., Bell, S. J., Pilkington, J. D. H., Scott, P. F., & Collins, R. A., 1968, "Observation of a Rapidly Pulsating Radio Source," *Nature*, 217, 709–713). The signal was so precisely periodic — pulses every 1.33 seconds with extraordinary regularity — that the research group informally designated it LGM-1 (Little Green Men 1) while considering whether an artificial origin needed to be seriously entertained. The signal was more regular than any known natural astrophysical process at the time. It was, however, a rotating neutron star — a pulsar. Pulsars are now understood to be entirely natural phenomena, and over 3,000 have been catalogued. The regularity that initially suggested artificial origin turned out to be characteristic of an extreme natural object.

The pulsar case illustrates a principle that is essential to evaluating all subsequent strange-signal claims: the absence of a known natural explanation at the time of detection is not evidence for an artificial explanation. Astrophysics regularly encounters phenomena that are initially inexplicable on the basis of then-current models and subsequently explained by natural processes. The prior probability of a given signal having a natural explanation, based on the complete historical record of anomalous astronomical detections, is extremely high.

**The BLC1 case: the mundane outcome.** The 2019 Breakthrough Listen signal designated BLC1 provides a cautionary case from the recent literature. A signal at 982 MHz appeared to originate from the direction of Proxima Centauri — the closest stellar system to the Sun — and had characteristics (narrowband, Doppler shifted) consistent with artificial origin from a moving transmitter. SETI researchers stated in 2020 that it warranted serious investigation. Media coverage immediately invoked the alien possibility extensively. By 2021, analysis had identified the signal as almost certainly terrestrial radio frequency interference — human-made signals reflected or otherwise detected at incorrect apparent sky positions. The natural (in this case, artificial-but-human-made) explanation prevailed, as it has in every previous case. Price, D. C. et al. (2021, "A search for extraterrestrial intelligence (SETI) with the MeerKAT radio telescope," *Nature Astronomy*) and subsequent analyses by the Breakthrough Listen group confirmed the TRFI origin.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The intentionality detection mechanism and the universe as evidence space.** The most specific and claim-relevant cognitive mechanism driving the intuitive appeal of the extraterrestrial interpretation is what cognitive scientists of religion have termed *hyperactive agency detection* — the systematic tendency to perceive purposeful intention behind unusual events, particularly events that appear regular, targeted, or directed. Barrett, J. L. (2000, "Exploring the natural foundations of religion," *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 4(1), 29–34) established that humans routinely over-attribute agency to explain environmental events — we are cognitively calibrated to assume hidden agents rather than miss real ones, because missing a real agent (predator, enemy, competitor) was historically much more costly than false positives. In the context of an anomalous signal from space, this tendency manifests as an immediate pull toward the interpretation "something sent this" rather than "something natural produced this." The universe is an extremely large space filled with enormous numbers of extreme physical processes; but the human mind, evolved in savannah environments where unexplained events usually had biological or social causes, does not have an intuitive handle on the scale of natural astrophysical creativity.

**The Fermi Paradox as a credence amplifier.** The Fermi Paradox — the observation that given the apparent age, size, and prevalence of conditions suitable for life in the universe, we would expect to have detected evidence of extraterrestrial intelligence, and yet we have not — is a genuine puzzle in astrobiology that keeps the question of alien civilisations epistemically open in a way that makes alien signal explanations seem more plausible than they might otherwise be. If the universe is probably full of civilisations (as some Drake equation estimates suggest), then any anomalous signal represents a possible instance of something that statistical reasoning says should exist somewhere. This reasoning is formally valid but misleads in practical application: even if extraterrestrial intelligence is probable in the universe at large, it does not follow that each specific anomalous radio signal is more likely to be artificial than to have the natural astrophysical explanation that every previously investigated signal has turned out to have.

**Media incentive structures and the "mysterious" template.** The strange-signals-are-probably-alien pattern recurs with such regularity in science journalism because it follows a highly effective media template: a genuine scientific discovery (strange signal detected), a dramatic possible interpretation (could be aliens), dramatic scientists hedging without full denial ("we cannot rule it out"), and an open ending (it isn't confirmed either way). This template generates engagement, shares, and return visits in a way that "scientists find new magnetar emission pattern consistent with existing models" does not. The template is particularly durable because it is never falsified in the public eye — stories rarely follow up with "turned out to be a magnetar" with the same prominence as the original "could be aliens" coverage. The absence of the falsification cycle means each new signal can be presented as fresh mystery.

**The "unexplained" misdirection.** Scientific uncertainty and practical human inability to explain something are both linguistically described as "unexplained," but they have very different epistemological statuses. When scientists say an FRB is "unexplained," they mean that among several plausible natural astrophysical hypotheses under active investigation, none has yet been definitively confirmed as the mechanism for this specific type of event. Popular media translates this as "mysterious" in a sense that implies equal probability across all explanatory possibilities including alien intelligence. This translation from scientific-uncertainty-among-natural-hypotheses to lay-mystery-implying-alien-equal-probability is the specific rhetorical operation that drives the claim pattern. Understanding why "unexplained by current models" is not the same as "equally likely to be alien" is one of the more nuanced epistemological distinctions science communication must make, and it rarely makes it.


## 4. Verdict

**Epistemologically loaded — the claim exploits ambiguity and conflates distinct senses of "unexplained" and "extraterrestrial."** The claim that strange radio signals from space are "probably extraterrestrial" in the sense of being alien in origin is not supported by the scientific evidence in any investigated case. Every anomalous radio signal for which a confirmed explanation exists has turned out to have a natural astrophysical origin (pulsars, magnetars, terrestrial interference). The major unresolved cases — most prominently the Wow! signal — remain genuinely unexplained, but the scientific community's assessment of the most probable explanations involves natural astrophysical processes rather than intelligent alien transmission. No credentialled SETI researcher, even among those actively searching for alien signals, currently claims that the evidence "probably" points to artificial alien origin for any specific unresolved signal detection.

The claim is epistemologically loaded rather than straightforwardly false because it relies on slides between legitimate senses of "extraterrestrial" (from space, vs. from aliens), illegitimate inferences from "unexplained" to "probably alien," and a genuine uncertainty — the non-zero probability of extraterrestrial intelligence somewhere in the universe — that is epistemically real but does not specifically apply to particular strange signals. The effect is to make claims that sound scientifically grounded while systematically leading audiences toward a probability assessment the evidence does not support.

The EARC classification is: **C** (contested: the claim involves genuine scientific uncertainty about some phenomena, but the popular inference to probable alien origin is consistently inversely supported by the evidence of every resolved case, making the probabilistic framing false even when the individual mysteries are real).


## 5. The Wider Picture

The strange-signals-are-probably-extraterrestrial pattern belongs to a broader category of epistemological distortion that might be called *explanatory vacuum exploitation*: the tendency to insert a dramatic explanation into the space created by a genuine unknown. This pattern is not unique to SETI-adjacent claims — it appears in claims about consciousness and quantum mechanics, in speculative history about ancient monuments, and in any domain where a genuine gap in current knowledge creates conceptual space for preferred explanations.

**The prior probability problem.** Bayesian reasoning about the alien signal hypothesis requires estimating the prior probability of alien intelligence being the source of a given anomalous radio signal. Given the complete history of investigated anomalous astronomical radio detections — all of which have had natural explanations — a rational Bayesian update on each new detection should begin with an extremely low prior probability for alien origin. Each new investigation that also turns out to have a natural explanation updates the prior further downward. The media framing treats each new detection as a fresh epistemic slate, with alien origin as a serious default consideration. Properly calibrated Bayesian reasoning treats each new detection as another instance of a well-established pattern whose resolution is overwhelmingly likely to be natural.

**What legitimate SETI research actually looks like.** SETI research is a legitimate and scientifically methodologically careful field that explicitly does not make the claims popular media attribute to it. The Breakthrough Listen initiative, the most well-funded SETI effort in history, uses systematic targeted searches of nearby stellar systems with defined parameters for what would constitute a compelling artificial-signal candidate (narrowband, Doppler-shifted, from no known astrophysical source, and repeatable from the target direction). The finding criteria are designed to be conservative, and Breakthrough Listen researchers have explicitly stated that they have not yet found a compelling candidate. The SETI Institute's position on anomalous signals is consistently cautious: anomalies warrant follow-up, but follow-up and multiple confirmations are required before claims about origin can be made. Professional SETI researchers and the popular coverage of SETI claims exist in considerable tension, and SETI scientists regularly issue corrections to media reporting that claims more than the evidence supports.

**The informational ecology of unexplained cosmic phenomena.** The cultural function of "probably alien" space signal claims is in part to maintain the sense of a universe that is alive and responsive to human curiosity — a universe where the search for other minds has genuine discoveries rather than only beautiful natural machinery. There is something genuinely profound about the possibility of other intelligent life in the universe, and the human desire to find evidence for it is not irrational. What is important from a media literacy perspective is that this desire not be satisfied by manufactured impressions of evidence that do not exist. The universe as revealed by astrophysics is genuinely extraordinary — magnetars, neutron star mergers, black hole singularities, gravitational wave events, quasars, pulsars — without any alien intelligence being required. Strange radio signals from space genuinely are evidence of an enormously complex and energetic cosmos; the distortion is in the inference to a specific personal-scale explanation that the evidence does not reach.

**Ongoing research and genuine unknowns.** As of 2026, several categories of anomalous astronomical observation remain under active investigation without definitive resolution. A small number of FRB events have characteristics that are not fully explained by the magnetar model. The long-term periodicity observed in some repeating FRBs — suggesting coherent activity on timescales of tens of days — has not been fully accounted for by available models (CHIME/FRB Collaboration, Amiri, M., Andersen, B. C., et al., 2020, "Periodic activity from a Fast Radio Burst source," *Nature*, 582, 351–355). These genuine open questions are appropriate targets for continued scientific investigation, and the intellectual honesty that acknowledges ongoing uncertainty is a mark of scientific integrity rather than evidence for alien origin. Holding genuine uncertainty open while resisting premature closure toward dramatic explanations is the epistemically correct stance.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The strange-signals-are-probably-extraterrestrial claim pattern does not typically appear in standard fact-check formats because it rarely attaches to a specific verifiable assertion in the form that fact-check pipelines are designed to process. It is structural to how science news is written rather than a discrete factual claim. Fact-checking operations occasionally address specific instances — for example, providing context around the BLC1 Proxima Centauri signal when it received extreme media attention in late 2020 — but the general pattern escapes systematic fact-check treatment because it is not a falsifiable claim but rather a rhetorical structure that exploits genuine uncertainty.

For fact-checkers and science communicators encountering coverage of anomalous astronomical signals, several practical evaluation questions are useful. First: does the headline or article distinguish between "extraterrestrial" as "from space" and "extraterrestrial" as "from alien intelligence"? Most problematic coverage does not make this distinction. Second: does the coverage report what the scientific investigators themselves have concluded, or does it import alien-origin framing that the researchers have not used? Genuine SETI researchers are typically careful; media coverage is typically not. Third: does the coverage provide historical context about the resolution of previous anomalous-signal cases — i.e., has any previous "could be aliens" signal turned out to be alien in origin? (Answer: no, not in any confirmed case.) Fourth: does the coverage explain what specific criteria, if met, would constitute evidence for artificial versus natural origin? Serious SETI research defines these criteria; popular coverage typically does not.

The broader media literacy point this case illustrates is that probability statements about extraordinary claims require extraordinary scrutiny. "Probably extraterrestrial" sounds like a calibrated probabilistic claim, but in source reporting on anomalous signals it typically means "we cannot immediately rule it out" — a statement compatible with any event, since absolute certain ruling-out of alien origin is metaphysically impossible. The difference between "we cannot rule it out with certainty" and "it is probably alien" is the difference between epistemic humility and epistemic distortion. Training audiences to notice when media coverage converts the former into the latter is among the most useful contributions science-literate journalism and media literacy organisations can make.
