# Does Stress Cause Stomach Ulcers?

## The Claim

Stomach ulcers are a disease of modern life, the folk wisdom says. The driven, overworked executive who skips meals, drinks too much coffee, and carries the weight of a company on his shoulders — he's the one who ends up doubled over in pain, his stomach lining etched by the acid of a life lived under pressure. Worry corrodes the gut the way a grinding wheel wears down metal. The ulcer is the body's invoice for a life of stress.

This story is so deeply embedded in cultural understanding — and was, for much of the twentieth century, so thoroughly endorsed by mainstream medicine — that it feels almost obviously true. It fits our intuitions about the mind-body connection, about the physical toll of psychological strain, about the kinds of people who get sick in recognizable ways. And yet the story is substantially wrong. The vast majority of peptic ulcers are not caused by stress. They are caused by a bacterium.

## The Evidence

The pivotal moment in ulcer science came not in a major research hospital or a well-funded clinical trial, but in Perth, Australia, in 1984, when a young gastroenterologist named Barry Marshall drank a Petri dish of bacteria.

Marshall and his colleague Robin Warren, a pathologist, had been studying curved gram-negative bacteria they found consistently in gastric biopsies from ulcer patients. Warren had noticed the organism — later named *Helicobacter pylori* — in the early 1980s when examining biopsies with unexplained gastric inflammation; the bacteria appeared repeatedly in tissue that showed the histological hallmarks of gastritis. Marshall became convinced that *H. pylori* was not merely associated with ulcers but was their cause. Their collaborative work resulted in papers submitted to the Gastroenterological Society of Australia in 1983 — abstracts that were rejected as uninteresting or implausible by the reviewing committee, a detail that has since become a standard example in science communication about the resistance of consensus to disconfirming evidence.

The resistance they encountered was rooted in what seemed like bedrock physiology. The stomach, with its hydrochloric acid maintaining a pH often below 2, was considered essentially sterile — an environment too hostile for bacterial colonization. Attributing disease to bacteria in such conditions seemed to violate fundamental principles. The stress-acid model of ulcers, developed over decades of careful physiological research by figures including Ivan Pavlov (who showed that psychological conditioning affected gastric secretion) and Hans Selye (who pioneered stress physiology), carried the weight of a sophisticated and well-documented paradigm.

Unable to conduct a formal human trial quickly enough through standard channels, Marshall made a dramatic decision. He cultured *H. pylori* from a patient, confirmed by endoscopy that he had no preexisting gastritis, drank a broth containing approximately one billion bacterial cells, and waited. Within three to five days he developed nausea, halitosis, and a feeling of fullness. Endoscopy confirmed active gastritis. He self-treated with tinidazole, an antibiotic, and recovered. The experiment was not double-blind, not controlled, and involved a sample size of one. But it was — for Marshall at least, and eventually for others — the kind of direct proof that accumulates weight: a healthy person deliberately colonized with the bacterium and developing gastric pathology in response.

The scientific community followed, slowly and then rapidly. By the late 1980s and early 1990s, epidemiological and clinical evidence was accumulating in multiple countries. *H. pylori* infection was found in 70–90% of patients with duodenal ulcers and 60–80% of those with gastric ulcers. The critical clinical test came with antibiotic eradication studies: patients treated with antibiotics to clear the infection had dramatically lower ulcer recurrence rates than those treated with acid suppressants alone. Standard treatment for peptic ulcer disease at the time — H2 receptor blockers like cimetidine, which reduced acid secretion and allowed ulcers to heal — produced healing followed by near-certain recurrence when treatment stopped, because the underlying infection was not addressed. Antibiotic eradication changed this: patients who cleared the infection remained ulcer-free. The disease that had been treated as a chronic condition requiring lifelong management could, in most cases, be cured.

The field consolidated rapidly through the mid-1990s. In 1994, the U.S. National Institutes of Health convened a consensus panel that concluded *H. pylori* eradication should be the primary treatment for peptic ulcer disease. In 2005, Marshall and Warren were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, with the citation explicitly noting the "tenacity and a spirit of intellectual daring" required to challenge the medical orthodoxy of the time.

## The Two Remaining Causes

The *H. pylori* revolution did not establish that stress plays no role in gastrointestinal disease — that would overcorrect. It established that the vast majority of peptic ulcers have an identifiable, treatable microbial cause. Several important qualifications follow.

**NSAIDs are the second major cause of peptic ulcers.** Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs — aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen, and related compounds — cause ulcers through a distinct mechanism: they inhibit prostaglandin synthesis (specifically via COX-1 inhibition), and prostaglandins normally help maintain the gastric mucosal barrier. Long-term NSAID use, particularly in older patients, carries substantial ulcer risk. This is why modern NSAID prescribing often includes concurrent PPI (proton pump inhibitor) therapy for gastric protection, and why COX-2 selective NSAIDs were developed (with the intention of sparing the gastric prostaglandin pathway, though this approach had its own complications).

**Stress ulcers are a real clinical entity — but a different one.** In critically ill patients in intensive care — those with severe burns, head trauma, mechanical ventilation, sepsis, major surgery, or multi-organ failure — acute stress-related mucosal disease (SRMD) can develop. These are distinct from the peptic ulcer disease of outpatients: they arise rapidly under conditions of extreme physiological stress, involve different pathophysiology (mucosal ischemia from reduced perfusion during systemic stress responses), and require different management. Prophylaxis with PPIs or H2 blockers is standard care in ICU settings for at-risk patients. The existence of "stress ulcers" in this precise clinical sense contributed to the longevity of the general belief that stress causes ulcers — the clinical entity is real, but its relevance is confined to critically ill patients, not to people with ordinary work pressure.

**Psychological stress does interact with ulcer disease.** Stress impairs immune function through multiple pathways, potentially allowing *H. pylori* to be more virulent or to evade clearance more effectively. Stress-related changes in gastric motility, acid secretion, and mucosal blood flow are real physiological phenomena. Psychological stress may worsen symptoms and impair healing even in *H. pylori*-caused ulcers. But these interactions are modulating factors operating on top of the primary bacterial or pharmacological cause — they do not constitute an independent causal pathway sufficient to produce ulcer disease in the absence of *H. pylori* or NSAID exposure in the vast majority of cases.

## Why the Belief Lasted So Long

The stress-causes-ulcers belief had several things working in its favor: a plausible mechanism, partial truth, a self-reinforcing clinical experience, and considerable commercial support for the acid-suppression paradigm that managed the disease without curing it.

The plausible mechanism was genuine. The autonomic nervous system demonstrably affects gastric physiology. Pavlov's classic experiments on salivary conditioning extended to gastric secretion — conditioned stimuli could provoke acid production. The relationship between emotional states and gut responses is part of ordinary experience; people feel stress "in the stomach," develop nausea under anxiety, experience appetite changes with depression. The framework of psychosomatic medicine, developed in the early-to-mid twentieth century by figures like Franz Alexander, applied this insight to peptic ulcers specifically, identifying a psychological profile — the orally fixated individual with dependency conflicts — supposedly characteristic of ulcer patients. This was serious medicine at the time, not quackery, and it contributed to the legitimate effort to take psychological factors in health seriously.

The partial truth was substantial. Researchers studying ulcer patients found high rates of stress, anxiety, and life event disruption — findings that seemed to confirm the hypothesis. What they could not disentangle, in the absence of the bacterial explanation, was whether these associations reflected causation, confounding, or the effects of the disease itself on psychological state. Sick people are stressed; ulcer pain is debilitating; the correlation between stress and ulcers in population samples was real even if the mechanism was misidentified.

The commercial structure of ulcer treatment created institutional inertia. H2 blockers (cimetidine, launched in 1977 as Tagamet, was the first blockbuster drug in pharmaceutical history) and later PPIs were highly profitable and genuinely effective at symptom management. They reduced acid, allowed ulcers to heal, and improved patients' quality of life substantially. The fact that ulcers recurred when treatment stopped was treated as a feature of the disease — chronic acid hypersecretion requiring maintenance therapy — rather than as evidence that the disease's actual cause was not being addressed. Drug companies had limited incentive to promote antibiotic treatments, which were cheap, potentially one-time, and not under their patent protection in the same way.

The medical training pipeline sustained the old model through simple institutional lag. Physicians trained in the 1970s and 1980s were trained on the stress-acid model; their clinical experience and their teaching reflected it. Updating clinical practice requires not just changing journal content but changing the mental models of practitioners who trained before the change, changing the curriculum taught in medical schools, and propagating the update through the informal networks by which clinical knowledge actually spreads. This process takes years, sometimes decades, even when the evidence is clear.

## The Aftermath: A Changed Standard of Care

The transformation of ulcer treatment was among the clearest improvements in clinical outcomes in late-twentieth-century medicine. The combination of *H. pylori* eradication and acid suppression — now standardized as "triple therapy" (typically two antibiotics plus a PPI, or "quadruple therapy" with bismuth added in regions of higher antibiotic resistance) — produces cure rates above 85% in most populations when patients complete treatment. A disease that had required lifelong management with daily medication, with near-certain relapse, became in most cases definitively curable.

Eradication rates vary by antibiotic regimen, local resistance patterns, and patient adherence, and the problem of antibiotic resistance in *H. pylori* is growing. In parts of the world where clarithromycin resistance is high, first-line triple therapy has lower success rates, and more complex regimens are required. But the fundamental principle — that the disease has an infectious cause and that curing the infection cures the disease — has been so thoroughly validated that it now seems almost obvious.

What changed along the way was not just the treatment but the entire conceptual category. "Peptic ulcer disease" as a stress-related chronic condition requiring lifelong acid suppression has been replaced by "peptic ulcer disease" as either an infectious disease (treat with antibiotics and acid suppression, cure) or a medication side effect (address the NSAID exposure, use gastroprotection). These are categorically different framings with categorically different clinical implications.

## What the Misconception Still Does

Despite four decades of evidence and clinical transformation, the stress-ulcer connection retains a foothold in popular understanding. Several mechanisms sustain it.

The experiential correlation remains real. People with demanding lives often have other ulcer risk factors — they may take NSAIDs more frequently for stress-related headaches and musculoskeletal pain, drink more alcohol, smoke, eat irregularly, and — critically — they carry higher *H. pylori* infection rates in some population studies, possibly because of early-life exposure patterns. The association between stress and ulcer experience in everyday life is genuine even if the causal mechanism runs through these cofactors rather than through stress directly corroding the stomach.

The narrative is emotionally coherent in a way that the bacterial explanation is not. Saying "you have an ulcer because you work too hard and worry too much" implies something about the person's relationship to their own life, their values and vulnerabilities. It is intimate. Saying "you have an ulcer because you've been colonized by a spiral-shaped bacterium that you probably picked up in childhood" is more clinically accurate and also more random, more out of the person's control. There is no obvious moral to draw from bacterial colonization; there is a clear moral to draw from stress — slow down, worry less, take care of yourself. Even if the causal story is wrong, the recommendation might not be bad advice.

Health communication moves slowly. Articles, advice columns, popular medical guides, and even some outdated clinical resources continue to list stress as a primary ulcer cause. The stress-ulcer link appears in introductory textbooks written before the field updated, and those textbooks remain in use. The cultural shorthand is deeply established.

## The Marshall Lesson

Barry Marshall's story has become a touchstone in science communication and philosophy of science precisely because it illustrates, in unusually clean form, several recurring patterns in how scientific change happens. A hypothesis that violated the prior consensus — bacteria cannot survive in the stomach — met initial rejection not through careful evaluation of evidence but through prior plausibility judgments. The researchers who might have investigated it were discouraged before they began. The self-experiment, whatever its methodological limitations, was a response to a situation where standard channels were closed: a researcher so convinced by his data that he found a more direct path to proof.

The resistance was not irrational. Scientific consensus is a reasonable prior, and most challenges to consensus are wrong. Requiring high evidence before accepting revolutionary claims is appropriate; the history of medicine also includes many cases where premature acceptance of implausible-seeming hypotheses led to harm. The problem is not that Marshall's peers were skeptical — skepticism was appropriate — but that the skepticism was sustained longer than the evidence warranted, in part because of the institutional and commercial forces that benefited from the existing model.

Stress and health are genuinely, deeply connected. The nervous system communicates extensively with the gut; the gut has its own distributed neural network, the enteric nervous system, sometimes called the "second brain." Psychological state affects gastrointestinal function in ways that are real, measurable, and clinically significant. But that genuine connection does not support the specific claim that ordinary psychological stress causes peptic ulcers. Getting the mechanism right matters, not just for scientific tidiness but because the mechanism determines the treatment. The person with an *H. pylori* ulcer needs antibiotics, not a vacation — though the vacation might not hurt, and would likely help with the stress-mediated factors that can worsen symptoms and slow healing.
