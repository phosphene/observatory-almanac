# Are Stretch Marks Caused by Weight Gain?

## The Claim

Stretch marks are, in the popular imagination, a mark of excess — the skin's record of having been stretched too far by too much weight. They appear in the taxonomy of body concerns alongside cellulite and fat deposits, grouped with the physical features that culture treats as evidence of something gone wrong, something that needs to be managed, minimized, corrected, or at minimum, concealed. The implicit logic embedded in the way stretch marks are discussed — in beauty media, in health advice columns, in the marketing of creams and oils and laser treatments — is that they record a body that gained weight it perhaps shouldn't have, or that let itself go.

This understanding has the mechanics partly right and the framing substantially wrong. Stretch marks do form when skin expands rapidly — weight gain can cause this, and does in some people. But weight gain is one cause among several that are equally biological and considerably less stigmatized, and the cultural focus on weight gain as the explanation distorts how people understand something that is, by any honest epidemiological measure, a feature of most human bodies.

## The Evidence

Stretch marks — medically termed striae distensae, from the Latin for "stretched" marks — form when the skin is required to expand faster than its connective tissue can adapt without injury. The skin's dermis contains a scaffolding of collagen fibers (which provide tensile strength and structure) and elastin fibers (which provide flexibility and recoil). This scaffold normally allows the skin to expand and contract considerably over the course of a lifetime. But when growth or expansion is rapid enough — when the skin surface must increase its area over days or weeks rather than months or years — the scaffold tears. The tears produce a scar-like structure within the dermis itself.

The visible result evolves in two recognizable phases. Initially, striae appear as raised, elongated marks that are red, pink, purple, or sometimes brown, depending on the person's underlying skin tone. These are called striae rubrae. They reflect active inflammation and increased vascular activity — the dermis is responding to the injury of the torn connective tissue. Over months to years, as inflammation resolves and the vascular supply to the marks diminishes, they fade and flatten into silvery, white, or lighter-than-surrounding-skin linear marks: striae albae. At this stage they are cosmetically less prominent but represent mature scar tissue within the dermis, and they are permanent in the sense that the structural change to the dermis does not reverse.

This mechanical process — rapid expansion tears connective tissue — is triggered by any sufficiently rapid tissue expansion, regardless of its cause. The contexts in which this commonly occurs span the full range of normal human life:

**Puberty** is among the most common. The adolescent growth spurt affects multiple dimensions: height increases rapidly, muscle mass develops, and in girls especially, the hips, breasts, and thighs change dramatically in shape and size over months. Studies of adolescent populations have consistently found stretch marks in the majority of participants when systematically examined. They appear on the thighs, buttocks, lower back, hips, upper arms, and in girls, the breasts, abdomen, and lateral hips. These are not marks of weight gain — they are marks of a body growing, doing precisely what adolescent bodies are supposed to do. The connective tissue simply cannot always keep pace with growth rates that are, by evolutionary design, quite rapid.

**Pregnancy** is so strongly associated with stretch marks that a specific medical term, striae gravidarum, exists for the form they take during gestation. The uterus expands enormously in the third trimester; the abdominal skin stretches over timeframes of weeks; the breasts grow rapidly as they prepare for lactation. Rates of striae gravidarum in published studies range from 50% to 90% of pregnant women depending on population and methodology, with most estimates in the 70–80% range. Genetic factors are among the strongest predictors — if a mother or sister developed stretch marks during pregnancy, the probability is substantially increased. Hormonal factors also play a role: the elevated glucocorticoid levels of pregnancy reduce the synthesis and cross-linking of collagen, making the skin more vulnerable to tearing under mechanical stress. Stretch marks are essentially universal accompaniments to pregnancy.

**Rapid muscle gain** in athletes and bodybuilders produces striae in exactly the areas of greatest growth — typically the shoulders, upper arms, pectoral region, and thighs. These are documented in dermatological literature and familiar to anyone who has trained intensively for strength or mass. They record the same mechanical process as the marks of a pubescent teenager or a pregnant woman: tissue expanding faster than the connective scaffolding can follow. Competitive bodybuilders sometimes display dramatic striae as a consequence of the speed and scale of their muscular development.

**Cushing syndrome** — a condition of cortisol excess, whether from a tumor, adrenal dysfunction, or exogenous corticosteroid therapy — produces a distinctive form of striae that are often wide, deep purple, and more prominent than typical growth-related marks. The mechanism is direct: cortisol directly inhibits the synthesis of collagen and elastin in the dermis, reducing the skin's tensile strength and making it far more vulnerable to tearing under any expansion. Prolonged systemic corticosteroid treatment (prednisone, dexamethasone) for conditions like asthma, rheumatoid arthritis, or inflammatory bowel disease can produce similar effects. In the clinical evaluation of unexpected prominent striae, corticosteroid exposure is routinely investigated as a cause.

Weight gain does produce stretch marks, through the same fundamental mechanism — fat deposition expands the skin surface — but it is one context among many, and not the most common one.

## The Epidemiology: Nearly Universal

When systematically examined across unselected adult populations, stretch marks are extraordinarily common. A 2015 review in the *British Journal of Dermatology* examined available prevalence data and found that estimates across studies ranged from roughly 50% to over 80% of adults at some point in their lives, depending on the population studied and how carefully the skin was examined. For adolescents during or shortly after puberty, some estimates approach 80–90% when examining the areas most commonly affected.

This is not a minority condition. Stretch marks are not an anomaly. They are a variant feature of human skin that is present in the majority of adults and in most people who have been pregnant or gone through a substantial adolescent growth spurt. They are, in any honest accounting, a normal feature of the human body — as common as wrinkles, far more common than many conditions that attract far less cultural attention.

They cause no medical problems. They do not affect skin function, barrier integrity, or susceptibility to infection. They are not associated with any adverse health outcomes. They are not symptoms of disease in the overwhelming majority of cases. The exception — the context in which new or worsening striae may warrant clinical attention — is unexplained rapid weight gain or the distinctive wide, purple striae that may signal hypercortisolism. But even these are diagnostic clues to an underlying condition, not harmful in themselves.

## The Treatment Industry

A multi-billion-dollar global industry has built itself around "treating" and "preventing" stretch marks — a category that, to be blunt, has a poor evidence base by any standard of clinical research.

Topical preventive treatments are most studied in the context of pregnancy, where the concern is substantial, the population is large, and the motivation to intervene is high. Multiple randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews have examined creams and oils containing ingredients like cocoa butter, shea butter, vitamin E, hyaluronic acid, and centella asiatica extract. The overall finding: none have demonstrated consistent preventive efficacy compared to plain emollients or placebo. The skin's tendency to develop striae during pregnancy is driven by the rate of fetal growth, the individual's genetic predisposition, and the hormonal environment — none of which topical creams can address. Some studies show trends toward benefit, some show no effect, and the evidence does not support specific topical recommendations for prevention.

Treatment of existing stretch marks — particularly striae albae, the mature silvery marks — is modestly more amenable to intervention. Pulsed dye laser and fractional CO2 laser treatments show improvements in appearance in randomized trials, reducing the visibility of marks by improving texture and pigmentation. Microneedling (collagen induction therapy), radiofrequency devices, chemical peels with trichloroacetic acid, and combination approaches show varying degrees of efficacy. The key limitation is that all these approaches improve appearance — reduce the contrast, improve texture, stimulate some collagen remodeling — without eliminating the marks. None produce the dramatic "removal" that marketing sometimes implies. Results also vary considerably by skin type, mark maturity, and treatment intensity.

The commercial interest in treating stretch marks would not be a problem if the marketing were accurate about what treatments can achieve and for whom. The problem is that the marketing tends to frame the problem as a correctable defect, which requires first establishing that the marks are a defect — and this framing feeds directly into the stigma that attaches to a near-universal human skin feature.

## The Stigma Problem

The cultural association between stretch marks and weight gain does not merely mislabel a biological cause. It does something more actively damaging: it places a normal and common feature of the human body into a shame category associated with excess, lack of discipline, or failure to manage one's body appropriately.

Consider who has stretch marks in the cultural framing: someone who gained weight they perhaps shouldn't have, whose body has recorded the evidence of that excess. Now consider who actually has stretch marks: most adolescents going through puberty, the vast majority of pregnant women, strength athletes and bodybuilders, anyone who grew quickly in their teenage years, and a majority of adults of any body type who look closely enough at their skin. The stigma targets the majority while nominally describing something negative.

The adolescent context is particularly significant. Teenagers going through puberty — growing, developing, doing exactly what biology requires of them — may find themselves with stretch marks on their thighs or hips or upper arms and receive the cultural message that these are marks of weight gain, implying that the growth of their pubescent body was somehow excessive or ill-managed. This is medically wrong and psychologically harmful. Body image research consistently identifies stretch marks among the most commonly cited sources of appearance-related distress in adolescents, particularly girls. The distress is substantially produced by the cultural framing, not by the marks themselves.

The pregnancy context is equally telling. The body of a woman who has grown a human being — who has undergone one of the most physiologically demanding things a human body does — is expected to display remorse for the evidence that process left. The booming market in products for eliminating "pregnancy striae," for "bouncing back," for restoring the "pre-baby body," implicitly frames pregnancy as something the body needs to recover from and erase. The stretch marks that nearly every pregnant woman develops are transformed from physical records of a profound bodily experience into problems to be corrected.

## Genetic and Physiological Predictors

Understanding what actually determines who develops stretch marks — and how severely — is useful both because it's scientifically interesting and because it replaces the weight-gain narrative with a more accurate account.

Genetic predisposition is the strongest identifiable predictor. Family history of stretch marks — whether parents or siblings developed them — is consistently associated with higher rates in multiple studies. This reflects the inheritance of skin structural characteristics: the relative composition of collagen subtypes in the dermis, the mechanical properties of elastin, the baseline tensile strength and extensibility of the skin. These are not factors under behavioral control; they are inherited along with other physical characteristics.

The speed of growth or expansion matters more than the total magnitude. The same final body size, reached slowly over years, is less likely to produce striae than the same size reached rapidly over months. This is why puberty, with its episodic and sometimes rapid growth spurts, is such a common context for striae — it's not the size of the adolescent body but the speed at which that body grows.

Hormonal environment, particularly the ratio of glucocorticoids to androgens, influences dermal tensile strength. Pregnancy elevates glucocorticoids; pubertal hormones have complex effects on skin; these factors interact with genetic predisposition to determine individual outcomes.

Skin color and type affect the visibility of striae more than their formation. Darker skin tones may show striae more prominently in the rubrae phase, when the contrast between the inflamed marks and surrounding skin is high; in the albae phase, the white marks may be more or less visible against different underlying tones. What looks dramatically prominent in one skin tone may be nearly invisible in another — meaning that the apparent prevalence of stretch marks in populations partly reflects ease of detection, not true differences in formation rates.

## A More Accurate Frame

Stretch marks are a record that a body changed. They appear wherever growth has been rapid enough that the dermis could not keep pace: the hips of an adolescent in a growth spurt, the abdomen of a woman who grew a child, the shoulders of a strength athlete pushing their limits, the thighs of a teenager whose body matured quickly. They are common enough to be considered a normal variant of human skin. They cause no medical problems. They fade over time. They require no treatment, though treatment is available for those who want it and should be described accurately rather than as "removal."

The weight-gain framing is not entirely false — weight gain can cause stretch marks, through the same mechanism as any other rapid expansion. But framing weight gain as the primary explanation for stretch marks attaches a shame narrative to what is, by any honest analysis, an ordinary feature of the human body's relationship to growth. The shame is manufactured. The marks are not a failure. They are the dermis recording, in the only way it can, that the body grew.

A teenager whose hips have developed marks during puberty, a new mother whose abdomen bears the striae of pregnancy, a young man whose shoulders carry the marks of his first year of serious training — all of these are normal. They are common. They are not evidence of something gone wrong. They are evidence of a body that was alive and changed.
