# Stretching before exercise prevents injuries and muscle soreness

## 1. The Claim

Physical education classes, sports coaches, athletic trainers, and fitness instructors across generations have transmitted a near-universal preparatory ritual: before engaging in vigorous physical activity, one should stretch. The practice carries an intuitive logic—muscles are like rubber bands or spring-loaded mechanisms, and a cold, tight rubber band snaps while a warmed, elongated one stretches. The specific form this ritual most commonly takes is static stretching: reaching for a fixed position, holding it for 15-30 seconds, releasing, and repeating. Before running, you hold a hamstring stretch. Before lifting weights, you reach across your chest to stretch your shoulders. Before a soccer match, you sit on the ground and reach for your feet.

The claim embedded in this ritual is threefold: stretching before exercise reduces injury risk, reduces the muscle soreness that follows intense activity (delayed-onset muscle soreness, or DOMS), and generally prepares the body for the demands about to be placed on it. These claims are often treated as settled, obvious, and so thoroughly established that questioning them seems like denying gravity. The stretching ritual is not merely popular; it is institutional. Athletic codes of conduct require it. Parents insist on it for young athletes. And the intuitive model that underlies it—a warmed, elongated muscle is a safer, more functional muscle—continues to dominate physical culture even as exercise science has reached substantially more complicated conclusions.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

The evidence on pre-exercise static stretching is one of the more striking cases of scientific consensus contradicting widespread practice and intuition. The key findings can be stated clearly:

**Static stretching does not prevent injury.** The most rigorous systematic review of stretching and injury prevention, conducted by Herbert and Gabriel and published in the *British Medical Journal* in 2002 (later updated as a Cochrane review by Herbert, de Noronha, and Kamper in 2011), found no evidence that pre-exercise stretching reduces injury risk. The authors pooled data from randomized controlled trials and concluded that any effect of stretching on injury risk was small and statistically indistinguishable from zero. Studies examining specific injury types—muscle strains, joint injuries, overuse injuries—generally fail to show protective effects of pre-exercise static stretching.

**Static stretching does not prevent muscle soreness.** Herbert and Gabriel's original 2002 review also examined the effect of stretching on delayed-onset muscle soreness and found that the evidence did not support a protective effect. This finding has been replicated in subsequent reviews. The physiological mechanism of DOMS—involving micro-damage to muscle fibers, inflammatory response, and the repair processes that follow—is not disrupted by pre- or post-exercise stretching to a clinically meaningful degree. People who stretch before exercise get as sore afterward as those who do not.

**Static stretching before activity may impair performance.** This finding has surprised many in the sports science community. Multiple studies have shown that acute static stretching, particularly when held for longer durations (>60 seconds), reduces muscle strength, power output, and sprint speed in the short term following stretching. A meta-analysis by Behm and Chaouachi (2011) in the *Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism* found that static stretching decreased strength by approximately 5-8 percent in the period immediately following stretching. The effect is larger with longer holds and more muscle groups stretched. The mechanism is debated but may involve neural inhibition, reduced stiffness of the musculo-tendinous unit, and decreased motor neuron excitability. For explosive or power-dependent activities, static stretching immediately before competition or training may be counterproductive.

**Dynamic warm-ups and sport-specific preparation do reduce injury risk.** This is where the directionality of the popular belief captures something real even as the specific practice is wrong. Physical preparation before vigorous activity does matter. What works is not static stretching but dynamic warm-up: graduated movement that progressively increases heart rate, body temperature, neuromuscular activation, and joint range of motion through activity-relevant movements rather than static holds. Research by McGowan, Pyne, Thompson, and Rattray (2015) in *Sports Medicine* documented the benefits of dynamic warm-up protocols for both performance and injury preparation. The FIFA 11+ warm-up protocol—a structured dynamic warm-up program for soccer—has shown substantial reductions in injury rates in randomized trials across multiple populations.

**Stretching improves flexibility, which has limited direct injury prevention benefits except in specific contexts.** Pre-exercise stretching does produce the effect it is mechanistically designed to produce: increased range of motion and flexibility during the period following stretching. This increase in range of motion is not neutral in all contexts. For activities with high flexibility demands—gymnastics, dance, martial arts, certain swimming events—adequate joint range of motion reduces the likelihood that the activity pushes beyond the physiological limit, potentially preventing specific types of injury. In these contexts, stretching before activity serves a purpose. The problem is that this specific and limited application has been universalized into a general-purpose injury prevention strategy applied to activities (running, strength training, team sports) where the limiting factor for injury is not range of motion flexibility.

**Post-exercise stretching for recovery: also weak evidence.** The belief in stretching extends beyond pre-exercise ritual to post-exercise recovery. Evidence for stretching-induced recovery benefits, including reduced soreness, reduced muscle fatigue, and faster return to performance capacity, is also weak. A Cochrane review on muscle soreness found that post-exercise stretching did not reduce DOMS or produce clinically meaningful recovery benefits.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The stretching-before-exercise belief is one of the more instructive examples of a practice that persists through institutional transmission, intuitive plausibility, and confounded personal experience rather than through evidence.

**The intuitive mechanical model.** Human beings tend to understand muscles through a mechanical analogy: cold rubber bands snap; warmed, elongated ones stretch safely. This model has surface validity. Cold temperatures do reduce tissue extensibility and mechanical resilience at the level of individual fibers and connective tissue. But the physiological reality is more complex: a dynamic warm-up increases muscle temperature and metabolic readiness through movement, not through elongation. The mechanical intuition correctly identifies temperature as relevant but misidentifies static stretching as the appropriate way to address it.

**Confounded personal experience.** Athletes who stretch before activity and avoid injury attribute their injury-free status partly to stretching. Athletes who skip stretching and sustain injuries blame the omission. This pattern of causal attribution is nearly impossible to correct through personal experience because the base rates are never visible: we don't see the counterfactual world in which we stretched but still got injured, or didn't stretch and remained uninjured. The noise of individual experience cannot distinguish a true protective effect from zero effect.

**Institutional transmission in physical education.** The stretching ritual has been taught in physical education programs for generations, transmitted through coach-to-athlete, teacher-to-student chains that gave it the authority of received wisdom. Professional athletic trainers and coaches learned to teach stretching as foundational practice. Questioning it required confronting deeply held professional beliefs. The exercise science community began generating contradictory evidence in the 1990s and early 2000s, but updating athletic culture's deeply institutionalized practices has been slow. Habits formed in early childhood physical education persist through adult athletic life.

**Confirmation from other cultures and traditional practices.** Stretching and flexibility preparation before vigorous activity appears in many athletic traditions and martial arts forms, lending it cross-cultural authority that can make the practice seem like universal human wisdom. These traditions predate modern exercise physiology, however, and their practices were developed for different reasons (including ritual, spiritual preparation, skill development) and different activity contexts than the claim about injury prevention.

**The partial truth in the related claim.** The intuition that *some form of preparation* reduces injury risk is correct. Active dynamic warm-up does reduce injury risk. When people attribute their post-warm-up injury-free status to their warm-up routine, part of their experience may be real—but the causal mechanism is the dynamic movement, not the static stretching component. Because the two are often packaged together, it is difficult to disentangle which part of the preparatory routine is producing which effect.

**Medical and coaching endorsement.** For much of the 20th century, sports medicine professionals and coaches recommended pre-exercise stretching as injury prevention. This professional consensus formed the backdrop against which personal experience was interpreted. When the scientific literature began challenging this consensus, it was difficult for the shift to propagate quickly through the many transmission channels—coach certification programs, physical education curricula, athletic codes of conduct, fitness culture—through which stretching advice reaches practitioners.

## 4. Verdict

**DIRECTIONALLY TRUE, SPECIFICALLY FALSE.** Physical preparation before vigorous exercise does reduce injury risk—but the specific practice of static stretching is not the mechanism. Static stretching before exercise does not prevent injury or muscle soreness according to the best available evidence (Cochrane reviews, multiple meta-analyses). For power-dependent activities, it may modestly impair performance. Dynamic warm-up and activity-specific movement preparation are the evidence-supported practices that deliver on the intuition behind pre-exercise preparation. In contexts requiring significant flexibility (gymnastics, dance, martial arts), stretching to maintain range of motion serves a legitimate purpose that can overlap with injury prevention. The broad, universal claim that pre-exercise stretching prevents injury and soreness is not supported.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The stretching evidence story is part of a broader revision in exercise science over the past three decades. Many practices that were institutionally transmitted as foundational to athletic preparation have been subjected to controlled testing and found wanting or revised. The prescription against exercising immediately after eating (not clearly supported for moderate exercise), the idea that you should "no pain, no gain" for muscle development (confounds productive fatigue with counterproductive overtraining), the belief that cooling down with light activity flushes lactic acid and prevents soreness (lactic acid clears quickly; the relation to DOMS is more complex)—these popular exercise prescriptions have all been revised or nuanced by the research literature.

What these revisions share is a gap between intuitive mechanical models of the body and the physiological reality. The body is not a simple machine that warms up like a car engine or clears waste products like a hydraulic system. The actual mechanisms underlying muscle fatigue, soreness, repair, injury, and adaptation are substantially more complex and often non-obvious. Exercise science as a rigorous empirical field has relatively recently acquired the methodological tools to run appropriately controlled studies on many of these questions—which is why some popular exercise prescriptions formed in athletic culture generations ago are only now being properly tested.

The FIFA 11+ program deserves particular attention as a model for evidence-based injury prevention. Developed by FIFA's medical research center (F-MARC), the program was tested in large-scale randomized controlled trials across professional and amateur soccer populations and showed 30-50 percent reductions in injury rates when implemented consistently. The program's components are primarily dynamic: structured running patterns, progressive jumping and landing mechanics, balance training, and controlled cutting movements. Static stretching is minimal. The success of FIFA 11+ demonstrates both that injury prevention through preparatory protocols is achievable and that the active mechanism is dynamic movement preparation rather than static flexibility work.

The persistence of static stretching culture despite contradictory evidence illustrates the general challenge of updating institutionally transmitted practices in response to research. Physical education curricula, athletic training certification programs, and sports coaching certifications all act as slow-moving institutional reservoirs that can sustain practices long after the research basis has shifted. Even in medical contexts, where clinical practice guidelines should in principle track evidence, updating entrenched practices among practitioners trained in earlier paradigms takes years to decades. The stretching case is instructive precisely because the evidence is relatively clear and the practice is so widespread: it shows how high the bar for belief revision can be even when evidence is reasonably unambiguous.

For practical purposes: if stretching feels good and improves your range of motion in ways that matter for your activities, there is no strong reason to stop—particularly if it is brief and preceded or followed by dynamic movement. The evidence against stretching is evidence against its efficacy for injury and soreness prevention specifically, not evidence that it causes harm. But if the purpose is injury prevention, the effort is better invested in a dynamic warm-up specific to the activity about to be performed.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Pre-exercise stretching as injury prevention has received some attention in health and fitness fact-checking contexts but has not been a major target for political or viral misinformation fact-checkers. The issue lives more in the domain of health journalism and exercise science communication than political fact-checking.

Major health and science journalism outlets have covered the stretching evidence. *The New York Times* Well section published an influential piece by Gretchen Reynolds in 2013, citing the Herbert and de Noronha Cochrane review and summarizing the evidence against pre-exercise stretching for injury prevention. *Runner's World* and other athletic publications have updated their recommendations over the past decade. Professional exercise science bodies, including the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM), have updated their official guidelines to recommend dynamic warm-up over static stretching before vigorous activity.

The challenge for communicators on this topic is that the evidence differentiates among many variables that are difficult to convey in brief form: type of stretching (static vs. dynamic), timing (before vs. after exercise), hold duration, activity type, and what outcome is being measured (injury, performance, soreness, range of motion). A fully accurate summary necessarily has multiple conditional clauses. The popular press tends to resolve this complexity by overcorrecting into "stretching is useless" or "stretching is harmful"—framings that are less accurate than "static stretching doesn't do what most people think it does, and other forms of physical preparation work better for the goals people usually have."

Responsible sports medicine communication now typically recommends sport-specific dynamic warm-up before activity and notes that static stretching can be part of a cool-down routine or flexibility training program without overpromising on injury prevention benefits. This represents genuine progress in translating exercise science into public guidance, even as the traditional stretching ritual persists in many institutional settings where the evidence revision has not yet fully penetrated.

The stretching claim is also illustrative of a broader issue in health and fitness communication: the difficulty of nuanced updates to simple, compelling folk beliefs. The simple claim—stretch before exercise to prevent injury—is easy to transmit, memorable, and embedded in ritual. The accurate update—do a dynamic warm-up specific to your activity; static stretching doesn’t prevent injury and may reduce performance but is fine for improving flexibility in a separate context—is more accurate but far harder to transmit in the contexts where exercise advice is usually delivered. Coaches have seconds, not minutes, to communicate warm-up instructions. Physical education teachers work with large groups of students across varied activities. The simple rule, even when wrong, has transmission advantages the accurate update lacks.

This transmission asymmetry is a recurring challenge in science communication. Simple, memorable, actionable rules spread faster and persist longer than accurate but complex updates. Changing institutionally embedded exercise practices requires updating coach certification programs, physical education standards, athletic trainer guidelines, and consumer fitness media simultaneously—a coordination problem that takes years even when the evidence is clear. The stretching case shows that the research literature can produce clear findings and those findings can be poorly reflected in practice for over two decades, not because the public is uninterested in accurate information but because the institutional transmission channels have their own inertia.
