# Did University Students Develop a Compound That Kills Brain Cancer Cells?

## The Claim

In late 2021, social media posts began circulating with breathless excitement: pharmacy students at the University of Findlay in Ohio had developed a compound that "kills brain cancer cells." The posts, shared across Facebook, Twitter, and Reddit, typically featured a photograph of the student research team alongside headlines that implied — or outright stated — that these young scientists had achieved a breakthrough in the treatment of glioblastoma, one of the deadliest cancers known to medicine.

The framing was irresistible. Young, photogenic pharmacy students. A plant-derived compound. A deadly cancer. The implicit narrative was unmistakable: where the pharmaceutical establishment had failed, youthful brilliance and natural ingredients had succeeded. Some versions of the claim emphasized the "natural" origins of the compound — it was derived from chalcone, a flavoring agent found in curry and other foods — adding a layer of alternative-medicine appeal to what was, in its original form, a perfectly conventional pharmaceutical research project.

What audiences understood from these posts was simple and powerful: students had found a cure, or something very close to a cure, for brain cancer. The compound could cross the blood-brain barrier. It could kill cancer cells. The problem, if there was one, was merely a matter of scaling up — of getting the medical establishment to pay attention to what these young researchers had accomplished.

This understanding was wrong in nearly every particular.

The posts had originated from a University of Findlay press release issued in August 2019 — more than two years before the viral surge. In the intervening time, the posts had been stripped of the caveats, qualifications, and context that even the original university announcement had included. The result was a kind of informational telephone game: real research, real students, a real compound, but a fictional breakthrough.

## What's Actually True

The kernel of truth at the center of this story is genuine, and worth understanding precisely because the gap between what actually happened and what people believed happened illustrates something important about how scientific information degrades as it travels through social media.

At the University of Findlay's College of Pharmacy, a group of students working under faculty supervision identified a compound they designated RK-15. The compound is primarily composed of chalcone, a member of the flavonoid family of organic compounds. Chalcones are widely distributed in nature — they occur in fruits, vegetables, spices, and tea — and have been the subject of pharmacological interest for decades. The anticancer and anti-inflammatory properties of various chalcone derivatives have been documented in the scientific literature since at least the 1990s, across hundreds of studies by dozens of research groups worldwide. The University of Findlay students were not working in uncharted territory; they were contributing to an established and well-populated field of inquiry.

What distinguished the Findlay team's work, at least in principle, was their focus on designing a chalcone-based compound specifically engineered to cross the blood-brain barrier. The BBB is a selectively permeable membrane that separates circulating blood from the brain's extracellular fluid. It is one of the body's most elegant and frustrating defense mechanisms — elegant because it protects the brain from pathogens, toxins, and fluctuations in blood chemistry; frustrating because it also blocks approximately 98% of small-molecule drugs and virtually 100% of large-molecule therapeutics from reaching brain tissue. This is the central challenge of neuro-oncology: even when you have a compound that can kill cancer cells in a petri dish, getting that compound into the brain in therapeutic concentrations is an entirely separate — and often insurmountable — problem.

The students' ambition was real and their target was legitimate. Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most common and most aggressive malignant primary brain tumor in adults. It occurs at a rate of approximately 1 per 10,000 cases in the general population, but accounts for roughly 16% of all primary brain and central nervous system tumors. The prognosis is devastating: median survival after diagnosis is approximately 14 to 16 months with standard treatment (surgery, radiation, and temozolomide chemotherapy), and the five-year survival rate hovers around 5 to 10%. GBM is, for practical purposes, incurable with current therapies. Any genuine advance in BBB-crossing drug delivery would be enormously significant.

But here is where the story departs from the viral narrative.

At the time of the university's August 2019 announcement, the compound RK-15 had not been tested in animal models. It had not been tested in human subjects. It had not been the subject of any peer-reviewed scientific publication. The university itself described the work as being in its "infancy" and acknowledged that it would require "years' worth of study" before any conclusions could be drawn about efficacy.

In other words, what the students had accomplished was the very first step in a process that typically requires 10 to 15 years and billions of dollars to complete. They had identified a candidate compound. They had not demonstrated that it kills cancer cells in vitro (in cell cultures). They had not demonstrated that it crosses the blood-brain barrier in a living organism. They had not demonstrated that it reaches glioblastoma tumors in therapeutic concentrations. They had not demonstrated that it is safe. They had not demonstrated anything, really, beyond the design and synthesis of a molecule with theoretically promising properties.

This is not nothing. Compound identification is the necessary first step in drug development, and undergraduate research experience is valuable regardless of whether the specific project produces a clinical therapy. But the distance between "we designed a molecule" and "we developed a compound that kills brain cancer cells" is approximately the same as the distance between "I bought a lottery ticket" and "I won the lottery."

Perhaps most telling is what has happened since 2019. As of this writing — nearly seven years after the original announcement — no peer-reviewed publication describing RK-15 has appeared in the scientific literature. No preclinical trial results have been reported. No regulatory filing has been made. The compound has, to all appearances, vanished from the public record after the initial university press release. This absence is not unusual; the vast majority of early-stage research compounds never progress beyond initial identification. But the silence is informative. It suggests either that subsequent testing failed to confirm the compound's promise, that the project lost funding or personnel (students graduate, after all), or that results were insufficiently novel to merit publication. Any of these explanations is more probable than the alternative: that a genuine breakthrough in glioblastoma treatment has been successfully developed but kept from the public for seven years.

## Why People Believe This

Understanding why this particular claim went viral — and why it continues to circulate years after its original appearance — requires understanding something about the architecture of hope.

**The "young geniuses solve cancer" narrative archetype.** There is a deep and durable cultural template for this story: the brilliant young outsider who succeeds where the establishment has failed. It appears in fiction (from Good Will Hunting to every superhero origin story) and in selective readings of real scientific history (the young Watson and Crick, the college-dropout tech founders). When pharmacy students appear to crack one of oncology's hardest problems, the narrative resonance is immediate and powerful. It flatters our belief that intelligence is more important than infrastructure, that fresh eyes see what jaded experts miss, that youth and enthusiasm can overcome the grinding complexity of biological systems.

The reality of modern drug development is nearly the opposite. Breakthroughs emerge from large, well-funded teams working within institutional frameworks, building incrementally on decades of prior research, with access to animal models, clinical trial infrastructure, regulatory expertise, and manufacturing capacity. A group of undergraduates, however talented, cannot replicate this ecosystem. But that reality is less emotionally satisfying than the archetype.

**Emotional resonance of brain cancer stories.** Glioblastoma is terrifying. Unlike many cancers, which can be managed as chronic diseases for years or decades, GBM typically kills within two years of diagnosis. It robs patients of cognitive function — of their personalities, their memories, their ability to recognize loved ones — before it kills them. Everyone knows someone, or knows someone who knows someone, who has been devastated by brain cancer. The emotional stakes of a potential cure are enormous, and the desire to believe that one exists overrides skeptical evaluation of the evidence.

**The university press release pipeline.** Universities have institutional incentives to publicize their research, particularly research conducted by undergraduates, which serves recruitment and fundraising goals. University communications offices are staffed by public relations professionals, not scientists, and their job is to make research sound as compelling as possible. The result is press releases that emphasize potential and downplay limitations — not because anyone is being dishonest, but because the genre conventions of the press release reward optimism. These releases are then picked up by local news outlets, which add their own layer of simplification, and eventually reach social media, where all remaining nuance is stripped away. The pipeline from "interesting preliminary result" to "students cure cancer" requires no conspiracy, no malice, and no deliberate misinformation. It is a structural feature of how information flows from laboratories to the public.

**The two-year recirculation gap.** One of the most instructive features of this case is the temporal gap between the original announcement in August 2019 and the viral surge in late 2021. This gap reveals something about the ecology of social media misinformation: old claims get recirculated when they encounter new audiences. A post that received modest attention in 2019 can go viral in 2021 if it is shared by an account with a larger following, if it happens to coincide with a news cycle about cancer research, or simply through the algorithmic lottery of platform recommendation systems. The recirculation strips whatever temporal context the original claim possessed. The audience in 2021 did not know — and was not told — that the claim was two years old, that no progress had been reported in the interim, and that the silence itself was informative.

**The absence of follow-up as an epistemological red flag.** This is perhaps the most important lesson of the case. When a genuinely significant scientific result is obtained, what follows is a characteristic and predictable sequence: peer-reviewed publication, replication by other laboratories, expansion of the research team, increased funding, conference presentations, preclinical trials, and eventually clinical trials. The absence of this sequence is itself evidence. When a university announces a promising compound and then the story simply stops — no publications, no trials, no follow-up announcements — the most parsimonious explanation is that subsequent work failed to confirm the initial promise. But social media has no mechanism for registering absence. There is no viral post that says "That compound from 2019? Nothing ever came of it." The original claim persists in the information ecosystem, uncorrected and unupdated, available for indefinite recirculation.

**Why people want cancer to have simple solutions.** At the deepest level, the appeal of this story reflects a human need for the world to be more tractable than it is. Cancer is genuinely complex — not one disease but hundreds, each with distinct molecular biology, each requiring different therapeutic approaches. The idea that a single compound, derived from a common food ingredient, might solve one of the hardest versions of the problem is immensely comforting. It suggests that nature has already provided the answers, that the barrier to curing cancer is not biological complexity but institutional inertia or corporate greed. This framing is emotionally satisfying and politically convenient. It is also, in nearly every instance, wrong.

## Verdict

The students' research at the University of Findlay was real. The compound RK-15 exists or existed. The ambition to develop a chalcone-based compound capable of crossing the blood-brain barrier to deliver medication to brain tumors was legitimate and scientifically coherent.

None of that makes the viral claim true.

The specific assertion that students "developed a compound that kills brain cancer cells" goes substantially beyond anything that was demonstrated, reported, or even claimed by the researchers themselves. No evidence has been presented that RK-15 kills cancer cells in any context. No evidence has been presented that it crosses the blood-brain barrier in a living organism. No evidence has been presented that it is safe, effective, or therapeutically useful. The compound was, at the time of the announcement, a theoretical design — a candidate molecule at the very earliest stage of the drug development pipeline.

Snopes rated the claim "Unproven," which is technically accurate: the claim has not been disproven, because the research has not progressed to the point where it could be either proven or disproven. But the "Unproven" rating is arguably generous to the viral framing. The posts that circulated on social media did not say "students are exploring a compound that might someday be tested for potential anticancer properties." They said, or strongly implied, that students had developed a compound that kills brain cancer cells. This specific claim — about demonstrated efficacy — is not merely unproven. It is unsupported by any publicly available evidence whatsoever.

The compound exists. The implied efficacy does not.

Seven years of silence have not disproven RK-15, but they have not supported it either. In the absence of any peer-reviewed publication, any preclinical trial data, any regulatory filing, or any subsequent university announcement, the most honest assessment is that this was an interesting student project that did not survive contact with the realities of drug development. That outcome is not shameful — it is, statistically, by far the most common outcome for early-stage compound identification. But it is a very different story from the one that went viral.

## The Wider Picture

To understand why the RK-15 story matters beyond its specific facts, you need to understand the reality of drug development — and the specific tragedy it intersects.

**The drug development pipeline is long, expensive, and almost always fails.** The journey from compound identification to approved therapy typically takes 10 to 15 years. The cost, on average, exceeds $2.6 billion per approved drug (a figure that includes the cost of all the compounds that failed along the way). The overall success rate from Phase I clinical trials to FDA approval is approximately 10%; the success rate from preclinical candidate identification — the stage RK-15 was at — is substantially lower. For oncology drugs specifically, the success rate from Phase I to approval is approximately 5%. For central nervous system drugs (those targeting the brain), the historical failure rate is even worse: approximately 97% of CNS drug candidates fail in development.

These are not abstract statistics. They represent a fundamental reality about biological complexity. The human body is not a simple machine that can be fixed with the right molecule. It is an extraordinarily complex adaptive system, and the history of drug development is largely a history of compounds that worked beautifully in the laboratory and failed catastrophically in living organisms — because they were metabolized too quickly, or caused liver toxicity, or failed to reach their target tissue in adequate concentrations, or triggered unexpected immune responses, or worked in mice but not in humans, or worked in some humans but not others.

**The "valley of death" between discovery and clinic.** In pharmaceutical research, there is a well-documented gap known as the "valley of death" — the stage between academic discovery and clinical development where promising compounds go to die. This is not a failure of will or funding (though both can be factors); it is a consequence of the exponential increase in complexity as research moves from isolated cells to whole organisms. A compound that selectively kills glioblastoma cells in a dish must also, in a living human, be absorbable, distributable, metabolizable, and excretable in appropriate ways; it must reach the brain in adequate concentration; it must not damage healthy brain tissue; it must not interact dangerously with other medications; and it must do all of this reliably across a diverse patient population. Each of these requirements is a separate filter, and the compound must pass all of them. Most don't.

**BBB-crossing is a major, heavily researched challenge.** The blood-brain barrier problem that the Findlay students were targeting is not a niche concern. It is one of the central challenges of neuropharmacology, and hundreds of research groups worldwide are working on it. Strategies include nanoparticle delivery systems, focused ultrasound to temporarily disrupt the BBB, receptor-mediated transcytosis, intranasal delivery, convection-enhanced delivery, and chemical modification of existing drugs to improve brain penetration. The literature on BBB-crossing strategies contains thousands of papers. The Findlay team's chalcone-based approach was not, in the context of this broader field, a novel concept — it was one entry in a very large and established research space.

**The chalcone research landscape.** Chalcones and their derivatives have been studied for anticancer properties by numerous research groups over several decades. A search of the biomedical literature reveals hundreds of papers on chalcone anticancer activity, including studies on brain cancer cell lines. This is important context because the viral framing of the story implied that the Findlay students had made a unique discovery. In reality, they were working in a well-established area of medicinal chemistry. Their specific compound, RK-15, may have had novel features — we cannot know, because no peer-reviewed description of it exists — but the general approach of using chalcone-based compounds against cancer cells was neither new nor unique to their group.

**How university press offices feed the hype cycle.** The University of Findlay's press release was, by the standards of university communications, fairly restrained. It acknowledged that the research was in its infancy. It noted that years of further study would be required. But press releases exist in a media ecosystem that selects for superlatives, and even a restrained announcement about "potential brain cancer treatment" enters a pipeline that reliably strips away qualifiers. University press offices know this. They issue dozens or hundreds of releases per year, and the ones that get picked up are the ones with the most dramatic framing. This creates an institutional incentive to push toward the boundary of what the research supports — not to lie, exactly, but to present results in their most flattering light. The downstream consequences, when those releases are further amplified and simplified by social media, are rarely considered and never borne by the university itself.

**The specific tragedy of glioblastoma.** The emotional power of the RK-15 story derives substantially from the specific horror of glioblastoma. GBM is not just deadly; it is cruel in a way that few other cancers match. Because it grows in the brain, it progressively destroys the cognitive functions that make a person who they are. Patients may lose the ability to speak, to recognize family members, to form new memories, to control their emotions. The disease typically strikes in middle age — in people with careers, families, and decades of expected life ahead of them. The standard of care (surgical resection followed by radiation and temozolomide) has not changed substantially since 2005 and extends median survival by only a few months. Immunotherapy, which has transformed the treatment of several other cancers, has shown limited efficacy against GBM, in part because of the BBB and in part because of the immunosuppressive microenvironment of brain tumors.

This context makes hope both precious and dangerous. Precious because patients and families desperately need it. Dangerous because it makes people vulnerable to claims that outrun the evidence. Every viral post about a "brain cancer cure" generates real emotional responses in people who are watching loved ones die. When those claims turn out to be overblown — as they almost always are — the resulting disappointment is not merely intellectual. It is a wound inflicted on people who are already suffering.

Hope is monetizable. It generates clicks, shares, donations, and media attention. The RK-15 story, in its viral form, generated all of these things. It cost nothing to the people who shared it and everything to the people who allowed themselves to believe it.

**The absence of any RK-15 publication years later tells a story.** In science, silence is data. When a promising compound is identified and then disappears from the literature, the most common explanations are: the compound failed to perform as hoped in subsequent testing; the research team disbanded (a near-certainty in this case, since the original researchers were students who have long since graduated); funding was not secured for further development; or the results were insufficiently novel to merit publication. None of these explanations is dramatic. All of them are common. Collectively, they represent the normal, expected outcome for the vast majority of early-stage research projects. The absence of follow-up on RK-15 is not a scandal or a suppression. It is the statistical baseline.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes approached this claim with their standard methodology. Reporter Madison Dapcevich traced the viral posts back to the original University of Findlay announcement, identified the two-year gap between the research and the viral surge, and attempted to contact the university for an update on the status of the research. The university did not respond — a silence that, combined with the absence of any scientific publications, strongly suggests that the project did not progress beyond its initial stages.

Snopes rated the claim "Unproven," one of their standard rating categories. The "Unproven" rating is distinct from "False" (the claim is demonstrably wrong), "Mostly False" (the claim contains some truth but is misleading), "Mixture" (the claim contains significant elements of both truth and falsehood), or "Unproven" (insufficient evidence exists to support or refute the claim). In Snopes' taxonomy, "Unproven" is reserved for claims that may have some basis but cannot be confirmed with available evidence.

This rating is defensible but reveals something about the structural limitations of fact-checking as currently practiced. The claim that students "developed a compound that kills brain cancer cells" is not merely unproven in the sense that we lack evidence to confirm it. It is positively unsupported: no evidence of cancer cell killing has ever been presented. A stronger case could be made for "Mostly False" or "Mixture" — the students and the compound are real, but the specific claim about killing cancer cells is fabricated by the social media amplification process, not supported even by the university's own announcement.

The challenge for fact-checkers is that "Unproven" occupies a comfortable middle ground that avoids the political and reputational risks of a harsher rating. Calling a claim about cancer research "False" or "Mostly False" can generate backlash ("You're saying the students' work is worthless!"), even when the rating applies to the viral framing rather than to the underlying research. The "Unproven" rating accurately captures the epistemic state — we don't know if RK-15 works — while sidestepping the normative question of whether the viral claim was reasonable or responsible.

**Fact-checking the absence of evidence.** One of the hardest problems in fact-checking is evaluating claims that depend on the absence of evidence. Snopes could not definitively say that RK-15 doesn't work, because no test results (positive or negative) have been published. They could only note that no evidence supports the claim. This is a genuine epistemological difficulty: the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, but it is also not nothing. In a domain where successful results are reliably published and celebrated, the consistent absence of results over a period of years is informative. Fact-checkers are trained to evaluate claims based on available evidence, but the heuristic of "if this worked, we would have heard about it by now" — while statistically sound — is difficult to formalize as a fact-checking standard.

**Following up on preliminary announcements.** The RK-15 case highlights a gap in the fact-checking ecosystem: the absence of systematic follow-up on preliminary research claims. When a university announces a promising compound, the announcement enters the information ecosystem and remains there indefinitely. But there is no corresponding mechanism for registering when the research fails to progress. No university issues a press release saying "That compound we announced three years ago? It didn't work out." The result is a ratchet effect: positive preliminary claims accumulate in the public record, while negative results and abandoned projects leave no trace. Over time, this creates a systematically distorted picture of scientific progress, in which every research direction appears to lead somewhere and none appears to fail.

Fact-checkers like Snopes perform an essential service by evaluating individual claims, but the deeper problem is structural. The information ecosystem is optimized for generating and amplifying hope and poorly equipped for registering the far more common outcome of hope disappointed. Until that asymmetry is addressed — through better science communication, more transparent reporting of negative results, or systematic tracking of research claims over time — the pipeline from preliminary announcement to viral misinformation will continue to operate exactly as it did in the case of RK-15.

The students at the University of Findlay did real work on a real problem. The compound they identified may or may not have had genuine therapeutic potential. We will likely never know. What we do know is that the story of their work, as told by social media, was not their story at all. It was a story that social media wanted to tell — about young heroes, natural cures, and simple solutions to complex problems — and the students' research was merely the raw material from which that more satisfying fiction was constructed.
