# A Study Found People Who Add Salt to Food Were More Likely to Develop Stomach Cancer

## The Claim

In April 2024, headlines cascaded across health media with a finding designed to make you put down the salt shaker: people who add salt to their food are roughly 40% more likely to develop stomach cancer. The claim circulated widely on Reddit, particularly in health and science subreddits, where the study was shared with titles ranging from the cautiously accurate ("Study links adding salt at table to increased stomach cancer risk") to the flatly causal ("Salt causes stomach cancer — new study confirms"). News outlets including Medical News Today, the New York Post, and various health-focused aggregators picked up the story, each compressing the finding into progressively more alarming framings.

The study behind the headlines — Kronsteiner-Gicevic et al., "Adding salt to food at table as an indicator of gastric cancer risk among adults: a prospective study," published in the journal *Gastric Cancer* in April 2024 — was a serious piece of epidemiological research. It drew on over 470,000 adults enrolled in the UK Biobank, tracked across roughly eleven years of follow-up, and found a statistically significant association between self-reported frequency of adding salt to food and subsequent stomach cancer diagnosis. The hazard ratios landed between 1.39 and 1.41 depending on model adjustments — the "roughly 40%" figure that anchored the coverage.

Snopes rated the claim **True**. And in a strict, narrow sense, it is: the study exists, the finding is real, and the statistical association is robust within the parameters of the analysis. But between "a study found an association" and "salt gives you stomach cancer," there lies a vast epistemological territory that most coverage — and most fact-checking — declined to explore. That territory is where this entry lives.

The social media discourse followed a predictable bifurcation. One camp treated the finding as confirmation of what they already believed — that salt is a dietary villain, that modern food is poisoning us, that science has once again proven the obvious. The other camp, smaller but vocal, dismissed the finding on reflexive grounds — that "everything causes cancer," that observational studies are worthless, that the establishment wants to take away another pleasure. Neither response engaged with what the study actually demonstrated, what it couldn't demonstrate, and what the distinction means for anyone trying to make informed decisions about their diet.

## What's Actually True

The Kronsteiner-Gicevic study is methodologically solid within the conventions of nutritional epidemiology — a field whose conventions deserve scrutiny but not dismissal. Here is what the study actually did, and what it actually found.

**The cohort.** The UK Biobank enrolled approximately 500,000 adults aged 40-69 between 2006 and 2010 across 22 assessment centres in England, Scotland, and Wales. This is one of the largest prospective cohort studies in the world, and it has generated hundreds of published analyses across virtually every domain of health research. The salt-stomach cancer analysis used data from 471,144 participants after exclusions for prevalent cancer, missing data on the salt question, and other standard quality filters.

**The exposure measure.** Participants were asked, via touchscreen questionnaire at baseline, how frequently they added salt to their food. The response options were: never/rarely, sometimes, usually, and always. This is a single question, asked once, about behaviour at one point in time. It is not a measurement of total sodium intake. It is not repeated across follow-up. It is a self-reported proxy for a behavioural pattern that may or may not correlate tightly with actual salt consumption. The researchers acknowledged this limitation explicitly: adding salt at the table captures discretionary salt use, whereas the majority of sodium in Western diets comes from processed and pre-prepared foods. A person who never salts their plate but eats processed food daily may consume far more sodium than someone who always reaches for the shaker but cooks from scratch.

**The outcome.** Over a median follow-up of approximately 11.2 years, 640 incident cases of gastric (stomach) cancer were identified through linkage with national cancer registries and hospital records. This is a critical number: 640 cases out of 471,000 participants. The absolute risk is low — roughly 0.14% of participants developed stomach cancer during follow-up, regardless of salt behaviour.

**The finding.** After adjustment for age, sex, Townsend deprivation index, ethnicity, BMI, smoking status, alcohol intake, physical activity, red meat consumption, fruit and vegetable intake, and total energy intake, the hazard ratio for those who reported "always" adding salt compared to "never/rarely" was 1.41 (95% confidence interval: 1.03–1.94). An alternative model using propensity score weighting yielded a hazard ratio of 1.39 (95% CI: 1.07–1.81). Both figures are statistically significant but sit at the lower range of what epidemiologists consider a meaningful signal — a point we will return to.

**The supporting literature.** This study did not emerge from a vacuum. The relationship between salt intake and stomach cancer has been investigated for decades, with a substantial body of concordant evidence. A 2009 meta-analysis published in the *World Journal of Gastroenterology* (D'Elia et al.) pooled data from prospective studies and found a dose-response relationship between salt intake and gastric cancer risk. A 2021 systematic review in *Frontiers in Nutrition* reinforced the association using updated data. A 2022 study in *Nutrients* examined dietary patterns and gastric cancer risk with similar findings. The World Cancer Research Fund (WCRF) rates salt and salt-preserved foods as having "strong evidence" for increasing stomach cancer risk. The WHO's International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has classified salt-preserved vegetables and salted fish as Group 1 carcinogens — meaning there is sufficient evidence in humans that these specific preparations cause cancer. Note the specificity: IARC does not classify "salt" per se as a carcinogen; it classifies certain salt-preserved foods, a distinction that matters enormously but is routinely collapsed in media coverage.

**The biological plausibility.** Salt damages the gastric mucosa — the protective lining of the stomach — through direct osmotic stress. High-concentration salt solutions cause cellular dehydration and inflammation. In the presence of *Helicobacter pylori*, the bacterium responsible for the majority of stomach cancers worldwide, salt-induced mucosal damage creates a permissive environment for chronic inflammation, atrophic gastritis, and eventually neoplastic transformation. Salt also increases the virulence of certain *H. pylori* strains by upregulating the CagA protein, a key oncogenic factor. Animal models, particularly in Mongolian gerbils, have shown that high-salt diets dramatically increase gastric cancer rates when combined with *H. pylori* infection. The mechanism is not speculative — it is well-characterized and biologically coherent.

## Why People Believe This

The finding that salt increases stomach cancer risk fits neatly into a pre-existing cultural narrative that has been building for decades: the story of salt as a dietary villain. This narrative has legitimate roots — the relationship between sodium intake and hypertension is among the most replicated findings in nutritional science — but it has also been simplified and moralized in ways that make people primed to accept any new indictment of salt without interrogation.

The "salt is bad" frame operates through several reinforcing channels. Public health messaging, from the WHO to national dietary guidelines, has emphasized sodium reduction for decades. Food labelling laws highlight sodium content. The word "salty" itself has acquired negative connotations in colloquial language. When a study arrives saying that salt is linked to yet another disease, it does not need to overcome scepticism — it slots into an existing mental model that has been trained to expect exactly this kind of finding.

Media amplification operates on complementary incentives. Health stories that confirm widespread beliefs generate engagement without controversy. A headline saying "salt linked to cancer risk" is simultaneously alarming (which drives clicks) and unsurprising (which prevents pushback). The finding achieves the ideal media pharmacokinetics: enough shock to motivate a click, enough familiarity to avoid cognitive resistance. Editors know this intuitively.

Confirmation bias operates at the individual level. People who already believe they should eat less salt — which, in the contemporary West, is most health-conscious adults — encounter the finding and file it under "things I already knew." The study becomes not new evidence to be weighed but validation of an existing commitment. This is psychologically comfortable but epistemically lazy. The relevant question is never "does this study agree with what I already believe?" but "what does this study actually add to the evidentiary picture, and how much should it update my confidence?"

There is also a subtler dynamic at work: the appeal of monocausal explanations for complex diseases. Cancer is terrifying precisely because its aetiology is typically multifactorial, stochastic, and partially beyond individual control. A finding that links cancer to a simple, controllable behaviour — adding salt to food — is psychologically attractive because it restores a sense of agency. If salt causes cancer, then not salting your food protects you. The world becomes simpler, more navigable, more just. That this simplification is precisely what the study's design cannot support is, for most readers, an unwelcome complication.

## Verdict

**True, but epistemologically loaded.** The study is real, the association is statistically significant, and it converges with a broader body of evidence linking high salt intake to gastric cancer risk. Snopes got the rating right. But the space between "true" and "useful" is where the important work happens, and most coverage declined to do that work.

**Observational ≠ causal.** This is a prospective cohort study. It can identify associations; it cannot establish causation. The researchers controlled for a substantial list of confounders, but residual confounding is unavoidable in observational nutrition research. People who always add salt to food may differ from those who never do in dozens of unmeasured ways — stress levels, cooking methods, socioeconomic microenvironments, other dietary components, healthcare-seeking behaviour. The study's own authors use the language of association, not causation. Headlines that translate "associated with" into "linked to" or "increases risk" are performing a semantic upgrade that the data does not authorise.

**Self-reported proxy, not intake measurement.** The exposure variable — frequency of adding salt at the table — is a behavioural proxy measured once at baseline. It does not capture total sodium intake. It does not capture changes in behaviour over the eleven-year follow-up. It does not distinguish between someone who adds a pinch and someone who buries their plate. Measurement error in nutritional epidemiology is endemic, and single-item self-report measures are among the noisiest instruments available. The researchers were transparent about this; the media was not.

**Relative risk in context of absolute risk.** A 40% increase sounds dramatic. But 40% of what? In this cohort, roughly 0.14% of participants developed stomach cancer over eleven years. Even if we accept the hazard ratio at face value and apply it crudely, we are looking at something like 0.20% absolute risk among "always" salters versus 0.14% among "never" salters — an absolute difference of approximately 0.06 percentage points. This is not nothing, but it is a very different proposition from what "40% more likely" suggests to a lay reader. The failure to contextualise relative risks with absolute numbers is one of the most persistent and damaging patterns in health communication, and this case is a textbook example.

**Healthy user bias in UK Biobank.** The UK Biobank is a magnificent resource, but it is not a representative sample of the UK population. Participants are healthier, wealthier, and more educated than the general population. Response rates to the initial invitation were around 5.5%. This creates a "healthy volunteer" effect that can bias associations in complex ways. People who volunteer for long-term health studies tend to have different baseline health behaviours, healthcare access patterns, and risk factor profiles than the general public. The extent to which findings from UK Biobank generalise to broader populations — particularly to populations with different dietary patterns, *H. pylori* prevalence, or genetic susceptibility profiles — remains an open question that the study itself flagged as a limitation.

**The dose-response question.** The study found a broadly dose-responsive relationship — more frequent salt addition associated with higher risk — which epidemiologists generally interpret as strengthening a causal inference. But the gradient was not steep, confidence intervals overlapped between middle categories, and the "always" category contained a relatively small number of participants and cases. The dose-response pattern is suggestive but not definitive.

The verdict, then, is not "false" or even "misleading." It is true, and reasonably so. But the truth is considerably more nuanced, conditional, and uncertain than any headline conveyed. The epistemological gap is not between the claim and reality — it is between the claim and its meaning.

## The Wider Picture

Stomach cancer is the fifth most common cancer worldwide and the fourth leading cause of cancer death. Its global burden is strikingly uneven. East Asia — particularly Japan, South Korea, and parts of China — bears a disproportionate share, with age-standardised incidence rates five to ten times higher than in Western Europe or North America. This geographic distribution is not random; it correlates with dietary patterns (high intake of salt-preserved vegetables, pickled foods, and salted fish), *H. pylori* prevalence, and genetic factors.

The *H. pylori* interaction is critical context for interpreting the salt finding. *Helicobacter pylori* colonises the stomachs of roughly half the world's population, with prevalence rates exceeding 80% in some developing countries and falling below 20% in parts of Northern Europe. *H. pylori* is the single strongest known risk factor for gastric cancer, classified by IARC as a definite (Group 1) carcinogen. The current scientific consensus holds that most stomach cancers arise through a cascade initiated by chronic *H. pylori* infection: chronic gastritis → atrophic gastritis → intestinal metaplasia → dysplasia → carcinoma. Salt's role in this cascade is primarily as a promoter and potentiator — it damages the gastric lining, makes it more susceptible to *H. pylori* colonisation, and enhances the bacterium's virulence. In populations with low *H. pylori* prevalence (such as the UK Biobank cohort), the absolute contribution of dietary salt to stomach cancer risk is likely smaller than in populations where the bacterium is near-universal.

The UK Biobank study could not adequately assess *H. pylori* status. Participants were not systematically tested for the infection, and the study did not stratify results by *H. pylori* seropositivity. This is a significant gap: the effect of salt on a *H. pylori*-negative stomach may be qualitatively different from its effect on a chronically infected one. The biological plausibility of the salt-cancer link is strongest in the context of *H. pylori* co-exposure, and in a cohort where *H. pylori* prevalence is relatively low and declining, the observed association may be attenuated or driven by a subpopulation that was not separately identified.

Salt-preserved foods in Asian diets deserve particular attention. The IARC classification of Chinese-style salted fish as a Group 1 carcinogen is based on strong epidemiological evidence linking regular consumption to nasopharyngeal and gastric cancers, particularly in southern China and Southeast Asia. Pickled vegetables, fermented soybean pastes, and other traditionally preserved foods contribute both sodium and potentially carcinogenic by-products (N-nitroso compounds, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons from smoking processes). The distinction between discretionary table salt and salt as a preservation agent — operating through different chemical pathways and in different dietary contexts — is scientifically important but almost never preserved in popular reporting.

A particularly striking epidemiological trend is the rising incidence of stomach cancer among younger adults, and particularly among young women, in several high-income countries. Data from the United States, United Kingdom, and parts of Europe show increasing rates of non-cardia gastric cancer (the type most strongly associated with *H. pylori* and dietary factors) in cohorts born after 1980. The reasons are not well understood. Some researchers have proposed changes in the gastric microbiome, antibiotic use patterns, dietary shifts, or autoimmune factors. Whatever the explanation, the trend complicates the simple narrative that stomach cancer is a disease of the old and the heavily salted.

Perhaps most importantly, the study highlights the tension between single-nutrient thinking and dietary pattern analysis. Modern nutritional epidemiology has increasingly moved away from isolating individual nutrients or foods toward examining overall dietary patterns — Mediterranean, DASH, Western, plant-based — recognising that foods are consumed together, interact biochemically, and produce health effects through synergies that single-nutrient analysis cannot capture. A person who adds salt to home-cooked vegetables, lean proteins, and whole grains is engaging in a fundamentally different dietary behaviour than a person who adds salt to processed convenience foods, even if their salt-shaker frequency is identical. The UK Biobank question — "do you add salt to your food?" — cannot distinguish between these scenarios. Its statistical power comes at the cost of dietary context, and that context may be more important than the salt itself.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated the claim **True**, and by the standards of conventional fact-checking, this is defensible. The study exists. The finding is accurately characterised. The claim as circulated — that researchers found a roughly 40% increased risk of stomach cancer among people who frequently add salt — is a fair summary of the published results. Snopes provided context, cited the study, and noted some limitations. The rating is not wrong.

But the fact-checking framework reveals its limitations here. The binary True/False architecture (with Mixture and other nuance categories sometimes deployed) forces a flattening of epistemological complexity. "True" collapses the distance between "a study found a statistically significant association in a specific cohort using a proxy measure" and "adding salt to your food increases your cancer risk." These are not the same claim, but the rating treats them as equivalent.

What Snopes did not interrogate is the leap from observational association to implied causation. The fact-check accurately described the study but did not push back on the causal framing embedded in the very question it was answering. When someone asks "did a study find that salt causes cancer?", the epistemologically rigorous answer is not simply "yes, a study found people who add salt were more likely to develop stomach cancer." The rigorous answer includes the caveat that "more likely to develop" in this context means "were observed at higher rates of diagnosis in a non-randomised, non-interventional study with a single self-reported behavioural proxy as the exposure variable." That caveat is not pedantry — it is the difference between understanding the finding and being frightened by it.

The relative-versus-absolute risk framing is another area where standard fact-checking underperforms. Reporting a 40% relative risk increase without anchoring it to the baseline absolute risk — roughly 0.14% in this cohort — creates a predictable misperception of magnitude. Behavioural economics research consistently shows that relative risk framings inflate perceived danger compared to absolute risk framings. A fact-checker who reports "40% more likely" without also reporting the absolute numbers is not lying, but they are participating in a framing that produces systematically inaccurate beliefs in readers. The Snopes piece, like the media coverage it was checking, operated within the relative-risk frame without breaking out of it.

UK Biobank selection bias received limited attention. The fact-check did not discuss the fundamental issue of generalisability — that a cohort with a 5.5% response rate, drawn from a specific national and demographic context, with known healthy-volunteer effects, produces findings that may not apply to other populations. This matters particularly for a dietary question, where cultural context (what foods are available, how they are prepared, what other dietary components accompany salt use) varies dramatically across populations. A finding from a predominantly white, relatively affluent British cohort cannot be straightforwardly applied to diets in East Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, or Latin America, where both salt use patterns and stomach cancer epidemiology differ profoundly.

The deeper structural issue is that fact-checking, as currently practised, operates primarily at the level of factual accuracy rather than epistemic quality. The question "is this claim true?" is adjacent to but distinct from "what should a reasonable person believe after encountering this claim?" Snopes answered the first question well. The second question — which requires engaging with study design limitations, effect size contextualisation, population generalisability, and the gap between statistical association and dietary advice — fell largely outside the fact-checking frame.

This is not a criticism unique to Snopes. It is a structural limitation of the fact-checking genre. The format privileges verifiability over meaning, accuracy over interpretation, and binary categorisation over probabilistic reasoning. For claims where the factual substrate is straightforward and the interpretive stakes are low, this works fine. For claims where a technically accurate finding can produce wildly miscalibrated beliefs — as in the salt-cancer case — the format is insufficient. The claim is true. What you should do with that truth is a different, harder, and more important question that fact-checking, by design, declines to answer.

The EARC determination for this entry is **E (Enhance)** — Snopes gets the core right, but the epistemological scaffolding that would help readers actually understand the finding, rather than merely know that it exists, is exactly the kind of work that the Truth Vault project exists to provide. The gap category is **distorted-but-grounded**: the underlying science is real and converging, but the journey from cohort study to kitchen-table advice passes through more uncertainty than anyone in the information chain — researchers excepted — bothered to acknowledge.
