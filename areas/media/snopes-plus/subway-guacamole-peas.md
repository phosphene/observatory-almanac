## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

On 6 July 2015, Cabel Sasser, co-founder of the Oregon-based software company Panic, posted a photograph to his Twitter account that appeared to show an in-store Subway restaurant advertisement announcing a new guacamole product containing peas. The photograph spread rapidly across Twitter and social media at a moment of heightened cultural sensitivity about peas in guacamole: the New York Times had re-circulated a 2013 article on peas-in-guacamole recipes on 1 July 2015, generating a wave of mockery and commentary, including a now-famous tweet from President Barack Obama (via the @POTUS account) declaring "respect the nyt, but not buying peas in guac. onions, garlic, hot peppers. classic." The cultural mood was primed for exactly the kind of outrage that a Subway-peas-in-guacamole announcement would generate.

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2015) evaluated the claim and assigned the rating **False**. The Subway advertisement shown in the photograph was fabricated: Sasser had Photoshopped a stock photograph of peas (purchased for $5 from Adobe's Creative Cloud Asset Store) onto a photograph of a real Subway advertisement, then posted the result as if it were genuine. Sasser himself confirmed this shortly after the photograph went viral, acknowledging the hoax and explaining his creative process. A Subway spokesman confirmed to journalists that the chain was not experimenting with peas in guacamole. The claim was entirely false, and the origin of the false claim was intentional creative fabrication rather than misinformation or misunderstanding.

This entry examines the creation and rapid identification of the hoax, the cultural context that made it immediately believable and shareable, the broader phenomenon of deliberate food-culture hoaxes on social media, the presidential guacamole tweet that provided the context, and what the speed of both the hoax's spread and its exposure reveals about the structural properties of viral food content.

## §2 The Fabrication and Its Exposure

The mechanics of the Sasser hoax were straightforward and by his own admission extremely inexpensive. A photograph of a Subway restaurant's interior promotional signage—the kind displayed on menu boards, counter cards, or wall advertisements within a franchise location—was used as the base. Sasser purchased a stock photograph of fresh peas from Adobe's Creative Cloud Asset Store for $5.00 and composited it onto or into the existing advertisement in a way that made it appear to be a genuine Subway promotional image. The resulting image was designed to look like an official Subway advertisement for a new guacamole product featuring peas. Posted to his Twitter account with the caption "Welp. That was fast." on 6 July 2015—just five days after the Obama guacamole-peas tweet—the image immediately attracted engagement from an audience primed to be outraged by exactly this development.

Within hours, the photograph was circulating beyond Sasser's follower network. The cultural timing was perfect: the peas-in-guacamole debate was already generating fresh commentary, and the apparent confirmation that a major chain was now institutionalizing the same travesty was exactly the kind of corporate villain moment that social media food discourse was structured to amplify.

The exposure was nearly as rapid. A Subway spokesman contacted by journalists denied that the chain had any plans involving peas in guacamole. Sasser himself confirmed the hoax on 7 July 2015, tweeting "I spent $5 in the Creative Cloud Asset Store for a photograph of peas," explaining that his caption "That was fast"—which appeared to reference how quickly the food industry had responded to the Obama-inspired debate—was intended to be a knowing joke for followers who understood the manufacturing process, not a sincere representation of a genuine advertisement. The brief time between the hoax's launch and its exposure—essentially less than 24 hours—was itself notable, though it did not prevent the fabricated image from spreading beyond recoverable reach on social media. The retraction tweet did not achieve anywhere near the same reach as the original fabrication.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Deliberate Hoax, Rapid Self-Disclosure, and the Structural Irreversibility of Viral Claims

The Subway guacamole hoax has several features that make it epistemologically distinctive within the taxonomy of food misinformation. First, it was a deliberate creative act by a named, publicly identifiable individual—not an anonymous post, not a misread news story, not an ambiguous claim, but a specifically manufactured fake that the creator acknowledged fabricating essentially immediately. This places it in a different category from most of the food myths that Snopes investigates, which involve either sincere misunderstanding, ambiguous evidence, or anonymous fabrication.

Second, the hoax was effective not because of sophisticated photographic fakery—the $5 stock-image production value was minimal—but because it exploited a culturally prepared audience. The credibility of the fake Subway advertisement derived not from the image's technical believability but from the audience's pre-existing expectation that such an announcement was exactly the kind of thing that would happen. The epistemological term for this mechanism is **prior-expectation amplification**: when a fabricated claim is structurally consistent with what an audience already expects or fears, the evaluation threshold for accepting it as genuine drops substantially.

Pennycook et al. (2018, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*) demonstrated experimentally that claims that are politically or culturally congruent with a reader's pre-existing beliefs are evaluated as more credible and shared more readily than formally identical claims that are incongruent, independent of the claims' actual truth value. The Subway peas hoax functioned through cultural congruence: within the community of guacamole purists who had already concluded that peas-in-guacamole was absurd, a corporate adoption of that absurdity was exactly what one might expect from the fast food industry.

The irreversibility observation is equally important. Sasser's confession tweet was posted on 7 July 2015, the day after the original hoax image. Evon (2015) reported the confession and Subway's denial. Yet the images and associated claims continued to circulate after the confession; the structure of social media sharing means that once an image has been embedded in thousands of shares, screenshots, and re-posts, the factual annotation that accompanies the original post is stripped from subsequent circulations. A consumer encountering the image on 10 July or 20 July or six months later would encounter the fake advertisement image without the accompanying confession.

This asymmetric persistence is a well-documented feature of visual hoaxes versus text corrections. Garrett and Weeks (2013, *Political Communication*) found that corrections to visual misinformation—photographs or images presented as authentic that were subsequently revealed as fabricated—showed significantly lower correction efficiency than corrections to text-based misinformation, because visual content persists in sharing networks independently of the text annotations that originally accompanied it.

## §4 The Peas-in-Guacamole Cultural Context

To understand why the Sasser hoax achieved rapid and extensive spread, it is necessary to understand the culturally charged status of peas as a guacamole additive in early July 2015. The New York Times food section had originally published a recipe from chef Dan Barber of Blue Hill restaurant that incorporated peas into guacamole as a way of extending the dip while preserving its creaminess and substantially reducing its caloric density—avocados are energy-dense, while peas are not. The recipe was presented in 2013 not as a replacement for traditional guacamole but as one approach to a particular culinary problem: how to make a dish that stretches a more expensive primary ingredient with a cheaper, high-volume secondary one while maintaining appeal.

When the Times re-circulated this recipe in July 2015, the response was substantially more inflamed than the original 2013 publication had generated—likely because the intervening years had seen guacamole achieve an unusual level of cultural hot-button status, partly driven by discussions about avocado prices, partly by the general intensification of food identity politics in online discourse. The Obama @POTUS tweet on 1 July 2015 declared: "respect the nyt, but not buying peas in guac. onions, garlic, hot peppers. classic." This tweet—from the presidential account, engaging with food trivia during the run-up to the Fourth of July holiday—was immediately understood as playful, but it also effectively validated the anti-peas position from the highest cultural authority and amplified the entire debate.

The participation of the presidential social media operation in real-time food culture debate is itself an epistemological event worth noting. It contributed to the perception that the peas-in-guacamole question was a genuine cultural flashpoint requiring resolution, which in turn made the idea that a restaurant chain might "capitalize" on the debate by offering a pea-enhanced guacamole both plausible and outrage-worthy.

Food authenticity discourse—particularly around dishes with strong cultural heritage claims—exhibits a predictable pattern of sensitivity to perceived adulteration. Guacamole, with its origins in pre-Columbian Mesoamerican cuisine (the Aztec word *ahuacamolli* denoted the avocado-based sauce in Nahuatl) and its status as a marker of Mexican culinary heritage, occupies a culturally charged space in American food identity politics. Claiming that peas belong in guacamole was heard not merely as a culinary suggestion but as a cultural provocation—and a corporate chain announcing pea-guacamole would have been understood as an act of institutional betrayal of culinary authenticity.

Valeri (2000, *Cooking up Nations: Food, Politics, and Cultural Identity*, in *Journal of Food and Foodways*) and subsequent scholars of food and cultural identity have documented how perceived adulteration of heritage dishes triggers responses that go beyond mere preference expression to something closer to identity defense. The Subway peas hoax was engineered—intentionally or intuitively—to trigger exactly this kind of response.

## §5 Cabel Sasser, Deliberate Hoax, and the Responsibility of Well-Followed Pranksters

Sasser's position as the hoax originator is complicated by his willingness to confess quickly and his apparent framing of the fabrication as a joke rather than a deception. He is a well-known figure in the independent software development community, and his social media voice is generally understood by his followers to include a sophisticated self-aware humor. The "Welp. That was fast" caption could be read, by a sufficiently context-aware follower, as a winking comment on his own fabrication rather than a sincere presentation of genuine evidence.

But once the image entered networks beyond his immediate follower community—which it did within hours—the contextual cues that signaled its ironic character were stripped away. The image traveled as a standalone object, and subsequent viewers had no access to Sasser's reputation for irony, his immediate confession, or the $5 stock photo admission. This is the inherent problem with context-dependent humor in social media sharing: jokes that depend on the audience knowing who you are do not travel as jokes.

The ethical dimension of deliberate hoaxes on social media platforms—even ones whose creator intends rapid self-disclosure—has been discussed in emerging social media ethics literature. Wardle and Derakhshan (2017, *Information Disorder: Toward an Interdisciplinary Framework for Research and Policy Making*, Council of Europe report) identified "fabricated content"—content that is entirely false and presented as true—as one of seven distinct types of information disorder, noting that even brief-lived deliberate fabrications contribute to overall epistemic environment degradation if they achieve viral reach before correction.

Whether Sasser's action constitutes irresponsible behavior depends on the framework applied: as a joke intended for a specific audience that got out of scope, it is categorically different from a malicious hoax; but the downstream effect on the information environment was not categorically different.

## §6 Research Gaps and Epistemic Assessment

The EARC gap assignment for this entry is **A (Archival)**. This is a clean, fully resolved factual case: the hoax was intentional, the creator confessed, Subway denied the claim, and the claim is false. No research gap exists on the core factual question. What is archivally incomplete is the spread data: the full extent of the image's circulation after Sasser's confession—how long it continued to be shared as authentic, in what networks, with what volume—is not publicly documented in any systematic way, though this information would be valuable for understanding the persistence of visual hoax content after confession.

The broader research gap, noted in §3, concerns the mechanics of visual hoax correction. The Sasser case is an ideal study subject for this research: a named creator, a specific origination time, a rapid confession, and presumably a recoverable spread pattern in retrospect (if social media platforms provided researcher access to share data). That such case studies are rarely systematically analyzed reflects the general unavailability of social media share data to researchers, a structural impediment to understanding viral claim dynamics.

For readers of this entry, the practical implication is simple: the Subway peas-in-guacamole advertisement is fake, Subway never made any such announcement, and the image that circulated was deliberately created by a named individual who admitted to creating it the following day. Finding this image still circulating should be treated as evidence of the persistence of visual content after debunking, not as evidence of the claim's validity.

**Verdict reaffirmed: False.** The Subway peas-in-guacamole advertisement was a deliberate photographic fabrication by Cabel Sasser, who confessed immediately after the image achieved viral spread. Subway denied the claim. The speed of spread and the persistence of sharing after confession illustrate structural features of visual misinformation in social media contexts that extend well beyond this specific case.
