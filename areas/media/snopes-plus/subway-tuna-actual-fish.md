## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In January 2021, two California plaintiffs—Karen Dhanowa and Nilima Amin—filed a lawsuit in the Northern District of California against Subway IP LLC, claiming that the sandwich chain's tuna did not consist of actual tuna or fish but rather "a mixture of various concoctions that do not constitute tuna, yet have been blended together by defendants to imitate the appearance of tuna." The suit alleged fraud, intentional misrepresentation, and unjust enrichment under federal and state consumer protection laws. One of the plaintiffs' attorneys told *The Washington Post* that the lab testing found "the ingredients were not tuna and not fish." Another attorney, Alex Brown, told CBS News: "We are conducting tests to figure out what it is. The lab tests thus far have only told us what it isn't."

Subway categorically denied the allegations. The company issued a detailed statement: "There simply is no truth to the allegations in the complaint that was filed in California. Subway delivers 100% cooked tuna to its restaurants, which is mixed with mayonnaise and used in freshly made sandwiches, wraps and salads that are served to and enjoyed by our guests." Subway characterized the lawsuit as "frivolous" and attributed it to attorneys "targeting the food industry in an effort to make a name for themselves in that space."

Snopes reporter Nur Ibrahim (2021) assessed the claim and rated it "Unproven." The rating reflected the genuine evidentiary complexity at the heart of the dispute: independent investigations produced contradictory results for reasons rooted in the biology of processed food rather than in deliberate deception or procedural error. A *New York Times* investigation found no amplifiable tuna DNA in submitted samples, but a lab spokesperson explained the result was ambiguous—it could indicate the absence of tuna or simply the denaturation of DNA through cooking. An *Inside Edition* investigation found that samples did contain tuna.

Ibrahim's article assessed the claim with careful calibration, separating what was true (the lawsuit existed, Subway denied it, lab results were contradictory), what was false (the lawsuit provided no details about what non-tuna ingredients were allegedly used), and what was undetermined (the actual species composition of Subway's tuna across its thousands of franchise locations).

This entry examines the science of DNA-based food species identification in processed foods, the legal and regulatory landscape of food labeling, the specific unresolved questions about the tuna supply chain at scale, and what the episode reveals about the epistemological limits of consumer investigations into industrial food composition.

## §2 The Evidence: Lab Testing, DNA Denaturation, and the Limits of Species Identification

The central scientific fact underlying the Subway tuna controversy—and the reason the claim cannot be simply confirmed or denied—is that cooking significantly degrades or destroys the DNA that species-identification testing requires. This is not a flaw in any particular laboratory's methodology but a fundamental constraint of the technology applied to the substrate.

**DNA barcoding for food authentication** — the dominant method for species identification in food fraud investigation — requires the amplification of short DNA sequences characteristic of particular species using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Primers designed for tuna species-specific mitochondrial or nuclear DNA sequences are applied to extracted genetic material from the food sample. If the characteristic sequences can be amplified, the species is identified; if not, either the species is absent or the DNA is too degraded for amplification.

The problem with applying this method to highly processed tuna products is well-documented in the food science literature. Chapela et al. (2007, *Food Chemistry*, Elsevier) demonstrated that thermal processing of canned tuna products substantially reduced the length and integrity of extractable DNA fragments and significantly impaired PCR-based species identification, particularly for methods requiring longer amplification targets. Real-time PCR with shorter target sequences was more robust but still showed reduced amplification efficiency in canned and processed samples compared to fresh fish.

This degradation effect is specifically why the *New York Times* laboratory results were ambiguous rather than exculpatory. The lab spokesperson's statement—"There's two conclusions. One, it's so heavily processed that whatever we could pull out, we couldn't make an identification. Or we got some and there's just nothing there that's tuna"—is scientifically accurate and not a hedged way of saying the sandwich didn't contain tuna. DNA denaturation under heat treatment is irreversible: cooked tuna does not contain intact, amplifiable tuna DNA in the same way that raw tuna does. The absence of amplifiable DNA in cooked tuna products cannot by itself distinguish between "tuna was present and was cooked" and "tuna was never present."

The *Inside Edition* testing (February 2021) that found tuna present introduces its own methodological questions. Without knowing whether the *Inside Edition* sample was tested at the same laboratory, using the same primer sets and amplification conditions, and on comparable amounts of material, the contradictory results cannot be straightforwardly attributed to a difference in actual product composition across the samples tested. Variables between collection, handling, and processing of samples could produce different results even from the same source material.

Subway's franchise model introduces genuine product heterogeneity as an additional variable. Subway operates with thousands of franchise locations, and franchise agreements provide individual operators with some latitude in sourcing. Ibrahim's (2021) article explicitly noted that the ingredients "are not uniformly sourced across franchises, and so tuna in one Subway franchise may have a different source than another even if they are both in California." This means the question "Does Subway's tuna contain actual tuna?" is not a question with a single answer: the answer may genuinely vary by location, supplier, and time.

Subway's own nutritional information page described its tuna as "Flaked tuna blended with creamy mayo then topped with your choice of crisp, fresh veggies" and noted an additive "to protect flavor." The flavoring additive is not identified on the public-facing website, which is permissible under current FDA labeling rules for generic "flavoring" categories but represents a gap in ingredient transparency.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Fundamental Limits of Consumer Food Authentication

The Subway tuna controversy is epistemically distinctive because it reveals structural limits in consumers' ability to verify the composition of processed foods—limits that exist for reasons of food chemistry rather than corporate opacity, though the two can interact.

The question "What is in my sandwich?" seems like it should be straightforwardly answerable by laboratory analysis. The entire field of food authenticity testing is premised on the assumption that such questions can be answered. Yet the Subway tuna case illustrates that for a specific category of products—those that have been significantly heat-processed before the point of consumer purchase—the primary molecular marker for species identification (DNA) may be degraded to the point where the question cannot be reliably answered, at least not by the most commonly used method.

This creates an asymmetric verification problem. Subway's suppliers presumably have access to the raw fish before processing and could verify species composition through fresh-tissue testing. By the time the product reaches a consumer who wishes to verify its composition, the processing that makes it palatable has also compromised the most powerful verification tool available. Consumers and independent investigators are attempting to answer a question using methods that are better suited to an earlier stage in the supply chain.

Spink and Moyer (2011, *Journal of Food Science*, Wiley-Blackwell) defined food fraud as "the deliberate and intentional substitution, addition, tampering, or misrepresentation of food, food ingredients, or food packaging; or false or misleading statements made about a product for economic gain." They noted that fish and seafood are among the most commonly adulterated food categories globally, citing the economic incentive to substitute lower-value species for higher-value ones. Oceana (2013, in a report titled *Oceana Finds Seafood Fraud Nationwide*) found that approximately one-third of seafood sold in the United States was mislabeled, with tuna being one of the most commonly mislabeled products—red snapper, tilapia, and escolar were frequently sold under the "tuna" label.

This broader context of documented seafood fraud provides an empirical backdrop that makes consumer skepticism about Subway's tuna facially plausible, even if the specific claim of the 2021 lawsuit was not supported by the available evidence. The lawsuit's credibility benefited from the well-established empirical fact that seafood fraud is real and widespread; the absence of specific evidence did not make the underlying concern implausible.

The FDA regulatory context adds further complexity. As Ibrahim's (2021) article noted, the FDA recognizes 15 species of nomadic saltwater fish that can be legally labeled "tuna." These include Skipjack tuna (*Katsuwonus pelamis*), Yellowfin tuna (*Thunnus albacares*), Albacore tuna (*Thunnus alalunga*), and several others. The claim in the original lawsuit itself softened over time—by June 2021, the complaints were centering not on "no tuna at all" but on whether Subway's product was "100% sustainably caught skipjack and yellowfin tuna" as the company's branding implied. This represents a significant narrowing of the original claim: the allegation shifted from species substitution fraud ("not fish at all") to potential species misrepresentation within the legally permissible tuna category.

Jacquet and Pauly (2008, *Marine Policy*, Elsevier) documented the pervasive mislabeling of seafood and argued that the complexity of global seafood supply chains—fish caught in one ocean, processed in another country, and sold through global distribution networks—makes species verification practically difficult even for well-intentioned actors. The supply chain distance between the fishing vessel and the consumer's sandwich creates multiple points at which species substitution, either deliberate or inadvertent through mislabeling, could occur.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Ibrahim's (2021) analysis is methodologically rigorous and appropriately cautious given the contested evidence. Several additional threads warrant further development:

**The lawsuit's outcome**: The investigation, published in January 2021 and updated in July 2021, could not report on the outcome of a lawsuit filed just days before publication. The case—Dhanowa et al. v. Subway IP LLC—continued through the court system for years after the Snopes article. Understanding the court's treatment of the evidence, whether discovery revealed the contents of the plaintiffs' lab reports, and what ultimately happened to the case would substantially inform the overall evidentiary picture.

**The specific lab testing methodology**: Neither the plaintiffs' attorneys nor the *New York Times* investigation publicly released full laboratory reports specifying the exact methods, primers, and amplification conditions used. Without this methodological transparency, evaluating the significance of the contradictory results (DNA not detected vs. tuna present) is impossible. The investigation appropriately noted the ambiguity but could not resolve it.

**Comparative testing at scale**: A single *New York Times* reporter sending samples from one location, and a single *Inside Edition* investigation, do not constitute statistically meaningful sampling of a chain with thousands of locations. If 10% of Subway franchise locations served mislabeled product, that would be a severe problem; if 0.1% did due to a single misbehaving supplier, it would be a different category of problem. The investigations cannot distinguish between these scenarios.

**The mayonnaise contribution**: Subway's tuna product is "flaked tuna blended with creamy mayo." The mayonnaise component could affect DNA extraction by creating a lipid-rich matrix that might affect instrument performance. Food scientists investigating processed tuna products typically account for this matrix effect; it is unclear whether the *New York Times* laboratory's protocol accounted for it.

**The broader seafood fraud empirical context**: Ibrahim's article notes in passing that the FDA recognizes 15 species as "tuna" but does not engage with the substantial empirical literature on seafood mislabeling. Given that approximately one-third of seafood in US retail is mislabeled (Oceana, 2013), framing the Subway claim purely as an "unproven lawsuit" rather than engaging with the general seafood fraud background misses an important contextual dimension.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Food Fraud, Supply Chain Opacity, and Consumer Rights

The Subway tuna controversy belongs to a wider and well-documented ecosystem of food authentication challenges that have become increasingly prominent as global food supply chains have grown in length and complexity.

Seafood fraud is one of the best-documented categories of food adulteration because it has attracted significant investigative attention from environmental and consumer protection organizations, particularly given the conservation implications of substituting endangered species for legal ones. Oceana's (2013, 2016) surveillance studies of US seafood mislabeling—which tested over 1,200 samples from restaurants and grocery stores using DNA barcoding—found mislabeling rates of 20–38% for samples explicitly labeled as tuna, with many samples identified as escolar (*Lepidocybium flavobrunneum*), a species associated with gastrointestinal side effects that the FDA once recommended should not be marketed as tuna.

The global tuna fishing industry operates at industrial scale. Skipjack and yellowfin tuna—the species most commonly used in canned and processed tuna products—are caught primarily in the Pacific and Indian Oceans, often by fleets operating under flags of multiple nations. The fish may be processed at sea, transferred to a processing vessel, transported to a canning facility in a second country, and distributed globally before reaching a US franchise. At each step, the opportunity for mislabeling—whether through deliberate fraud or paperwork error—exists, and traceability back to the original fishing event is often limited.

The Food Safety Modernization Act (FSMA, 2011) and its implementing regulations significantly expanded FDA's authority over food supply chain traceability but do not require real-time species verification at the point of consumer sale. The EU's approach is somewhat more stringent: EU Regulation 1379/2013 on the common organization of the markets in fishery and aquaculture products requires commercial denomination, scientific name, production method, and catch area to be indicated on all fish products, creating a stronger paper trail for species verification.

Consumer class action litigation has become a significant enforcement mechanism in the food authenticity space in the absence of sufficient regulatory oversight. The Subway case was part of a wave of food fraud litigation in California (under California's particularly favorable consumer protection statutes) targeting fast food and restaurant chains over ingredient and sourcing claims. While critics characterize such litigation as opportunistic—Subway's statement explicitly referred to attorneys "targeting the food industry"—proponents argue that private litigation fills enforcement gaps left by underfunded regulatory agencies.

The *New York Times* reporter's meta-conclusion is worth examining carefully: "The larger issue was not 'Is this real tuna?' but rather 'What does it take to find out where your food is coming from, and why is it so difficult?'" This reframe shifts the analytical lens from a binary authenticity question (tuna or not) to a structural supply chain transparency question (can consumers and journalists verify food composition through available means?). The answer—demonstrated by the investigation itself—is that under current conditions, they often cannot, and the barriers are technological, economic, and regulatory rather than merely journalistic.

## §6 Verdict Assessment and Broader Implications

The Snopes Unproven verdict for the claim that Subway's tuna contains no actual tuna or fish is appropriate and reflective of the genuinely contested evidentiary situation as of 2021. The claim was not proven by the plaintiffs' lawsuit (which provided no specifics about the alleged substitute ingredients), and it was not disproven by Subway's denial or the contradictory lab results. The scientific explanation for why the *New York Times* DNA testing was ambiguous—DNA denaturation through cooking—prevents a definitive characterization in either direction based on that test alone.

The *Inside Edition* testing that found tuna present provides some evidence in Subway's favor, but the methodological differences between the two testing exercises prevent a clean resolution. The forensic picture remains genuinely murky at the level of the specific claim.

The broader implications are substantial:

**For consumers**, the most important takeaway is not whether Subway specifically uses real tuna but the structural insight that DNA-based verification of processed fish products is technically constrained by the processing itself. Consumer certainty about the species composition of processed seafood products—at any restaurant or grocery chain—is limited by the same constraints that made the Subway testing ambiguous. Verifying claims made about fish species at the point of sale is genuinely difficult.

**For food policy**, the Subway case illustrates a gap in the US regulatory framework: there is no mandatory real-time species verification requirement for seafood products sold at restaurants. The FDA's recognition of 15 species as "tuna" creates legal latitude that could be exploited by actors seeking to minimize input costs, and the inspection and enforcement infrastructure for fast-food seafood authenticity is inadequate compared to the scale and volume of the market.

**For epistemology of food litigation**, the Subway case illustrates the pattern of consumer class action suits where the plaintiffs' original claim ("no tuna or fish at all") is based on lab tests that, as the case unfolds, may show something more nuanced (questionable species verification, not complete absence of fish). The litigation narrative ("Subway's tuna is fake") travels much further than the more accurate characterization ("Subway's tuna resists definitive species verification because cooking degrades DNA").

**EARC classification: C (Contested)** — The claim is genuinely disputed and the available evidence does not resolve it definitively. Gap classification: **contested-evidence-with-methodological-gaps** — the Snopes Unproven rating appropriately reflects the evidentiary limits, but the investigation did not fully engage with the food science literature on DNA denaturation in processed seafood, the broader seafood mislabeling literature, or the subsequent evolution of the lawsuit's specific allegations. The case remains a productive lens for examining consumer food verification epistemology.
