# Is the Sun On 'Lockdown,' Threatening Life on Earth?


## 1. The Claim

In mid-May 2020, as the COVID-19 pandemic kept billions of people under varying degrees of lockdown, a different sort of confinement story began circulating online: the sun itself, it was claimed, had entered a "lockdown" period of dramatically reduced activity that would bring catastrophic consequences to Earth. The claim asserted that solar activity had fallen to such low levels that life on Earth would be threatened by famine, crop failure, earthquakes, and freezing weather.

The claim gained mainstream amplification when the New York Post published an article on May 14, 2020, under the headline "The sun has entered a 'lockdown' period, which could cause freezing weather, famine." The piece, like many pandemic-era news items blending science with urgency, struck a nerve with an already anxious public. Social media users shared the story widely, and the terminology—"lockdown" applied to our nearest star—created a resonant but deeply misleading parallel between human public health measures and astrophysical processes.

The core assertion had several interrelated components: that the sun had entered an unprecedented period of reduced activity; that this quiescence was somehow analogous to human lockdowns; that the reduction in solar activity would lead to measurable cooling on Earth; that decreased solar output would disrupt agriculture to the point of famine; that the change in solar magnetic activity could trigger earthquakes; and that all of these effects combined would threaten human civilization. Each of these subclaims built on the others to create a narrative of imminent, sun-driven catastrophe.

The timing was significant. Spring 2020 represented the peak of pandemic anxiety in much of the world. Confinement measures had upended normal life, unemployment had spiked, and media consumers were hungry for explanations—both scientific and sensational—for what felt like an unprecedented moment. The sun lockdown narrative provided a cosmic-scale explanation that seemed to validate the sense that everything was out of balance.

This was not the first time solar activity had been linked to terrestrial catastrophes in popular imagination, and it would not be the last. The sun has long served as a canvas onto which humans project their anxieties—from ancient eclipse omens to modern fears of solar flares knocking out the electrical grid. The "lockdown" claim drew on this deep well of solar mythology while dressing it in superficially scientific language.

## 2. What's Actually True

The sun does indeed go through cycles of activity, and the period in question genuinely represented a low point in that cycle. The factual foundation of the claim rests on legitimate solar physics, which is why the claim has persisted rather than being dismissed outright as fantasy.

The sun's activity—most commonly measured by the number and intensity of sunspots on its surface—fluctuates on a roughly 11-year cycle. This solar cycle, properly called the Schwabe cycle after the German astronomer who discovered it in the 19th century, consists of a solar maximum (when sunspot numbers are highest and solar flares most frequent) and a solar minimum (when sunspot numbers drop to near zero). The current understanding of this cycle, refined over nearly two centuries of observation, reveals that the sun's magnetic field undergoes a complete polarity reversal every 11 years, driving the ebb and flow of sunspot activity.

By spring 2020, the sun was indeed at or very near the minimum of Solar Cycle 24. Solar Cycle 24 had begun in late 2008, reached its maximum in April 2014 (a relatively weak maximum compared to historical averages), and had been declining steadily since. By late 2019 and early 2020, the sun had entered a period with zero or near-zero sunspots on many days—a condition astronomers call the solar minimum.

Dr. Tony Phillips, a former NASA researcher who runs the space weather website Spaceweather.com, confirmed in a May 2020 email to Snopes that scientists expected the solar minimum to last through much, if not all, of 2020. This was not controversial among heliophysicists; it was an expected phase of a well-understood cycle.

Solar minimums do have real effects, though they are primarily at the level of space weather rather than terrestrial surface conditions. During solar minimum, the sun's reduced ultraviolet output causes Earth's upper atmosphere to cool and contract slightly. This reduction in atmospheric drag means that space debris and defunct satellites linger longer in low Earth orbit—a phenomenon of genuine concern for spacecraft operators. Additionally, the reduction in solar wind and magnetic activity means that more galactic cosmic rays reach the inner solar system, increasing radiation exposure for astronauts and high-altitude air travelers. Coronal holes (regions where the sun's magnetic field opens outward, allowing solar particles to escape) tend to be longer-lived during solar minimum, and the high-speed solar wind streams they produce can cause geomagnetic storms when they interact with Earth's magnetosphere.

The New York Post's article correctly noted that solar minimums are associated with slightly reduced total solar irradiance—the amount of sunlight reaching Earth. This reduction is on the order of about 0.1 percent between solar maximum and solar minimum, a variation that scientists measure with sensitive instruments but that has negligible direct effects on surface temperatures.

It is also true that historical periods of prolonged solar inactivity, most notably the Maunder Minimum (roughly 1645–1715), have been loosely correlated with cooler climatic periods. The Maunder Minimum coincided with the coldest part of the Little Ice Age, though the causal relationship between low solar activity and the Little Ice Age remains debated among climate scientists, with volcanic activity and ocean circulation changes likely playing larger roles.

So the factual kernel of the claim—that the sun had entered a minimum phase in 2020—was accurate. What the claim got wrong was the significance of that phase.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes's fact-check, published May 20, 2020, by staff writer Jessica Lee, correctly identified the claim as "Mostly False" and provided a thorough examination of the underlying solar physics. The article properly separated the two components of the claim: the factual observation that the sun was in a minimum phase, and the false assertion that this minimum would cause freezing weather, famine, and earthquakes on a scale threatening life on Earth.

Snopes's sourcing was solid. The article consulted NASA's educational materials on solar activity, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's solar cycle predictions, and direct correspondence with Dr. Tony Phillips. The piece correctly explained the 11-year solar cycle, the distinction between solar maximum and minimum, and the types of effects that solar minimums actually produce.

The Snopes rating of "Mostly False" was appropriate, as the claim contained a genuine factual observation wrapped in dramatic exaggeration. The "lockdown" metaphor itself received appropriate scrutiny, with Snopes noting that while the term was evocative, it fundamentally misrepresented the nature of solar minimums as normal, expected phenomena rather than unusual events.

Where Snopes's treatment could be extended is in exploring the broader ecosystem that made this claim virulent. The COVID-19 pandemic context—the reason "lockdown" resonated—deserves deeper analysis than the article provided. The claim did not emerge in a vacuum; it was part of a wave of pandemic-era misinformation that sought to draw cosmic parallels to human confinement.

Additionally, the claim's longevity and recurring nature merit examination. Solar minimums reliably trigger similar headlines every 11 years, often with the same exaggerated forecasts of global cooling and agricultural collapse. The New York Post article itself was based on earlier coverage in the British tabloid The Sun (fittingly named) and other outlets that have recycled the "sun lockdown" or "sun going quiet" narrative during every solar minimum since the early 2000s. Snopes's article treated the claim as a discrete event, but it belongs to a recurring genre.

Snopes also did not address the claim's relationship to broader climate misinformation. The suggestion that reduced solar activity could offset or reverse global warming has been a persistent theme in climate skepticism, and the "sun lockdown" narrative provided a superficially scientific basis for this claim. While Snopes is not obligated to address every downstream implication of a claim, the climate context adds meaningful depth to the analysis.

Finally, the Snopes article did not explore the relationship between solar activity and earthquakes, one of the more extraordinary subclaims in the original post. The claim that reduced solar activity could trigger seismic events has no support in geophysical literature, and its inclusion in the original narrative is a clear marker of pseudoscience. Explicitly addressing why this connection has no scientific basis would have strengthened the fact-check.

## 4. Why This Matters

The "sun lockdown" claim matters beyond its immediate falsity because it represents a significant category of misinformation that exploits genuine science to manufacture fear. The pattern is consistent: take a real but mundane scientific observation, exaggerate its implications, attach apocalyptic consequences, and watch it spread.

This pattern has concrete costs. Every solar minimum, resources are wasted debunking the same exaggerated claims. Public understanding of solar physics remains confused because sensational coverage drowns out accurate science communication. And crucially, the "sun is going quiet" narrative feeds into climate misinformation by suggesting that natural solar variation could be responsible for or could counteract human-caused climate change—a position that the overwhelming consensus of climate science rejects.

The epidemiological context of 2020 made the claim particularly damaging. In a period of maximum public anxiety, misinformation that suggested cosmic forces were aligning with the pandemic created a sense of inevitability and helplessness. The "sun lockdown" was not just wrong; it was wrong in a way that amplified despair and undermined the rational assessment of risk.

There is also a deeper epistemological issue: the claim reveals how science communication can fail when journalists prioritize narrative resonance over accuracy. The New York Post article's headline succeeded because "lockdown" was the word of the moment, not because it accurately described solar physics. This is a pattern that repeats across many domains of science journalism, where metaphor and analogy are chosen for their emotional impact rather than their precision.

The claim also illustrates the challenge of correcting misinformation about naturally cyclical phenomena. Because solar minimums are real, and because they recur every 11 years, the debunking of the 2020 claim does not prevent the same narrative from resurfacing in 2031, 2042, and beyond. Each cycle brings a new generation of media consumers and a new opportunity for sensational coverage. The structural incentives that produce such coverage—the premium placed on novel, alarming headlines—remain unchanged.

The economic dimensions of the claim are also worth considering. In 2020, the pandemic had already caused massive economic dislocation; adding the specter of solar-induced famine and freezing weather created a compounding sense of crisis that could influence everything from commodity markets to insurance underwriting. While it is difficult to measure the direct economic impact of a specific misinformation narrative, the aggregate effect of climate and environmental misinformation on decision-making is measurable and significant. The "sun lockdown" claim contributed to a broader ecology of doomism that undercuts both rational risk assessment and constructive action.

There is also a generational aspect to consider. Each solar cycle produces a new cohort of media consumers who have not seen the previous iteration of the same story. The 2020 narrative targeted a generation that had never experienced the 2008–2009 solar minimum coverage, nor the 1990s coverage before that. This demographic amnesia means that news organizations can recycle the same sensational framing every 11 years without their audience recognizing it as old content freshly repackaged.

For the Truth Vault, this entry serves as a reference point for a recurring class of misinformation: the catastrophic-solar-event narrative. Future iterations of similar claims—whether about solar flares, coronal mass ejections, or solar minimums—can be mapped against the baseline established here. By documenting the 2020 iteration in detail, we provide a resource for researchers who will encounter the same narrative in future cycles.

## 5. Research Notes

The primary source for the fact-check portion is Snopes's own investigation, which drew on NASA's educational materials about solar activity, NOAA's solar cycle prediction panel, and direct correspondence with Dr. Tony Phillips of Spaceweather.com. These sources are authoritative within their respective domains.

For supplementary context on the Maunder Minimum and the Little Ice Age, the paleoclimate record shows that the correlation between solar activity and surface temperature during this period is weak compared to volcanic and ocean-circulation factors. Contemporary climate models that incorporate solar forcing consistently show that even a prolonged Grand Solar Minimum would offset at most 0.3°C of warming—far less than the 1.2°C of anthropogenic warming observed since the Industrial Revolution.

The New York Post article that sparked the viral wave was published on May 14, 2020, and credited to staff. It drew on a May 2020 press release or coverage from The Sun (UK), which itself was recycling a narrative that appears near the end of every solar cycle. A Nexis search for the phrase "sun lockdown" shows spikes in 2008–2009 (the minimum between Cycles 23 and 24), 2019–2020 (the minimum between Cycles 24 and 25), and will likely show another in the early 2030s.

The earthquake claim—that solar minimums trigger seismic activity—warrants brief additional examination. The hypothesis that solar activity could influence earthquakes has been proposed by a fringe of researchers but has consistently failed to survive rigorous testing. The US Geological Survey maintains that no credible mechanism connects solar variability to earthquake initiation, and no statistical relationship between solar cycle phase and seismic frequency has been demonstrated in the peer-reviewed literature. The inclusion of this subclaim in the original viral post is a reliable indicator that the author was drawing on pseudoscientific rather than scientific sources.

For those interested in the actual science of solar minimums, NASA's "Solar Minimum Is Coming" article (2017) and NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center provide accessible, authoritative information. Glyn Pickett's research on the solar cycle's effects on Earth's atmosphere offers a deeper technical background.

A methodological note: the Snopes fact-check relies heavily on direct correspondence with a single scientist (Dr. Phillips) and NASA/NOAA educational materials. While these sources are credible, future investigations might also consult the Solar Influences Data Analysis Center (SIDC) at the Royal Observatory of Belgium, which maintains the definitive sunspot count database, or the peer-reviewed literature on solar-terrestrial interactions published in journals such as Solar Physics and the Journal of Geophysical Research: Space Physics.

## 6. Verdict

The claim that the sun entered a "lockdown" period in 2020 that threatened life on Earth with famine, earthquakes, and freezing weather is **mostly false**. The factual foundation—that the sun was in a solar minimum in 2019–2020—is correct, but the catastrophic implications attributed to this routine astronomical event are entirely unsupported.

The 11-year solar cycle is a normal, well-understood phenomenon. Solar minimums occur predictably and have been observed for centuries. While they produce real effects at the level of space weather—including changes in atmospheric drag, cosmic ray flux, and geomagnetic activity—they do not threaten human civilization with famine, cold, or seismic disaster.

The "lockdown" metaphor was misleading by design. It created a false equivalence between a human public health response to a novel virus and a natural astrophysical cycle that has operated for billions of years. The term's emotional resonance during the COVID-19 pandemic amplified the claim's spread but did not make it more accurate.

The claim's persistence across multiple solar cycles suggests that it fills a psychological need for cosmic-scale explanations of terrestrial anxiety. Like many forms of misinformation, it succeeds not because it is convincing on its merits but because it provides a narrative that feels meaningful at times of uncertainty. The correct response is not to mock those who share these claims but to provide clear, patient explanations of the underlying science—and to recognize that the same narrative will likely resurface when the sun next approaches its minimum.
