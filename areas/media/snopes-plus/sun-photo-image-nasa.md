# Close-up image of the sun is not NASA photo. Here's the real story


## 1. The Claim

In March 2025, an image captioned as "the most clearest picture of the sun ... Taken by NASA" attracted more than 266,000 reactions on Facebook and propagated across Instagram, Threads, X, and YouTube. The image showed what appeared to be an extreme close-up of the solar surface: swirling red, orange, and white structures following coherent curved patterns that, under close examination, appeared to trace something like magnetic field lines across the stellar disk. The visual impression was striking — simultaneously alien and orderly, dramatically different from both the featureless pale disk of ordinary naked-eye sun observation and the grainy or pixellated telescope photographs most people have seen. The image's visual quality and the confidence of its captioning made it widely shared and widely believed.

Snopes investigated and rated the claim Miscaptioned. NASA Heliophysics Public Affairs confirmed in email to Snopes that the image was "not a NASA image," noting that "any official NASA news or imagery will be shared via an official agency communications channel." A reverse image search using Google Lens traced the image to astrophotographer Jason Guenzel (@thevastreaches), who had originally posted it to Instagram on January 12, 2021 — more than four years before the 2025 viral wave. Guenzel confirmed to Snopes directly that he created the image, that it was not an unedited photograph, and that he had applied heavy digital processing to "accentuate features that follow the magnetic field lines of the Sun" as "a creative decision to make these features more visible and prominent." The versions circulating on social media in 2025 showed evidence of additional processing beyond Guenzel's original: exaggerated color balance in the red-orange spectrum, and in some instances the image had been mirrored — a technique sometimes used to evade reverse-image-search detection.

The attribution claim — that this is NASA imagery — is false. The image is the work of a named astrophotographer who described it as a stylized artistic rendering. Snopes' Miscaptioned verdict is accurate. But the fact-check, in correctly resolving the attribution question, leaves unaddressed the more scientifically generative question the case raises: what is the actual epistemological relationship between Guenzel's artistic astrophotography and NASA's own published solar imagery? The answer is that the distinction between them is not "processing versus no processing" — since all solar imaging involves extensive processing — but something subtler and more important: the difference between processing that preserves quantitative physical information and processing that distorts it for aesthetic effect.


## 2. What's Actually True

The factual resolution of the attribution claim is clear. Four independent lines of evidence confirm that the image is Guenzel's and not NASA's:

1. **Institutional denial:** NASA's Heliophysics Public Affairs Office stated directly that the image was not a NASA product and confirmed the agency's practice of distributing official imagery exclusively through official agency channels.

2. **Reverse image search provenance:** Google Lens identified the image's original posting to Guenzel's Instagram account on January 12, 2021, well before the 2025 viral cycle.

3. **Creator confirmation:** Guenzel confirmed his authorship directly to Snopes and explicitly described the processing methodology: "I took this opportunity to heavily process the resulting image into a stylized rendition of the visible surface of the sun." He described the result as a "creative decision" rather than a scientific representation.

4. **Additional manipulation evidence:** The social media versions showed evidence of subsequent processing beyond Guenzel's original — altered color balance and mirroring — suggesting that at least some of the 2025 viral copies were further degraded from the already-stylized original.

The image is derived from a real telescope photograph of the sun taken by Guenzel under admitted atmospheric conditions he described as "sub-par." The underlying observation is authentic — a real telescope pointed at the real sun — but the visual appearance of the circulating image results primarily from post-processing decisions made for aesthetic rather than scientific reasons. The solar surface features Guenzel chose to accentuate — structures following magnetic field line orientations — are real features of the photosphere, but the rendering makes them far more dramatic and clearly defined than the original poor-conditions observation supported, and adds colorization that has no scientific basis in standard solar photometry.

Snopes' Miscaptioned verdict is accurate in both its components: the image is incorrectly attributed (to NASA rather than to Guenzel) and the depiction may be somewhat misleading (a stylized artistic rendering of solar features rather than a scientific instrument product). For practical purposes — telling audiences whether this is really a NASA photograph — the verdict fully resolves the claim.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

Snopes correctly identifies the misattribution, but the deeper epistemological issue — what separates Guenzel's artistic image from legitimate scientific solar imagery, and what real NASA solar images actually look like — is not addressed. This matters because the most common public response to the Miscaptioned verdict will be to update the belief "this is a NASA photograph" to "this is not a NASA photograph" without understanding what the NASA alternative actually looks like or why it looks different.

The critical insight is that all published solar images involve significant post-processing. This is not a minor technical aside; it is a fundamental feature of solar (and, more broadly, astronomical) imaging that is almost never communicated in public-facing science content. NASA's own flagship solar imaging mission, the Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO), produces images that are false-color composites constructed from measurements at extreme ultraviolet (EUV) and UV wavelengths — wavelengths entirely invisible to human eyes. The SDO's Atmospheric Imaging Assembly (AIA) captures full-disk images every 12 seconds in 10 separate wavelength channels, each corresponding to plasma at a different temperature range, from approximately 60,000 K (chromospheric plasma visible at 1600 Å) to approximately 20 million K (flare plasma visible at 131 Å) (Pesnell et al., 2012, "The Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO)," *Solar Physics*, 275(1-2), 3–15). None of these wavelengths is visible to unaided human vision; all SDO images circulating in media are false-color representations in which colors are assigned to wavelength channels by convention.

When NASA publishes a striking image of a solar prominence, active region, or coronal mass ejection, that image has been: calibrated from raw detector counts to absolute flux values; corrected for instrumental artifacts (flat-fielding, dark-current subtraction, cosmic ray removal); assigned false colors corresponding to its wavelength channel; possibly composited across multiple wavelength channels with different color assignments; and brightness-scaled to optimize human visual perception of the features of interest. Each of these steps involves choices — which wavelengths to blend, what color to assign each wavelength, what brightness scale to use — that are made by scientists and imaging specialists for a combination of scientific clarity and aesthetic communication value.

The meaningful distinction between this legitimate scientific processing and Guenzel's artistic processing is not whether processing occurred — both are heavily processed — but whether the processing preserves or distorts the physical information content. SDO false-color composites accurately represent the spatial distribution of plasma at different temperatures: the blue regions in a 171 Å EUV composite genuinely correspond to coronal plasma at approximately 600,000 K; the gold regions in a 304 Å composite genuinely correspond to chromospheric and transition-region plasma at 50,000 K; the spatial relationships between these features are preserved at the SDO's ~700 km spatial resolution. The color assignments are arbitrary conventions but the underlying spatial and thermal information is faithfully rendered (Lemen et al., 2012, "The Atmospheric Imaging Assembly (AIA) on the Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO)," *Solar Physics*, 275(1-2), 17–40).

Guenzel's processing, by his own description, began with an observation of acknowledged "sub-par" quality and applied aesthetic amplification to make features "more visible and prominent" as a "creative decision." The features his processing highlighted are real solar structures — photospheric granulation and magnetic flux concentrations do organize along field line geometries — but the rendering dramatically exceeds what the underlying observation justified. The 2025 social media versions introduced further arbitrary color shifts and spatial transformations (mirroring) that have no informational justification whatsoever.

The distinction can be stated precisely: NASA's false-color solar imagery is a scientifically faithful representation of physical reality encoded in non-visible-light wavelengths, converted into human-visible display with preserved spatial and thermodynamic information. Guenzel's imagery is a visually calibrated image of one real observation, dramatically enhanced to achieve aesthetic effect, with the rendering choices driven by artistic rather than scientific criteria. Both involve processing. One preserves physical information content; the other distorts it. The former is science communication; the latter is astrophotographic art. Neither is "raw" in any meaningful sense — but they are different in what they preserve.

This distinction is rarely articulated in science communication, partly because the standard popular framing of "real" vs. "fake" images does not accommodate the spectrum from raw scientific data to artistic interpretation. Audiences who accept NASA's false-color EUV composites as "real solar images" are applying an implicit norm that processing is acceptable when the source is institutional and the purpose is scientific — but they often cannot articulate the norm, making it unavailable to apply in novel cases like Guenzel's.

The Parker Solar Probe mission illustrates the character of cutting-edge solar science in practice. Fox et al. (2016, "The Solar Probe Plus Mission: Humanity's First Visit to Our Star," *Space Science Reviews*, 204(1-4), 7–48) described a spacecraft designed to approach within 9.86 solar radii of the sun's center — closer than any previously constructed object — equipped primarily with particle detectors, magnetometers, and plasma analyzers rather than conventional imaging systems. The Parker Solar Probe does not take conventional photographs of the solar surface in the mode most people imagine; it measures particle fluxes, magnetic field vectors, and plasma wave properties in situ in the corona. The publicly circulated images associated with Parker Solar Probe announcements are largely from supporting Earth-orbit instruments (principally the SDO and STEREO missions), processed exactly as described above.

Solar Orbiter (Müller et al., 2020, "The Solar Orbiter mission," *Astronomy & Astrophysics*, 642, A1), the ESA/NASA mission launched in February 2020 and currently in its nominal science phase, carries a High Resolution Imager (HRIEUV) with a planned spatial resolution of approximately 200 km at perihelion, making it the highest-resolution solar EUV instrument currently operating. Solar Orbiter's first perihelion images, published in July 2020, revealed "campfires" — small-scale coronal brightening events at spatial scales below the resolution of previous instruments. These images are genuine frontier science; they are also false-color composites at 174 Å (roughly corresponding to ~1 million K plasma) and significantly post-processed to optimize scientific content visibility. They are not as immediately visually dramatic as Guenzel's image, because their design priorities are scientific rather than aesthetic.

This contrast is epistemically important: the actual highest-science-value solar images are often visually unassuming compared to artistic astrophotography, precisely because scientific imaging is constrained by instrumental characteristics, wavelength selection, and physical accuracy requirements that prioritize information fidelity over visual drama. The images that go viral — Guenzel's artistically processed visible-light composition; highly colorized SDO composites — are optimized for human visual impact. Real science images are optimized for measurement fidelity. The two optimization targets sometimes produce similar-looking results and sometimes produce dramatically different ones.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** Miscaptioned  
**Confidence:** High (direct creator confirmation; NASA institutional denial; provenance trace to original 2021 Instagram post)  
**Epistemic Basis:** Institutional attribution verification, reverse image search, direct creator communication  
**Snopes verdict:** Miscaptioned

The image is not a NASA photograph. It is an artistically processed solar photograph by astrophotographer Jason Guenzel, who explicitly described the processing as a creative stylistic decision made under conditions of poor observational data quality. Social media versions circulating in 2025 were further altered with additional color processing and spatial transformations applied after the original post. The NASA attribution is false.

The underlying observation is a real solar photograph, and the features the processing accentuates (magnetic-field-following structures in the photosphere) are real solar phenomena. The miscaptioning involves false institutional attribution and misleading representation of the image's scientific precision status. It is not, however, a fabrication: unlike AI-generated solar imagery, Guenzel's image is derived from direct telescopic observation of the actual sun, processed artistically rather than generated computationally.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The sun has been continuously monitored by space-based instruments for more than three decades, and the scientific understanding of solar structure, dynamics, and magnetism is among the most precise in astrophysics. The gap between this precision and public understanding of solar imaging — including what NASA's solar instruments actually produce and how those images relate to visual reality — is substantial and has meaningful consequences for science communication.

**The solar atmosphere — layered structure and temperatures:** The solar surface visible in conventional optical photographs is the photosphere: a layer approximately 500 km thick from which most visible-wavelength photons escape to space. The photosphere has a characteristic granular texture produced by convective cells (granules) approximately 1,000 km in diameter that persist for 10–20 minutes, carrying heat from the convection zone below. Above the photosphere is the chromosphere (temperatures increasing from ~7,500 K to ~20,000 K over 2,000 km), followed by the transition region (temperatures rising sharply from ~20,000 K to ~500,000 K over a few hundred km), and above that the corona (1–3 million K). This temperature inversion — the corona being hundreds of times hotter than the photosphere it overlies — is known as the coronal heating problem and remains one of the primary unsolved problems in solar physics. Parker Solar Probe measurements are contributing in situ data relevant to two leading mechanisms: magnetic reconnection events and Alfvénic wave turbulence (Bale et al., 2019, "Highly structured slow solar wind emerging from an equatorial coronal hole," *Nature*, 576(7786), 237–242).

**What NASA's SDO actually produces:** The SDO produces approximately 1.5 terabytes of data per day — more than any previous science satellite in history at the time of its launch. Its image archive, now exceeding 350 terabytes, constitutes the most detailed observational record of any star in the universe. All imagery published from the SDO for public consumption has been processed from raw detector data through the pipeline described above: calibrated to absolute values, artifact-corrected, false-colored by wavelength, and typically brightness-scaled. A composite SDO image combining 171 Å (blue, ~600,000 K), 193 Å (gold, ~1.2 million K), and 211 Å (red, ~2 million K) appears dramatically colorful and detailed — but its colors are assigned by convention, not by any physical relationship to human-visible electromagnetic radiation from those structures.

None of this processing is improper. It is standard practice in astronomical imaging and is required to make physically meaningful data accessible through human visual channels. But it means that the naive expectation — "NASA's real solar photographs show what the sun actually looks like" — must be qualified: NASA's real solar data shows what physical solar phenomena look like when encoded in non-visible-light measurements and rendered in false color for human visual perception. This is genuinely what the sun looks like in those physical variables; it is not what the sun looks like to human eyes.

**Solar photography versus conventional astrophotography:** Conventional telescopic photography of the sun in visible light — the category in which Guenzel's work falls — requires specialized equipment (solar filters, often hydrogen-alpha or calcium K-line filters for chromospheric features, or white-light filters for photospheric granulation) and is most commonly practiced in the amateur astrophotography community. White-light solar photography shows the photosphere as a yellow-white disk with sunspots, limb darkening, and granular texture resolvable at sufficient aperture. Hydrogen-alpha photography (at 656.3 nm, the Balmer alpha transition) shows prominences, filaments, and spicules with dramatically different appearance from white-light images. These represent genuine solar photography — actual images of the sun at specific wavelengths — but they are categorically different from the false-color EUV imagery that constitutes most publicly circulated "NASA solar images."

Guenzel's image appears to be derived from white-light or hydrogen-alpha photography, then processed with artificial colorization and contrast enhancement to produce a visually dramatic result that conveys a scientific-looking but scientifically unspecified representation of the solar disk. For a viewer unfamiliar with the full processing stack that separates raw telescope images from published science products, Guenzel's image is visually indistinguishable from a legitimate heavily processed NASA product. This is a common failure mode in misattribution: not deliberate fabrication but visual indistinguishability between artistically processed and scientifically processed imagery.

**The misattribution pipeline:** The mechanics by which Guenzel's 2021 image became viral NASA-attributed content in 2025 illustrate the standard viral misattribution pipeline. An visually striking image — detached from its original attribution context by stripping or ignoring creator metadata — enters circulation and attracts resharing. A resharer, seeking to increase credibility and engagement, attaches a high-prestige institutional attribution (NASA is the most common attribution for dramatic space and astronomical imagery). The attributed image propagates faster than the unattributed original because institutional prestige increases click probability and decreases skepticism. By the time the image reaches its 266,000-reaction virality, the attribution has been repeated often enough to function as apparent social proof.

The deliberate mirroring of some viral versions — identified by Snopes — suggests at least some versions were specifically modified to evade reverse-image search, indicating intentional deception beyond naive misattribution. This places the 2025 circulation somewhere on the spectrum between innocent misattribution and active disinformation, though the most common mechanism remains innocent resharing of previously misattributed copies.

**Solar research frontiers:** Active solar physics research is focused on three interconnected problems: the coronal heating problem (why the corona is millions of degrees hotter than the photosphere), the solar wind acceleration problem (how the quiet-sun solar wind is accelerated to several hundred kilometers per second), and solar activity prediction (forecasting flares and coronal mass ejections with lead times sufficient for operational space weather response). Parker Solar Probe measurements through its close perihelion passes have yielded in situ observations of magnetic "switchbacks" — rapid reversals in the radial magnetic field — whose contribution to coronal heating is under active investigation (Bale et al., 2019, cited above). Solar Orbiter's coordinated remote-sensing and in situ measurements are designed to address the same questions from a different vantage point and at different solar latitudes than Parker Solar Probe's ecliptic orbit.

Real imagery from both missions tends to look quite different from Guenzel's dramatic composition: Parker Solar Probe's WISPR imager captures wide-field views of the corona in visible light that appear as diffuse structured brightness gradients, not the dramatic close-up surface features that make the viral image visually striking. Solar Orbiter's HRIEUV images show highly structured, physically meaningful, but visually modest (low dynamic range, single false-color wavelength) renderings of active regions and campfires. The striking visual drama of Guenzel's image is a product of his artistic processing, not a characteristic of scientifically valuable solar imagery.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes' handling of this case is methodologically sound. Three independent verification steps were employed: institutional denial from NASA Heliophysics Public Affairs (the most direct possible source for attribution verification); reverse image search establishing provenance; and direct creator confirmation establishing not only authorship but processing methodology. The Miscaptioned rating accurately captures the dual error: false attribution to NASA and misleading representation of the image's scientific status.

The limitation is scope: Snopes answers "is this NASA's image?" and correctly says no. It does not address "what does NASA's equivalent imagery actually look like?" or "why does Guenzel's processed image differ in an important scientific way from NASA's processed imagery?" These contextual questions are outside the operational scope of viral fact-checking but represent the most scientifically valuable aspects of the case.

For viewers who receive the Miscaptioned verdict and update only the attribution belief — now knowing the image is not NASA's — without updating their understanding of what NASA solar imagery actually is, the correction has limited epistemological value. They will now accurately know that this specific image is not NASA's, but they will remain without a framework for evaluating future solar imagery claims. The deeper update — that all published solar imagery involves extensive processing, and that the meaningful distinction is between scientifically faithful and artistically distorted processing — requires more context than a standard verdict can provide.

AP Fact Check, Science Feedback, and lead Snopes rivals have addressed related cases involving James Webb Space Telescope images, where AI-generated fakes visually resemble authentic JWST products, raising the same boundary problem in a different domain. Science Feedback in particular has developed the concept of "authentic but misleading" categorization — images that are genuine photographs but are rendered or captioned in ways that give false impressions of their scientific status — which is directly applicable to the Guenzel case. Extending this conceptual framework to solar astrophotography would improve the quality of fact-checking in this domain.

The underlying problem is that both scientific and artistic solar imagery require heavy processing, and the processing itself does not distinguish them. Attribution by creator alone (NASA vs. Guenzel) is necessary but not fully sufficient for public understanding: audiences who know only the attribution fact still lack the background to evaluate the scientific status of similar images in the future. Building that background requires explaining not just what was misattributed but what the correctly attributed versions of comparable solar imagery actually look like — and why they look different.
