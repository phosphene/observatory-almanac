# Sunscreen chemicals absorbed into the bloodstream cause cancer


## 1. The Claim

A more specific variant of sunscreen safety concern — distinct from the general "sunscreen causes cancer" claim — focuses on the pharmacokinetic finding that organic sunscreen filters are absorbed through the skin and enter systemic circulation. The claim runs as follows:

**The pharmacokinetic premise**: When you apply sunscreen to your skin, the active chemical ingredients — particularly oxybenzone (benzophenone-3), avobenzone, octocrylene, octisalate, homosalate, and octinoxate — don't stay on the skin surface. They penetrate the stratum corneum (the outermost skin layer) and enter the bloodstream.

**The harm inference**: Because these chemicals reach systemic circulation, they are "absorbed into your body" in a way that differs from topical-only products. Since several of these compounds have shown hormonal activity in laboratory settings, since oxybenzone in particular has been detected in blood, urine, and breast milk, and since some have been found in bloodstream concentrations that exceed the FDA's threshold for automatic systemic safety determination, they may cause cancer or endocrine disruption through internal exposure.

**The actionable conclusion**: You should replace chemical/organic sunscreens with mineral alternatives (zinc oxide, titanium dioxide) that don't absorb into the bloodstream, or avoid sunscreen entirely in favor of sun avoidance and clothing.

This specific absorption variant of the sunscreen cancer claim gained substantial momentum following two FDA-commissioned clinical studies published in the *Journal of the American Medical Association* in 2019 and 2020 (temporarily attributed to researchers Matta et al.). These studies confirmed for the first time in controlled conditions that multiple organic sunscreen filters reach systemic concentrations exceeding 0.5 ng/mL — the FDA's threshold above which additional toxicological data is needed. The studies generated widespread media coverage including CNN headlines describing sunscreen chemicals "seeping into the bloodstream at potentially unsafe levels," triggering significant consumer concern.

Separate but related concerns involve oxybenzone's detection in breast milk (Schlumpf, M. et al., "Endocrine Active UV Filters: Developmental Toxicity and Exposure Through Breast Milk," *CHIMIA International Journal for Chemistry*, 62(5), 2008), its presence in amniotic fluid and cord blood (Schlumpf, M. et al., "Exposure patterns of UV filters, fragrances, parabens, phthalates, organochlor pesticides, PBDEs, and PCBs in human milk," *Chemosphere*, 2010), and its detection in biological samples from male sunscreen users (Frederiksen, H. et al., "UV-filters in sunscreens and their effects on sperm cell function," *Endocrine Society*, 2016). These findings are presented as evidence of bioaccumulative or persistent systemic exposure creating cancer risk.

## 2. What's Actually True

**The absorption finding is real.** The Matta et al. (2019) study, published in *JAMA*, enrolled 24 healthy volunteers who applied one of four commercially available sunscreen formulations (two sprays, one lotion, one cream) under maximal use conditions — 2 mg/cm² applied to 75% of total body surface area four times daily for four days, simulating a day at the beach repeated over four days. Blood plasma was sampled and analyzed for six active sunscreen ingredients. All six compounds — avobenzone, oxybenzone, octocrylene, homosalate, octisalate, and octinoxate — exceeded 0.5 ng/mL in plasma samples. In the follow-up Matta et al. (2020) *JAMA* study expanding to 48 participants with additional formulations, oxybenzone reached mean plasma concentrations exceeding 200 ng/mL — hundreds of times above the 0.5 ng/mL threshold.

**The 0.5 ng/mL threshold is not a safety limit.** This is the most important clarification for interpreting these studies. The FDA's 0.5 ng/mL threshold is not a level above which harm occurs. It is the concentration above which the FDA requires additional systemic toxicity testing before confirming GRASE (Generally Recognized as Safe and Effective) status under current OTC drug regulations. The threshold was established as a conservative, precautionary trigger for further data gathering — not as evidence of danger. The FDA studies showed that more data is needed, not that the current concentrations cause harm. The FDA's own accompanying statement on the 2019 Matta study explicitly said: "These findings do not mean that individuals should refrain from using sunscreen."

**What the plasma concentrations mean in context.** Oxybenzone's 200+ ng/mL plasma concentration in maximal use conditions sounds alarming but requires pharmacological context. First, maximal use conditions (75% body surface, four applications daily, four days) substantially exceed typical real-world sunscreen use. Second, concentration alone does not predict effect — the biological activity of a compound at a given concentration depends on receptor affinity, tissue distribution, metabolic clearance rate, and the biological endpoint of interest. Third, comparison with pharmacologically active compounds provides context: aspirin's therapeutic plasma concentration is around 150,000 ng/mL — 750 times higher than oxybenzone's maximal-use level. Most prescription medications operate at plasma concentrations in the microgram per milliliter range — thousands to tens of thousands of times higher than oxybenzone levels from sunscreen use.

**The endocrine disruption evidence: what it shows and what it doesn't.** The most substantive scientific concern about oxybenzone is its activity at sex hormone receptors. Multiple in vitro studies (Schlumpf, M. et al., "In vitro and in vivo estrogenicity of UV screens," *Environmental Health Perspectives*, 109(3), 2001; Krause, M. et al., "Sunscreens: Are they beneficial for health? An overview of endocrine disrupting properties of UV-filters," *International Journal of Andrology*, 2012; Nakagawa, Y. & Suzuki, T., "The metabolism and cytotoxicity of 2-hydroxy-4-methoxybenzophenone in isolated rat hepatocytes and the xenoestrogenic activity in MCF-7 human breast cancer cells," *Chemico-Biological Interactions*, 2002) have documented oxybenzone's ability to bind estrogen receptors and influence estrogen-sensitive gene expression in cell culture systems. Animal studies have found effects on reproductive development when rodents are exposed to oxybenzone at high doses. This is real laboratory evidence of hormonal activity.

However, connecting this laboratory evidence to human cancer causation requires several inferential steps that the evidence does not yet support: (1) the in vitro concentrations at which oxybenzone shows receptor activity are typically orders of magnitude higher than the plasma concentrations achieved through sunscreen use; (2) receptor binding in cell culture does not reliably predict systemic endocrine effects in complex organisms with regulatory feedback systems; (3) human epidemiological evidence linking sunscreen use to hormone-sensitive cancers (breast, endometrial, ovarian, prostate) does not exist — if anything, sunscreen users tend to have better health outcomes in these studies due to confounding with health-conscious behavior; and (4) the animal studies showing reproductive effects used oxybenzone doses that cannot be extrapolated to human sunscreen exposure levels without substantial uncertainty.

**Oxybenzone detection in breast milk, amniotic fluid, and semen.** These biomonitoring findings are genuine — oxybenzone has been detected in human breast milk at concentrations ranging from 0.03 to 267.5 ng/mL (Schlumpf, M. et al., *Chemosphere*, 2010), in amniotic fluid and cord blood (slightly lower levels), and in semen. These detections confirm that oxybenzone undergoes transplacental and mammary gland transfer, which means fetuses and breastfed infants may be exposed through maternal sunscreen use. This is biologically significant because early developmental periods may involve greater sensitivity to hormonal disruption than adult life. However, the finding that exposure occurs is not the same as the finding that harm results. No study has demonstrated adverse reproductive or cancer outcomes in children born to sunscreen-using mothers or in individuals whose mothers used sunscreen while breastfeeding.

**Mineral sunscreens and the alternative.** The two FDA-designated GRASE sunscreen active ingredients — zinc oxide and titanium dioxide — work primarily through physical UV reflection rather than absorption and chemical energy dissipation. These compounds do not meaningfully penetrate the stratum corneum as nanoparticles; multiple studies have confirmed that zinc oxide and titanium dioxide remain on the skin surface with negligible transdermal absorption. For consumers who prefer to minimize systemic absorption of sunscreen ingredients, mineral sunscreens offer an evidence-supported alternative. The protective effectiveness of mineral sunscreens is somewhat different from organic filters — zinc oxide provides broad UVA and UVB coverage, titanium dioxide is stronger in UVB — but appropriately formulated mineral sunscreens offering SPF-30+ protection are available and effective.

**No current evidence that absorbed sunscreen causes cancer.** Despite the genuine pharmacokinetic data and the legitimate regulatory data gap, no study has demonstrated that sunscreen chemical absorption causes cancer in human populations. Given the scale of sunscreen use globally — billions of people, cumulative use over decades — if organic sunscreen filters caused meaningful cancer risk through systemic absorption, epidemiological signals would be expected to emerge in cancer registries, healthcare utilization data, or large-scale cohort studies. No such signal exists. Cancer registry data in populations with high sunscreen penetration (Australia, Scandinavia, the United Kingdom) do not show elevated hormone-sensitive cancer rates attributable to sunscreen use, and the UV-protective benefits in these populations are documented.

## 3. Why People Believe This

**The logical plausibility of absorption → harm.** The folk reasoning is straightforward and not inherently irrational: if a compound is absorbed into the body and reaches systemic circulation, it has the potential to interact with biological systems in harmful ways that topically restricted compounds cannot. This is the principle behind drug delivery systems — transdermal patches work precisely because absorption into the bloodstream is achievable. The leap from "it reaches the bloodstream" to "it might cause harm" is cognitively natural even when the toxicological evidence required to establish harm is absent.

**The FDA study headlines.** Media coverage of the Matta et al. (2019) and (2020) JAMA studies provided exact fodder for the absorption-to-harm narrative. CNN's widely shared headline "Sunscreen ingredients can seep into bloodstream, study says" was accurate in its narrow factual claim but misled by failing to convey what the finding meant in regulatory context. Readers encountered the news that sunscreen chemicals "seep into the bloodstream" without the essential clarification that this triggers a data collection requirement rather than establishing safety concern. The gap between the accurate narrow claim and its public interpretation reflects systemic problems in science communication, not necessarily media bad faith.

**Breast milk and fetal exposure as emotional amplifiers.** The finding that oxybenzone reaches breast milk and amniotic fluid dramatically amplifies the claim's emotional salience. Harm to infants and fetuses constitutes the most concerning category of chemical exposure in public risk perception research; Paul Slovic's risk perception data shows that exposures involving children, invisible risks, and involuntary exposure receive heightened fear responses. The breast milk finding transforms sunscreen from a personal risk question to a parental responsibility question, which fundamentally changes how it is processed emotionally even without changing the underlying evidence status.

**The difficulty of proving chemical safety to a negative.** The precautionary principle, applied as "we cannot prove this won't harm you," shifts the burden of proof in ways that standard risk assessment frameworks don't accept but that many consumers find persuasive. The FDA's data gap request is accurate — there is genuinely insufficient pharmacokinetic safety data for most organic sunscreen filters under current regulatory standards. But "insufficient data" is not the same as "evidence of harm," and many consumers don't readily maintain this distinction. When expert consensus is "we don't have enough data," folk reasoning often resolves the uncertainty toward caution — which, in this case, means avoiding sunscreen, which increases UV exposure, which increases cancer risk.

**Wellness influencer amplification of regulatory uncertainty.** The FDA's proposed rule and the JAMA absorption studies were extensively amplified by wellness influencers, natural cosmetics brands, and anti-pharmaceutical health communities who have strong commercial and ideological incentives to encourage consumers to replace "chemical" sunscreens with "natural" mineral alternatives or with non-sunscreen sun protection approaches. This amplification is not purely information flow — it involves selective emphasis, the conflation of "more data needed" with "dangerous," and the promotion of alternative products without equivalent scrutiny of their evidence base. The market dimension matters: the clean beauty and mineral sunscreen markets stand to gain from consumer migration away from organic sunscreen filters.

**Prior cases where absorbed cosmetics caused harm.** Consumer anxiety about absorbed sunscreen compounds is not without historical precedent. Lead in cosmetics caused real harm before regulatory restriction. Certain topical hormones in cosmetics were absorbed and caused unintended hormonal effects, particularly in children. Topical antibiotics like neomycin cause widespread sensitization through skin absorption. The principle that topically applied compounds can cause systemic effects through absorption is empirically validated by historical examples, which creates rational grounds for asking about any topically applied compound: what are its systemic effects? That is precisely what the FDA's data gap closure process is designed to answer.

## 4. Verdict

**UNPROVEN (and currently contradicted by available evidence, though not definitively closed).** The specific claim that absorbed sunscreen chemicals cause cancer has no current evidentiary support, but it is grounded in real pharmacokinetic data and genuine regulatory uncertainty in ways that make a simple "False" inadequate.

**What is definitively established**: Organic sunscreen filters — particularly oxybenzone, avobenzone, and octocrylene — are absorbed through the skin and reach measurable systemic concentrations under maximal use conditions. Oxybenzone in particular reaches plasma levels that exceed the FDA's 0.5 ng/mL threshold for additional toxicological data, sometimes by orders of magnitude.

**What is not established**: That these systemic concentrations cause cancer, endocrine disruption, or any other adverse health outcome in human populations at real-world exposure levels. The in vitro hormonal activity documented for oxybenzone involves concentrations far exceeding what sunscreen use achieves systemically. No human epidemiological evidence links sunscreen use to increased cancer risk. The FDA's data gap exists because existing pharmacokinetic data was insufficient to confirm GRASE status under updated standards, not because evidence of harm was found.

**The correct risk calculus**: UV radiation from the sun, at proven carcinogen status (IARC Group 1), poses a definite and substantial cancer risk. Sunscreen reduces this risk through a mechanism with randomized controlled trial support showing 50–73% risk reduction for specific cancer types. The absorbed sunscreen chemicals pose a theoretically uncertain but currently undemonstrated risk. Under any reasonable risk calculus, continuing to use broad-spectrum sunscreen remains the evidence-supported choice.

**For consumers with genuine concerns**: The mineral sunscreen alternative is legitimate and evidence-supported. Zinc oxide and titanium dioxide provide effective broad-spectrum protection without meaningful systemic absorption, and the FDA has confirmed their GRASE status. When chemical absorption concerns are important to an individual, switching to mineral formulations captures sunscreen's protective benefit while reducing or eliminating systemic absorption of organic filter compounds. This is a rational precautionary choice even without evidence of harm from organic filters.

**The open regulatory question**: The FDA's data gap process should be resolved. Several European sunscreen filters with extensive real-world use records and better pharmacokinetic data than many US-approved filters could be evaluated and approved, providing consumers with more options. The FDA's ability to gather this data efficiently is a legitimate policy concern, and advocacy for faster regulatory review of sunscreen ingredients is well-founded — but it is distinct from the claim that current absorbed sunscreen compounds cause cancer.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**The FDA's OTC monograph system and its limitations.** Sunscreen is regulated in the United States as an over-the-counter drug under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, which requires that active ingredients be GRASE — Generally Recognized as Safe and Effective. The OTC monograph system, under which sunscreen approval occurs without individual drug applications, was designed to streamline market access for ingredients with long safety records. However, it was also established using a scientific standard appropriate to the 1970s-80s era, before modern pharmacokinetic techniques made systemic absorption readily measurable at parts-per-quadrillion levels. Modern analytical chemistry can detect compounds in biological samples at concentrations that would have been unmeasurable when sunscreen ingredients were originally evaluated. This creates a situation where compounds that were appropriately evaluated at their introduction now generate detectable absorption data that the original regulatory framework didn't anticipate, requiring the FDA to ask whether the original safety determination remains valid with more sensitive measurements.

**The Sunscreen Innovation Act of 2014 and its regulatory aftermath.** Congress passed the Sunscreen Innovation Act (SIA) specifically to accelerate FDA evaluation of eight additional sunscreen active ingredients — mostly UV filters developed in Europe and used globally — that sunscreen manufacturers had asked to have approved in the United States for years. The SIA established deadlines and procedures for FDA review. Despite the legislative push, most of these ingredients remained unapproved as of 2024 due to data gaps, administrative process, and resource constraints. The contrast between Europe's 27+ approved sunscreen filters and the United States' 17 (including the two mineral GRASE ingredients) reflects regulatory pathway differences, not systematically different safety profiles. This regulatory asymmetry is cited by dermatologists as a public health concern because some European-approved filters, particularly Tinosorb S and Tinosorb M, offer superior photostability and UVA protection compared to available US options.

**Oxybenzone and environmental policy.** The ban on oxybenzone and octinoxate in Hawaii (effective 2021) and similar legislation in Palau, Bonaire, the US Virgin Islands, and several other jurisdictions reflects the strongest evidence-basis in the sunscreen safety debate — not human harm, but ecosystem harm. At concentrations around 62 parts per trillion, oxybenzone has been shown to induce coral bleaching, interfere with coral larval settlement, and affect coral DNA. These sub-parts-per-billion environmental effects have no bearing on human cancer risk from sunscreen use (which would occur at plasma concentrations measured in nanograms per milliliter — hundreds of thousands of times higher per unit volume), but the environmental bans have been misrepresented in public discourse as evidence of human health danger.

**The biomonitoring revolution and its communication challenges.** Modern biomonitoring — the capacity to detect environmental chemical exposures in human biological samples at parts per trillion — has created a systematic challenge in environmental health communication. The detection of any compound in human blood, urine, or tissue is not equivalent to evidence of harm; it means that analytical sensitivity has advanced faster than toxicological understanding of low-level chronic exposures. Environmental health scientists like Philippe Grandjean (*Only One Chance*, 2013) have argued for precautionary approaches to detected biomonitoring levels before harm is established, given early developmental sensitivity. Others, like Gary Ginsberg and Brian Toal (*What's Toxic, What's Not*, 2006), have argued that communicating detection levels without dose-response context creates unjustified public anxiety. This debate about how to communicate biomonitoring data is directly relevant to the sunscreen absorption story: detection is real, harm is not established, and the communication challenge is how to convey that distinction to publics who reasonably want protective guidance.

**Historical dermatological attitudes toward sunscreen.** The mid-20th century dermatological community was divided about sunscreen's cancer prevention role, and some academic voices in the 1990s — including researchers like Marianne Berwick — argued that the epidemiological evidence for sunscreen's protective effect was weaker than assumed, and that disinhibition effects might offset UV reduction benefits. These concerns were carefully studied through subsequent research, including the Green et al. randomized controlled trial (Green, A.C. et al., "Daily sunscreen application and betacarotene supplementation in prevention of basal-cell and squamous-cell carcinomas of the skin," *The Lancet*, 354, 1999; and follow-up: Green, A.C. et al., "Reduced melanoma after regular sunscreen use: randomized trial follow-up," *Journal of Clinical Oncology*, 29(3), 2011), and definitively resolved in favor of sunscreen's protective efficacy. The earlier academic debates about sunscreen efficacy sometimes persist in lay understanding as evidence that "experts aren't sure sunscreen helps," when the current expert consensus is strongly supportive of sunscreen use.

**Paraben parallels: a cautionary example for the absorption debate.** Parabens — synthetic preservatives used in cosmetics and personal care products — underwent a similar scare cycle in the early 2000s after Darbre, P.D., Aljarrah, A., Miller, W.R., Coldham, N.G., Sauer, M.J., & Pope, G.S. ("Concentrations of parabens in human breast tumours," *Journal of Applied Toxicology*, 24(1), 2004) published a study detecting parabens in breast tumor tissue. Media amplified this as evidence that parabens in cosmetics caused breast cancer. Subsequent research showed that parabens were ubiquitous in the environment from food and other sources, that the concentration in breast tissue was not elevated compared to neighboring non-cancerous tissue, and that the estrogenic activity of parabens in cell culture occurred at concentrations orders of magnitude higher than cosmetic use levels. The resulting paraben scare drove a major "paraben-free" marketing wave without epidemiological evidence of harm — and replacements including phenoxyethanol and sodium benzoate have their own safety question profiles. The paraben cycle is a cautionary example for the oxybenzone debate: absorption detection, in vitro hormonal activity, and biomonitoring findings all occurred in the paraben case without establishing cancer causation.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Snopes coverage.** This specific claim — the absorbed-compounds-cause-cancer variant — is not addressed as a distinct fact-check by Snopes, which is why it carries `not-addressed` status in this entry. The general sunscreen-causes-cancer claim (covered in the companion Truth Vault entry) is rated "False" by Snopes, and that rating implicitly covers this variant, but the specific pharmacokinetic story — absorption is real, harm is uncertain, mineral alternatives exist — warrants separate treatment because its evidence structure is more nuanced than the benzene contamination or FDA-rule misinterpretation stories that the general Snopes piece addresses.

**Health Feedback and AFP Fact Check coverage.** Health Feedback, an IFCN-accredited fact-checking organization specializing in health information, has directly addressed the Matta et al. JAMA studies and reached a consistent conclusion: the clinical finding that organic sunscreen filters are absorbed into the bloodstream is accurate; the inference that this causes harm is unsubstantiated. AFP Fact Check has addressed similar viral claims, emphasizing the distinction between the FDA's data gap inquiry and an established finding of harm. These treatments are technically accurate but typically brief, and don't fully engage with the legitimate scientific debates about endocrine disruption, developmental exposure, or mineral sunscreen alternatives that give the claim its perceived credibility.

**Why the absorption variant warrants separate Truth Vault treatment.** The absorption-specific variant has different evidence characteristics from the general "sunscreen causes cancer" claim: it is anchored in real, peer-reviewed, FDA-commissioned clinical data published in JAMA, not in misrepresentation of contamination events or regulatory misinterpretation. It involves an active regulatory process with genuine data gaps. It has substantive in vitro evidence of hormonal activity (not cancer causation, but a mechanism worth scientific attention). And it has a legitimate policy-relevant alternative (mineral sunscreens) that consumers can reasonably prefer without clinical harm from their choice. Standard "True or False" fact-checking formats struggle with claims of this evidence structure, which is precisely the gap that the Truth Vault's `evidence-contested` gap category is designed to fill. A reader who encounters the claim that absorbed sunscreen causes cancer deserves to understand: what the absorption data actually shows, what it doesn't establish, what the regulatory process means, why in vitro endocrine data doesn't translate directly to harm, and what actionable alternatives exist for concerned consumers. That is a more complex information need than a binary rating satisfies.

**The EARC-C classification.** This entry is classified EARC-C (Evidence-Contested) rather than EARC-D (Demonstrably False) because the pharmacokinetic data is genuine, the regulatory uncertainty is real, and the in vitro hormonal activity evidence — while insufficient to establish harm — represents a legitimate scientific open question rather than a fabricated claim. The honest summary is: absorption is proven, harm is not, the FDA's regulatory response is appropriate, mineral alternatives are available, and continuing to use broad-spectrum sunscreen remains the best available protective choice for skin cancer prevention based on current evidence. Future research may shift this classification if either direction of evidence — either harm from systemic absorption or definitive long-term safety confirmation — becomes established.
