# Chemicals in Sunscreen Are Absorbed Into the Bloodstream and Potentially Harmful

## 1. The Claim

In the summer of 2020, a viral Facebook post by a Florida mother alleged that chemicals in her infant daughter's sunscreen had caused "weird episodes" of "zoning out" and "dropping of her head," which she interpreted as possible petite mal seizures, also known as absence seizures. The post was shared thousands of times and prompted widespread concern among parents about the safety of chemical sunscreens for children. It tapped into a longer-running current of anxiety about the chemicals used in personal care products and their potential health effects.

The broader claim asserted that common sunscreen ingredients, including oxybenzone, avobenzone, octisalate, octocrylene, homosalate, and octinoxate, are absorbed through the skin into the bloodstream and that this absorption poses health risks including neurological problems, endocrine disruption, reproductive harm, and developmental issues. The claim drew on a combination of legitimate scientific research, regulatory developments at the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and anecdotal reports from concerned consumers.

At the heart of the controversy was a 2019-2020 period in which the FDA proposed updated regulations for sunscreen ingredients, requesting additional safety data on several commonly used chemical filters. This regulatory action was widely interpreted by some commentators—and amplified by social media—as proof that sunscreens were dangerous and that the government had confirmed the risks. The reality was considerably more nuanced, involving questions about the adequacy of historical safety testing, the difference between detection and harm, and the balance between preventing skin cancer and minimizing chemical exposure.

Snopes investigated the claim in August 2020, rating it "Mixture" with a bifurcated finding: "What's True" acknowledged that some evidence shows sunscreen chemicals can be absorbed into the skin and bloodstream, and that some studies have linked exposure to certain chemicals with endocrinological, reproductive, and developmental issues—though those findings do not necessarily apply to levels found in commercial sunscreen products. "What's False" noted that absorption does not equate to risk, that the scientific community remains uncertain about the implications of absorption, and that the FDA requested further research rather than concluding that the chemicals are harmful.

## 2. What's Actually True

The claim that sunscreen chemicals are absorbed into the bloodstream is supported by scientific evidence. A 1997 study published in *The Lancet* first demonstrated that sunscreen ingredients could be detected in the blood after topical application. More significantly, a 2008 study conducted in coordination with the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES)—an ongoing federal program that assesses the health and nutritional status of Americans—found that 97 percent of urine samples tested contained oxybenzone, one of the most common chemical sunscreen ingredients. This finding indicated that chemical sunscreen ingredients are absorbed systemically after application to the skin.

The FDA's regulatory actions in 2019 and 2020 lend credence to the idea that the agency had unresolved questions about sunscreen safety. In February 2019, the FDA proposed a new rule updating sunscreen regulations to reflect current scientific understanding. As part of this proposal, the agency requested additional safety data on 12 sunscreen ingredients, including oxybenzone, avobenzone, octisalate, octocrylene, homosalate, and octinoxate—all of which are active ingredients in chemical sunscreens. The FDA did not declare these ingredients unsafe; rather, it determined that the available data were insufficient to make a definitive safety determination under modern regulatory standards. This distinction is critical and frequently lost in translation from regulatory language to public discourse.

Several peer-reviewed studies have investigated the potential health effects of sunscreen ingredients. Research on oxybenzone has suggested possible endocrine-disrupting properties, meaning the chemical could interfere with hormone systems in the body. Studies in animal models have linked high-dose oxybenzone exposure to reproductive and developmental effects. Similarly, homosalate has been investigated for potential endocrine activity. However, a crucial caveat applies across these studies: the exposure levels at which effects are observed in animal studies often exceed the levels humans encounter through normal sunscreen use by significant margins.

The class-action lawsuit against Babyganics, a company that marketed sunscreen and other products as "natural," was real. The 2015 lawsuit alleged that Babyganics misrepresented its products as free of synthetic chemicals when they in fact contained ingredients including octinoxate, octisalate, and octocrylene. The suit was settled for $2.2 million in 2016. Babyganics subsequently removed octinoxate from some formulations due to concerns about its effects on ocean reefs, while continuing to use other chemical filters.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes's "Mixture" rating was the correct classification for this claim, which contains both accurate and misleading elements. The article, written by Madison Dapcevich, did an effective job of identifying the core distinction between absorption and harm—a distinction that is frequently elided in public discourse about chemical safety. The article also provided useful background on the two types of sunscreen (chemical and physical/mineral), the FDA regulatory framework, and the Babyganics lawsuit, which was the backdrop for the viral Facebook posts.

The article's explanation of the FDA's 2019 proposed rule was accurate and appropriately contextualized. Snopes noted that most sunscreen products were approved before modern drug evaluation standards existed and that the FDA's request for additional safety data reflected updated regulatory expectations rather than confirmed danger. This is a subtle but important point that many media outlets failed to communicate accurately during the same period.

However, the Snopes article could have been substantially stronger in several respects. Most significantly, the article did not include quantitative information about the levels of absorption observed in the FDA's own studies. In January 2020, the FDA published the results of a clinical trial in the *Journal of the American Medical Association* (JAMA) that found that several sunscreen ingredients, including oxybenzone, avobenzone, octisalate, octocrylene, and homosalate, were absorbed into the bloodstream at levels exceeding the FDA's threshold for requiring additional toxicological testing. The study found that these chemicals reached plasma concentrations above 0.5 nanograms per milliliter after a single application. This was a significant finding and directly relevant to the public debate. The Snopes article mentioned that research showed absorption but did not engage with the specific JAMA study or its regulatory implications.

Relatedly, the article lacked a quantitative risk-benefit analysis. Sunscreen is a rare product category in which a known risk (skin cancer from UV exposure) is weighed against a potential risk (chemical absorption with uncertain effects). The article noted that health authorities including the American Academy of Dermatology Association continue to recommend sunscreen use, but it did not provide data on the burden of skin cancer, the efficacy of sunscreen in preventing it, or how the magnitude of the chemical absorption risk compares to other common exposures. Readers were left with the correct but incomplete understanding that "absorption doesn't mean harm" without the quantitative context needed to make informed personal decisions.

The article also did not explore the history of the "chemicals are dangerous" claim in personal care products or the patterns of skepticism that characterize this domain. The sunscreen controversy is one manifestation of a broader cultural phenomenon in which regulatory caution is interpreted as confirmation of danger—a phenomenon that also characterizes debates about vaccines, food additives, and water fluoridation. Understanding this pattern would help readers evaluate the sunscreen claim within a larger framework of risk communication challenges.

Finally, the article's handling of the neurological effects claim was somewhat superficial. The central anecdote driving the viral spread—a mother's claim that sunscreen chemicals caused her daughter's neurological episodes—was not independently investigated. The article noted that the neurological effects of sunscreen ingredients remain "poorly understood and understudied," which is accurate, but it did not explore whether alternative explanations for the child's symptoms were considered, whether medical professionals had evaluated the case, or whether the anecdotal report had any basis in documented medical literature.

## 4. Why This Matters

The sunscreen chemical absorption debate matters because it sits at the intersection of several high-stakes public health questions: the appropriate regulatory framework for products that have been on the market for decades, the communication of scientific uncertainty to a public that demands definitive answers, and the balance between preventing the known harms of UV exposure and minimizing potential harms from chemical exposure.

Skin cancer is among the most common cancers in the United States, with millions of cases diagnosed annually. The use of sunscreen is one of several recommended preventive measures, along with protective clothing, shade seeking, and avoidance of peak sun hours. If public confidence in sunscreen is undermined by incomplete or misleading information about chemical risks, the public health consequences could be significant. The concern is not hypothetical: surveys have shown that a growing number of Americans, particularly younger adults, are skeptical about sunscreen safety, and some are reducing their use or switching exclusively to mineral formulations.

The FDA's 2019-2020 regulatory actions were intended to strengthen the evidence base for sunscreen safety, not to signal that sunscreens were dangerous. However, the communication challenge is real: when a regulator says "we need more data," the public often hears "this product might be unsafe." This is not an irrational inference, but it is one that requires careful contextualization. The Snopes article's "Mixture" rating correctly identified the ambiguity but did not fully equip readers to navigate it.

The sunscreen debate also illustrates the challenges of anecdotal evidence in the age of social media. A single mother's report about her child's symptoms, shared on Facebook and amplified by the platform's algorithm, generated more public concern than decades of epidemiological research. The asymmetry between the emotional power of anecdote and the statistical power of population-level data is a persistent feature of health-related disinformation and one that is unlikely to diminish as social media platforms continue to prioritize engagement.

Finally, the controversy reveals the gap between what the public expects from product safety regulation and what the system actually delivers. Most sunscreen ingredients were approved decades ago, under standards that would be considered inadequate by modern regulatory requirements. The FDA's "generally recognized as safe and effective" (GRASE) designation for OTC drugs does not require the same level of pre-market testing that new prescription drugs must undergo. This regulatory gap is not unique to sunscreen—it applies to many categories of consumer products—but it creates a vulnerability that can be exploited by those seeking to undermine public confidence.

## 5. Research Notes

The 2019-2020 sunscreen safety debate occurred alongside a series of related controversies that together created an impression of regulatory crisis. The FDA's proposed rule on sunscreens was part of a broader effort to modernize the OTC monograph system, which has been criticized for decades as slow, inflexible, and insufficiently rigorous. The proposed rule was issued in February 2019, and the agency specifically requested additional safety data on 12 of the 16 active ingredients currently used in sunscreen products. However, two of the most common mineral filters—zinc oxide and titanium dioxide—were determined to be GRASE based on available evidence.

The JAMA study published in January 2020 by Matta et al. was a landmark in sunscreen safety research. It was the first study to systematically measure blood concentrations of sunscreen ingredients under conditions of maximal use—meaning participants applied sunscreen to 75 percent of their body surface area four times per day for four days. Under these conditions, all six tested chemical ingredients reached plasma concentrations above the FDA's threshold of 0.5 ng/mL, which triggers additional toxicological evaluation. The study was not designed to assess whether these levels cause harm; it was designed to determine whether they trigger the regulatory threshold for further investigation. This distinction is essential but frequently overlooked in media coverage.

The NHANES data on oxybenzone detection in 97 percent of urine samples is often cited as evidence of widespread exposure, but it is important to distinguish between detection and biologically significant concentration. The NHANES study measured the presence of oxybenzone at very low concentrations—typically at parts-per-billion levels. Toxicological significance at these concentrations remains uncertain and debated.

The European Union has taken a somewhat different regulatory approach to sunscreen chemicals. The EU's Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety (SCCS) has reviewed several sunscreen ingredients and recommended concentration limits based on safety assessments. For instance, the SCCS has determined that oxybenzone is safe at concentrations up to 6 percent in sunscreen products. In 2022, the EU reclassified oxybenzone as a potential endocrine disruptor, but this classification did not result in an outright ban; rather, it triggered additional labeling requirements and concentration restrictions. This measured regulatory response contrasts with the more alarmist interpretations that circulated in the United States.

The Babyganics settlement, while confirming that the company's "natural" marketing claims were misleading, did not establish that the chemical ingredients in its products were harmful. The lawsuit was about truth in advertising, not product safety. This distinction was often lost in the social media coverage.

The American Academy of Dermatology Association and the American Academy of Pediatrics have both affirmed their recommendations for routine sunscreen use, including chemical sunscreens, as part of a comprehensive sun protection strategy. Their recommendations are based on the well-established link between UV exposure and skin cancer and the demonstrated efficacy of sunscreen in reducing that risk.

## 6. Verdict

**Snopes Rating: Mixture — Upheld with amplification.**

The claim that sunscreen chemicals are absorbed into the bloodstream is supported by scientific evidence, including the FDA's own clinical research, which found that several common chemical sunscreen ingredients reach measurable plasma concentrations after topical application. The claim that these chemicals are potentially harmful is not fully supported by the available evidence, though the scientific community remains uncertain about the long-term implications of chronic, low-level exposure.

Snopes correctly classified this claim as "Mixture" and accurately distinguished between the established fact of absorption and the unresolved question of harm. However, the article could have been strengthened by engagement with the specific JAMA study, quantitative risk-benefit analysis, and more thorough investigation of the anecdotal neurological claims that drove the viral spread.

The Truth Vault affirms the Snopes rating while recommending that readers understand the distinction between a compound being detectable in the body and a compound causing harm at those detectable levels. For most individuals, the well-established benefits of sunscreen in reducing skin cancer risk continue to outweigh the uncertain and understudied potential risks of chemical absorption. Individuals with particular concerns may choose mineral sunscreens (zinc oxide and titanium dioxide), which the FDA has determined are generally recognized as safe and effective and which are not absorbed through the skin to the same degree.

The public would be well served by clearer communication from regulators, media, and health authorities about the uncertainty inherent in chemical safety assessment and the difference between regulatory caution and confirmed danger.
