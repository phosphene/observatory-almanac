## §1 · Claim & Verdict

The claim holds that chemicals in commercial sunscreen products are absorbed into the human bloodstream. This claim gained prominent traction following the publication of two FDA-funded studies in the *Journal of the American Medical Association* in 2019 and 2020, which demonstrated measurable plasma concentrations of multiple common ultraviolet (UV) filter chemicals following topical application. The claim circulates in two registers: as a factual assertion about absorption (accurate) and as an implied toxicological concern suggesting that this absorption is dangerous (not established).

**Verdict: True — with essential context.** Systemic absorption of chemical UV filters is a confirmed empirical fact documented by rigorous clinical trials. The FDA's own research found that six UV filter chemicals exceeded the agency's safety threshold for additional toxicological testing after a single day of use. However, exceeding this threshold does not mean the chemicals are unsafe — it means the data required to make a safety determination do not yet exist. The FDA has not withdrawn any sunscreen ingredient from the market and explicitly continues to recommend sunscreen use. The clinical significance of systemically absorbed UV filters is a genuinely open question that the existing literature cannot resolve, and public communication of the facts has been systematically biased toward alarm rather than calibrated uncertainty.

## §2 · Background & Context

### The chemistry of UV filtration

Commercial sunscreens use two broad classes of UV-protective agents. **Mineral (physical) sunscreens** use particles of zinc oxide (ZnO) or titanium dioxide (TiO₂) that reflect and scatter UV radiation rather than absorbing it. These compounds function at the skin surface and have not been shown to penetrate into the bloodstream at clinically significant levels. **Chemical (organic) sunscreens** use synthetic organic molecules — primarily oxybenzone (benzophenone-3), avobenzone (butyl methoxydibenzoylmethane), octocrylene, homosalate, octisalate (ethylhexyl salicylate), and octinoxate (ethylhexyl methoxycinnamate) — that absorb UV radiation and convert it to heat. These molecules necessarily interact at the molecular level with skin and its surface chemistry and have long been suspected capable of transdermal penetration.

Oxybenzone has been the most studied and most debated of these compounds. It is the most widely used UV filter in U.S. commercial sunscreens, present in an estimated 65% of chemical sunscreen products. It is also an endocrine-disrupting compound in laboratory cell and animal studies and is detectable in urine, breast milk, and blood plasma in studies of sun-exposed populations — findings that have been known in the biomonitoring literature since at least the early 2000s.

### The FDA regulatory pathway and the GRASE determination

Under U.S. law, sunscreen active ingredients are regulated as over-the-counter (OTC) drugs by the FDA. The legal framework requires that sunscreen ingredients be classified as "generally recognized as safe and effective" (GRASE) to be marketed without a New Drug Application. In 2019, the FDA published a proposed rule updating its OTC sunscreen framework. This rule classified only two active ingredients — zinc oxide and titanium dioxide — as GRASE Category I (safe and effective). It classified 12 other chemical UV filters as GRASE Category III (data insufficient to determine GRASE status) and requested that manufacturers and researchers conduct additional safety testing.

This move was technically conservative (more data requested) but was widely reported as a finding that chemical sunscreens are unsafe — a mischaracterization that had measurable effects on public behavior.

The FDA simultaneously established a threshold for systemic absorption above which additional toxicological testing would be required: **0.5 nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL)** in plasma. This threshold is an administrative trigger for further study, not a safety limit. The FDA does not claim that absorption above 0.5 ng/mL constitutes a health hazard — only that the safety data required to confirm the absence of hazard must be generated if that level is exceeded.

### Application scenarios and real-world use

An important methodological note about the FDA clinical trials: subjects applied sunscreen to 75% of body surface area four times daily — a protocol designed to simulate maximum possible exposure (e.g., full-body application at the beach every two hours as instructed). This is not how most people use sunscreen. Most real-world sunscreen application involves smaller areas (face, arms), lower frequency, and less complete coverage. Plasma concentrations in normal use scenarios may be substantially lower than those measured in the trials, though this question hasn't been systematically investigated for all UV filter ingredients.

## §3 · Epistemic Analysis

### The "detected ≠ harmful" distinction and its systematic violation

The discovery of a compound in human blood provides two and only two pieces of information: (1) the compound can translocate from the site of application to the circulatory system, and (2) it is present at a measured concentration. Neither piece of information, alone or together, constitutes evidence of harm. Yet journalistic and social media coverage of the FDA sunscreen absorption studies systematically presents detection as constituting, at minimum, a serious concern requiring behavioral change — frequently in the same breath as cell-culture and animal studies of oxybenzone's endocrine-disrupting properties.

This conflation violates a fundamental principle of toxicology: **the dose makes the poison** (Paracelsus). Cell-culture studies demonstrating estrogenic activity at oxybenzone concentrations of thousands of nanomoles per liter are not directly informative about health effects in humans with plasma concentrations of tens of nanograms per milliliter — orders of magnitude lower, in a biologically complex systemic context with metabolic clearance pathways, protein binding, and immune surveillance that in vitro systems do not model.

**Epistemic novelty:** The epistemically honest characterization of what is currently known is this: six common UV filter chemicals are absorbed into the bloodstream at concentrations that exceed the FDA's threshold for triggering further study. The question of whether those concentrations produce any clinically meaningful health effect in humans is genuinely unanswered. The null hypothesis of no effect has not been tested; it has merely not yet been rejected. This state of genuine scientific uncertainty is not well served either by blanket dismissal ("studies show sunscreen is safe") or by alarm-generating reporting ("sunscreen chemicals found in blood"). Calibrated uncertainty is the epistemically appropriate stance — and it is nearly absent from public communication on this topic.

This matters practically because the communication vacuum has been partially filled by anti-sunscreen product marketing (mineral-only sunscreen brands have commercial incentives to amplify chemical sunscreen concerns) and by wellness-media frameworks that consistently misread "requires more study" as "probably dangerous.

## §4 · Evidence Review

### The 2019 JAMA preliminary study

**Matta et al. (2019)** published in the *Journal of the American Medical Association* (JAMA) a pilot clinical trial in which 24 healthy participants applied one of four commercial sunscreen formulations (two sprays, one lotion, one cream) to 75% of their body surface area four times daily for four days. Blood samples collected over seven days were analyzed for four UV filter chemicals: avobenzone, oxybenzone, octocrylene, and ecamsule. All four chemicals exceeded the 0.5 ng/mL FDA threshold in plasma after a single day of application. Maximum plasma concentrations observed: avobenzone 4.0 ng/mL, oxybenzone 209.6 ng/mL. The study was explicitly designed as a pilot to determine whether systemic absorption exceeded the threshold — not to assess clinical safety outcomes.

### The 2020 JAMA expanded study

**Matta et al. (2020)**, published in *JAMA*, extended the pilot with 48 healthy participants, four formulations, and six UV filter chemicals: avobenzone, oxybenzone, octocrylene, homosalate, octisalate, and octinoxate. All six exceeded the 0.5 ng/mL threshold after a single day of application under maximal-use conditions. Notable quantitative findings:
- Oxybenzone reached maximum plasma concentrations of up to **258.1 ng/mL** — the highest of any filter tested
- Avobenzone reached up to **7.1 ng/mL**
- Multiple chemicals remained detectable in blood seven days after the last application, with some exceeding the FDA threshold through day 21 of washout

These are pharmacokinetically significant findings: they establish that chemical UV filters do not simply pass transiently through the skin and exit; they accumulate in tissue compartments and clear slowly. The biological significance of this accumulation remains unknown.

### Biomonitoring and population studies

**Calafat et al. (2008)** published in *Environmental Health Perspectives* analyzed U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data and found oxybenzone detectable in the urine of 96.8% of surveyed Americans, reflecting widespread environmental and cosmetic exposure. This study established population-level ubiquity of oxybenzone exposure independent of direct sunscreen application — through other personal care products, indirect environmental exposure, and food-chain contamination in coastal environments — complicating efforts to attribute specific health effects to sunscreen use per se.

**Janjua et al. (2004, 2008)**, writing in the *Journal of Investigative Dermatology*, conducted controlled human exposure studies involving whole-body application of oxybenzone and other UV filters and documented reproductive hormone-level changes in male participants, though the observed changes were within normal physiological range and did not meet established clinical thresholds for endocrine disruption. The studies demonstrated biological activity but did not establish harm.

### The skin cancer risk basis for continued sunscreen recommendation

The protective effect of sunscreen against UV-induced skin cancers is among the most robustly established facts in dermatological epidemiology. The **QSKIN study** (Hughes et al., 2013, *Journal of Clinical Oncology*) — a large Australian prospective cohort study — demonstrated that regular daily sunscreen use over a 10-year period reduced melanoma incidence by 50% among participants who applied sunscreen consistently versus intermittently. This magnitude of protective effect substantially outweighs any plausible harm from chemical UV filter absorption for which no clinical evidence of harm yet exists.

**Green et al. (1999)** in the *Journal of the National Cancer Institute* provided earlier randomized trial evidence of sunscreen's protective benefit against squamous cell carcinoma specifically.

The FDA's epidemiological basis for continued sunscreen recommendation despite the Category III classification of chemical UV filters is grounded in this cumulative evidence base: the known, quantified risk of UV-induced skin cancer is weighed against a potential absorption-related risk that has no established clinical endpoint. The balance, under existing evidence, clearly favors sunscreen use.

## §5 · Counterarguments & Nuances

**"Zinc and titanium are safer."** Mineral sunscreens using zinc oxide and titanium dioxide are classified as GRASE Category I by the FDA and have not been shown to penetrate into the bloodstream at clinically significant concentrations. They are a legitimate alternative for people who prefer to avoid chemical UV filter uncertainty. However, they carry their own tradeoffs: they are less photostable under prolonged UV exposure (particularly titanium dioxide), may require more frequent reapplication, leave visible white residue on darker skin tones that impairs aesthetic acceptability and thus real-world adherence, and offer lower SPF ratings in some formulations.

**Environmental concerns: coral reefs.** Oxybenzone and octinoxate have been demonstrated to cause developmental abnormalities in coral larvae at low concentrations in controlled studies (**Downs et al., 2016**, *Archives of Environmental Contamination and Toxicology*). Hawaii, Palau, the U.S. Virgin Islands, and other jurisdictions have enacted restrictions on reef-based sunscreen use. While this represents a genuine environmental concern, it is distinct from the human health question and does not establish human toxicity.

**Breast milk and fetal exposure.** Chemical UV filters have been detected in breast milk and in cord blood, raising concerns about fetal and neonatal exposure during critical developmental windows — periods when endocrine disruption would be most consequential. **Schlumpf et al. (2008)** documented oxybenzone in breast milk samples from Swiss mothers who used UV filter-containing products, finding detectable concentrations in a significant proportion of samples. This pathway of exposure has not been systematically assessed for clinical outcomes. It arguably represents the most legitimate biological concern in the literature and is the least well-communicated dimension of the absorption issue.

**Under-application vs. absorption concern trade-off.** Population studies consistently show that sunscreen is dramatically under-applied in practice: the majority of users apply between 20% and 50% of the recommended amount (**Autier et al., 2007**, *European Journal of Cancer*). This means that most users are already absorbing lower levels of UV filter chemicals than the maximal-use trial protocol generates, while simultaneously receiving substantially less UV protection than the labeled SPF indicates. Communication that emphasizes absorption risk without correcting for under-application may push users toward even less complete coverage, worsening UV protection outcomes.

## §6 · Conclusion & Implications

The claim that sunscreen chemicals are absorbed into the bloodstream is true and documented by rigorous FDA-commissioned clinical research. The 2019 and 2020 JAMA studies by Matta et al. established systemic absorption of six UV filter chemicals at concentrations exceeding the FDA's threshold for requiring additional safety data, with some compounds reaching high plasma concentrations and clearing slowly. This is a significant finding that warrants continued investigation.

What it does not warrant is sunscreen avoidance. No evidence establishes clinical harm from chemically absorbed UV filters at the concentrations observed in real-world use. The protective effect of sunscreen against UV-induced skin cancers — melanoma, squamous cell carcinoma, basal cell carcinoma — is robustly established and constitutes the largest dermatological mortality prevention intervention currently available at the individual consumer level. An individual who stops using sunscreen in response to absorption concerns is making a risk trade that exchanges an uncertain, poorly characterized potential harm for a well-characterized, quantified harm.

The appropriate public health response is equally clear: accelerate the generation of safety data on chemical UV filters that the FDA has requested from manufacturers and researchers; in the meantime, make individual decisions about chemical versus mineral sunscreen based on accurate information about both trade-offs; and prioritize high-compliance sunscreen use over concern-driven under-application. The dermatology community's clear and consistent message — continue using sunscreen; the UV cancer risk is real and large; the absorption risk is not established — is correct and deserves amplification rather than dilution by alarm-framing in popular media.
