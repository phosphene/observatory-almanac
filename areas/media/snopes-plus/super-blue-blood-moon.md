# "A Super Blue Blood Moon Is an Extremely Rare and Dangerous Astronomical Event"


## 1. The Claim

On January 31, 2018, sky watchers across the Western United States, parts of Asia, and Australia witnessed a total lunar eclipse occurring when the Moon was simultaneously at perigee (its closest orbital approach to Earth) and constituted the second full moon of the calendar month. Media coverage of this celestial convergence produced one of the most successful pieces of astronomical hype in recent memory: the "super blue blood moon."

The compound label generated immediate viral traction. News outlets, science communication accounts, and popular astronomy sources competed to characterize the event as historically rare, visually spectacular, and cosmically significant. Some accounts claimed such a triple convergence had not occurred since the 19th century. Others implied or stated outright that the event was uniquely dangerous, citing folk traditions associating lunar phenomena with physical, psychological, or societal disruptions. Emergency management social media accounts fielded questions from concerned citizens about whether the event posed any physical risk.

The "super" modifier referred to the Moon being at or near perigee during the full moon phase—a configuration sometimes called a "supermoon" that supposedly produces a dramatically larger and brighter lunar disk. The "blue" modifier referred not to the Moon's color but to the calendrical coincidence of two full moons occurring within a single calendar month—a usage of "blue moon" that is itself a relatively recent and contested convention. The "blood" modifier referred to the reddish-orange coloration the Moon takes on during total lunar eclipse, when Earth's shadow fully envelops the lunar surface and the only light reaching it is refracted through Earth's atmosphere at the horizon—a phenomenon sometimes separately called a "blood moon."

Together, these three labels, each carrying its own tradition of media amplification, were stacked into a compound phrase whose dramatic impact substantially exceeded any of its components. The event became a cultural moment, trending across social media platforms for days before and after the eclipse, generating extensive news coverage, and prompting planetariums, observatories, and science museums to organize viewing events and issue educational materials.

The "dangerous" component of the claim draws on overlapping traditions: folk belief that lunar phases affect human behavior and health, neo-astrological interpretations of lunar events as harbingers of change, and the intuitive but scientifically unsupported notion that an astronomically dramatic event must carry commensurate physical consequences. This dimension of the claim appeared most prominently in fringe wellness and spiritual communities, but bled into mainstream media coverage through tone and framing choices that left the question of physical effects ambiguous.


## 2. What's Actually True

To assess the "super blue blood moon" claim accurately, each component must be evaluated independently before the compound can be examined.

**The "Supermoon" Component**

The term "supermoon" was coined by astrologer Richard Nolle in 1979 and defined as a new or full moon occurring within 90% of perigee—the Moon's closest orbital approach to Earth. The Moon's orbit is elliptical, ranging from approximately 356,500 km at perigee to 406,700 km at apogee. When the full moon coincides with perigee, the lunar disk appears roughly 7% larger in angular diameter and approximately 15-16% brighter than a full moon at apogee.

These differences are real but visually imperceptible to most observers. Human visual perception is poorly calibrated for detecting absolute size differences in isolated objects viewed against the sky, and the typical human eye cannot distinguish the 7% angular size difference between a perigee full moon and an apogee full moon without direct side-by-side optical comparison. Multiple controlled perception studies have confirmed that viewers cannot reliably identify whether a given full moon is a "supermoon" or an ordinary full moon from visual inspection alone.

The gravitational effects of a perigee full moon on Earth's oceans are slightly amplified relative to an average full moon—perigean spring tides are marginally higher than ordinary spring tides—but the effect is modest and routine, representing a small percentage increase above normal tidal ranges. There is no credible scientific mechanism by which the approximately 6-7% reduction in Earth-Moon distance during perigee would produce physical effects on human physiology, mental states, or terrestrial geology beyond the tidal footnote.

Furthermore, with Nolle's 90% of perigee definition, "supermoons" occur three to four times per year. They are not rare events. Under some broader definitions applied by media outlets, a supermoon simply means a full moon that is "closer than average"—a definition that makes supermoons even more frequent. The rarity framing that attached to the 2018 event was not driven by the "super" component.

**The "Blue Moon" Component**

The folk expression "once in a blue moon" denotes extreme rarity, but the specific calendrical definition of "blue moon" as the second full moon in a calendar month is a modern convention with a confused etymological history. The modern usage derives from a 1946 misinterpretation in Sky and Telescope magazine of an older Maine Farmer's Almanac convention that used "blue moon" to denote the third full moon in a season containing four full moons—a somewhat different technical definition from the currently popular variant.

Second-in-a-month full moons occur approximately every 2.5 years, since the lunar cycle of approximately 29.5 days is slightly shorter than most calendar months. This makes them reasonably uncommon but hardly astronomical rarities—the expected wait for a blue moon is on the order of 30 months at any given moment, not generations. The "blue" in "blue moon" has nothing to do with actual lunar coloration.

The Moon can appear genuinely blue on Earth under specific atmospheric conditions—most notably following major volcanic eruptions or large wildfire events that inject fine particulate matter at the appropriate particle sizes into the upper atmosphere. Forest fires in Canada in 1950 and the Pinatubo eruption in 1991 produced well-documented blue moon appearances. This phenomenon is entirely unrelated to the calendrical "blue moon" definition and is a source of persistent popular confusion.

**The "Blood Moon" Component**

Total lunar eclipses are reliably spectacular visual events and the "blood moon" label accurately captures their striking appearance. When Earth fully occludes the Sun from the Moon's perspective, the only illumination reaching the lunar surface passes through Earth's atmospheric limb—the narrow crescent of atmosphere visible from space at Earth's horizon. This atmosphere scatters shorter-wavelength light (blue) while passing longer-wavelength light (red and orange), producing the characteristic reddish-orange coloration.

But total lunar eclipses are not rare. They occur on average twice per year globally, with any given location on Earth experiencing a total lunar eclipse visible to the naked eye roughly every 2-3 years. The use of the "blood moon" label—which originated in popular evangelical Christian prophetic interpretation of the Book of Joel and was substantially amplified by pastor John Hagee's 2013 book Blood Moons—contributed to a mythology of ominous significance that total lunar eclipses had not carried in mainstream popular astronomy prior to approximately 2014.

**The Compound Event**

The January 31, 2018 event genuinely was the convergence of all three component phenomena. It was the second full moon within January 2018 (the first having occurred January 1), it occurred when the Moon was near perigee, and it featured a total lunar eclipse visible from appropriate viewing locations.

The claim that such a triple convergence had not occurred since 1866 was widely circulated and is technically accurate under a specific set of definitions—but the accuracy of the rarity claim depends entirely on how strictly one defines each component. Under slightly more flexible definitions, similar convergences are substantially less rare. The apparent precision of "not since 1866" gave the rarity claim a specific historical anchor that amplified its impact; the actual calculation involves somewhat arbitrary definitional choices.

No physical danger was associated with the event. Total lunar eclipses produce no radiation hazard. Unlike solar eclipses, lunar eclipses are entirely safe to view with the naked eye throughout their duration. The Moon's gravitational effects on Earth do not meaningfully increase during total lunar eclipse. There is no scientific pathway by which the visual appearance of the Moon during any phase or eclipse configuration would cause physical harm to observers or affect human physiology beyond ordinary moonlight levels.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The "super blue blood moon" phenomenon illustrates the compounding nature of astronomical hype, where each layer of mythological inflation builds on the constructed significance of the terms already in circulation.

Each component label carries its own mythological freight. "Supermoon" arrived in popular consciousness via astrology and was subsequently adopted by science communicators who found it a useful hook for lunar engagement content. "Blue moon" carries folk associations with extreme rarity through the common idiom. "Blood moon" carries millennia of culturally embedded ominous associations—lunar redness has been interpreted as an ill omen across cultures from ancient Mesopotamia through medieval Europe—amplified in modern contexts by evangelical apocalyptic interpretation. Stacking all three labels into a single compound phrase creates a narrative object whose suggested significance is multiplicative rather than additive.

Media organizations have structural incentives to amplify astronomical events. Space-related content generates strong engagement across demographic groups, planetary and lunar events are visually compelling and photograph well, and astronomy stories allow science publications to reach audiences who do not typically consume science content. The combination produces persistent pressure to characterize astronomical events as rare, dramatic, and significant regardless of their actual scientific standing.

The 2018 event benefited from coinciding with the rise of social media as the primary distribution channel for science communication. Platform algorithms reward engagement, which favors content that generates emotional responses—wonder, concern, urgency—over content that accurately represents statistical normalcy. The fact that "super blue blood moon" is an intrinsically compelling phrase, alliterative and evocative, made it highly optimized for the attention economy regardless of whether the underlying event merited the descriptor.

Folk tradition provides deep and persistent foundations for belief in lunar effects on human wellbeing. Across cultures, the Moon has been associated with madness (the word "lunacy" derives from Luna), fertility cycles, agricultural timing, and prophetic significance. These associations are entrenched enough that even populations with strong scientific literacy often retain residual intuitions about lunar influence. The "dangerous" component of the super blue blood moon claim tapped into this reservoir of folk cosmology in ways that pure astronomical hype could not have achieved independently.

The specific claim about the event being the first of its kind since 1866 provided a temporal anchor that made the rarity feel empirically grounded rather than speculatively inflated. Historical rarity claims of this type—"not since the 19th century"—carry inherent credibility because they appear to be the product of careful astronomical calculation, and most audiences cannot independently verify or falsify them.


## 4. Verdict

**Composite mythology.** The January 31, 2018 "super blue blood moon" was a real astronomical event whose component parts genuinely converged on that date. The convergence was unusual under specific definitional frameworks. The event produced a visually striking total lunar eclipse that was safe, beautiful, and worth observing.

It was not "extremely rare" in any scientifically meaningful sense—each component phenomenon occurs multiple times per decade, and similar convergences have occurred within living memory depending on definitional choices. It was not dangerous by any scientific standard—total lunar eclipses pose zero health risk and the Moon's gravitational effects during perigee are trivially measurable at best. The compound label "super blue blood moon" was a successful piece of media packaging that stacked three separately inflated astronomical terms to produce an impressive noun phrase whose total dramatic impact far exceeded the sum of its components.

The genuinely interesting science of the 2018 event—the physics of atmospheric light scattering that produces lunar eclipse coloration, the mathematics of orbital mechanics that determines eclipse timing and frequency, the actual tidal effects of perigean full moons—received far less coverage than the hype cycle. This ratio accurately reflects media incentive structures but represents a missed opportunity for productive public science engagement.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The super blue blood moon episode is a case study in what might be called "astronomical inflation"—the systematic tendency of popular science coverage to characterize celestial events as rarer, more spectacular, and more physically significant than they actually are.

This inflation has measurable consequences. Repeated encounters with hyperbolic astronomical coverage produce calibration problems in popular audiences, who lose the ability to distinguish between genuinely rare astronomical events (a naked-eye comet, a transit of Venus, a total solar eclipse at a specific location) and ordinary cyclical phenomena packaged with dramatic labels. When everything is "once in a lifetime," nothing is.

The inflation also degrades trust in science communication more broadly. Audiences who observe clear discrepancies between promised visual spectacle and actual observational experience—particularly for "supermoons," which frequently disappoint viewers expecting a dramatically larger Moon—learn to discount science journalism hyperbole as a category. This skepticism, while reasonable in its proximate application, can generalize into distrust of legitimate scientific communication.

The "blood moon" label has a specific and troubling genealogy in modern American evangelical apocalypticism, centered on the "blood moon tetrad" prophecy promoted by John Hagee and others during 2014-2015. This tradition interprets total lunar eclipse tetrads—four consecutive total lunar eclipses without a partial eclipse between them—as prophetic omens of significant events in Jewish and world history, drawing on Biblical passages in Joel, Acts, and Revelation. The lunar eclipse tetrads themselves are real astronomical phenomena; the prophetic interpretation is not scientifically founded. The casual mainstream adoption of "blood moon" as a generic label for total lunar eclipses helped normalize vocabulary with active apocalyptic connotations, contributing to the blurring of astronomical observation and prophetic interpretation in popular media.

Serious astronomical communication organizations, including the International Astronomical Union, the American Astronomical Society, and the Astronomical Society of the Pacific, have repeatedly addressed the problems with "supermoon" amplification and related hype phenomena. The IAU has noted the lack of a standardized, scientifically meaningful definition for "supermoon" and cautioned against its uncritical adoption in science journalism. These interventions have had limited effect on media practice, where engagement optimization consistently overrides definitional precision.

The broader ecology of astronomical hype includes the persistent myth of the "Mars as big as the full moon" email hoax, annual claims about meteor showers that will produce "hundreds of shooting stars per minute," and regular inflated coverage of solar activity as posing imminent threats to power grids and electronic infrastructure. The super blue blood moon is the most successful recent example of this genre but exists in a crowded field.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The super blue blood moon presents a hybrid challenge for fact-checkers: the astronomical facts are real, the danger claim is scientifically baseless, and the "rarity" claim depends on definitional choices that are not straightforwardly true or false.

Several fact-checking exercises addressed specific components of the coverage. Claims that the event was the first of its kind since 1866 were evaluated by multiple outlets and found to be technically accurate under specific definitional frameworks while misleading about overall rarity. NASA and the Jet Propulsion Laboratory issued public communications clarifying that the event posed no danger and providing accurate context for each component phenomenon—but these communications reached substantially smaller audiences than the hype coverage they were correcting.

The "dangerous" component is the cleanest fact-checking target. Total lunar eclipses carry no danger risk for observers. This is unambiguous and easily verifiable. Fact-checkers who addressed this specific sub-claim consistently rated it false. However, the danger framing was often subtle—present more as tone and implication than explicit claim—which made it resistant to the direct quotation and rebuttal methodology most fact-checking uses.

The rarity claims are methodologically harder. When a claim depends on definitional choices (how strictly to define "supermoon," which "blue moon" convention to use, whether to require full uninterrupted visibility from a single location), the true/false binary becomes inadequate. Fact-checkers operating under those constraints either provided conditional verdicts ("true under definition X, misleading under definition Y") or avoided the question entirely. Neither response is fully satisfying.

The most effective responses to super blue blood moon hype came not from formal fact-checking but from clear, pre-emptive science communication: explanatory articles published before the event that accurately described each component phenomenon, its actual frequency, and the visual reality observers could expect. This proactive framing model—anticipating hype cycles before they peak rather than rebutting them after they've propagated—represents the most promising structural response to recurring astronomical inflation, though it requires institutional investment in patient, calibrated science communication that most media organizations are not structured to provide.
