# 'Super-Earth' exoplanet discovery is real, but it isn't sending 'strange signals'


## 1. The Claim

Circulating extensively across social media platforms in January 2026 — and echoing an earlier wave of posts from July 2025 — was the claim that NASA technology had discovered a "super-Earth" exoplanet outside our solar system that was emitting "mysterious, repeating signals." Specific posts on X and Instagram named the planet TOI-1846b, described it as approximately twice the size of Earth and located 154 light-years away, and attributed the discovery to NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite. The signals were variously described as "strange," "mysterious," and in some versions "unexplained," language that reliably activates public interest in the possibility of extraterrestrial intelligence.

The tabloid version of the story, most prominently as reported by the Daily Mail, leaned hardest into the mysterious framing, presenting the transit signals as though they were anomalous transmissions rather than predictable physical observations. Social media posts elaborated further: some implied the signals might be of artificial origin, others suggested that NASA was withholding information about the planet's unusual properties, and an undercurrent of posts positioned the discovery as potential evidence of extraterrestrial life or civilisation. The "super-Earth" designation added an additional layer of populist fascination — the phrase evokes a larger, possibly more habitable version of our world rather than the technical mass and radius classification it actually represents in planetary science.

The claim arrived in a media environment already saturated with reports of unusual cosmic signals and purported alien discovery. Multiple high-profile news cycles in recent years — Tabby's Star, `Oumuamua, the BLC-1 radio signal scare, and various exoplanet atmosphere detections — have primed general audiences to treat any mention of "signals" from space as proximate to the extraterrestrial hypothesis. Against this backdrop, the TOI-1846b story was predictably transformed in transmission from a legitimate planetary science paper into something resembling a first contact briefing.

The essential form of the claim that circulated widely: NASA (or scientists using NASA technology) detected a super-Earth that was sending strange or mysterious signals, and this required explanation.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The discovery is real and scientifically significant.** Exoplanet TOI-1846b was confirmed as a real exoplanet through legitimate peer-reviewed research. The discovery team, based at the Oukaimeden Observatory in Morocco, published their findings in a paper by Soubkiou et al. (2025, arXiv:2506.18550), submitted in June 2025. The researchers validated TOI-1846b using data from NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS), combined with multicolor ground-based photometric observations, high-resolution imaging, and spectroscopic data — a methodologically thorough multi-instrument confirmation approach that represents exactly the kind of rigorous cross-validation exoplanet researchers currently employ.

TOI-1846b is classified as a super-Earth with a radius of approximately 1.8 Earth radii (R⊕). NASA defines the super-Earth category as planets two to ten times the mass of Earth that are smaller than Neptune, potentially composed of gas, rock, or a combination of both. The Soubkiou et al. (2025) paper concluded that the planet most probably has a water-rich bulk composition based on its radius, making it a scientifically interesting target for further characterisation. It orbits an M dwarf star — a red dwarf — at a distance of approximately 154 light-years from Earth.

**The "strange signals" were transit signals — a routine detection method.** The NASA spokesperson who responded to Snopes's inquiry offered a straightforward correction of the sensational framing. What the researchers detected were transit signals: the predictable, measurable dimming of a star's light that occurs when a planet passes in front of it from our line of sight. This phenomenon, known as the transit method or transit photometry, is one of the primary and most well-established techniques for detecting exoplanets. It is not mysterious, not unexplained, and not strange in any scientific sense of those words. TESS was specifically designed to detect exactly this type of signal; NASA's Exoplanet Archive lists more than 6,000 confirmed exoplanets, 725 of which were confirmed using TESS data since the satellite launched in April 2018.

Transit signals reveal a planet's presence through the physics of stellar occultation: as the planet crosses the disk of its host star, it blocks a small fraction of the star's light from reaching our instruments. The depth of the transit dip, its duration, and its periodicity encode information about the planet's size, orbital distance, and orbital period. None of this is anomalous or alien — it is straightforward geometrical optics applied at stellar distances.

**Why M dwarf stars make particularly good transit targets.** The Soubkiou et al. (2025) paper specifically noted that M dwarf stars like TOI-1846b's host are "promising candidates for the search of small, temperate exoplanets using transit methods" because the transit signal is more pronounced compared to Sun-like stars of larger radius. When a small planet transits a small M dwarf, it blocks a proportionally larger fraction of the star's light than the same planet would block crossing a Sun-like star, making detection easier and characterisation more precise. This is a feature of stellar physics, not a sign of anomalous planetary behaviour.

**The "radius valley" is a known feature of planetary science.** Several posts drew attention to NASA's mention of the "radius valley" in the context of TOI-1846b, treating it as further evidence of strangeness. The radius valley — also called the "Fulton gap" after Fulton et al. (2017, *The Astronomical Journal*), who identified it in Kepler data — is a statistically observed pattern in the distribution of known exoplanet sizes: there are relatively few planets with radii in the range of approximately 1.5 to 2.0 Earth radii. Planets tend to cluster below and above this range, producing a gap or valley in the size distribution. TOI-1846b, at 1.8 R⊕, falls within this valley, which is why the Soubkiou et al. paper described it as occupying the "intriguing radius valley area." The physical cause of this gap — likely photoevaporation of planetary atmospheres or core-powered mass loss — is an area of active research, but the gap itself is well-documented and not mysterious in the alien-signals sense.

**What the scientists actually said.** Soubkiou et al. (2025) described their finding neutrally and scientifically: "TOI-1846b is a super-Earth-sized planet, with a radius of approximately 1.8R⊕, placing it within the intriguing radius valley area of exoplanet sizes." The paper noted that "such planets are relatively rare, and their study can provide vital clues about planet formation and evolution processes." None of this language remotely suggests mysterious signals, and the characterisation of the planet as "water-rich" was a probabilistic inference from composition models, not an observation of actual liquid water or life-supporting conditions.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The extraterrestrial hypothesis as default interpretation.** There is a deep and enduring cultural template through which any mention of space, signals, and the unknown is processed: the possibility of contact with non-human intelligence. This template — shaped by decades of science fiction, SETI coverage, UFO/UAP discourse, and individual moments of genuine scientific ambiguity like the 1977 Wow! signal — operates as an interpretive shortcut that converts "signal from space" into "possibly aliens" without requiring the intermediate step of evaluating what the signal actually is. Research in science communication consistently finds that public interest in space news spikes sharply when stories are framed around potential life detection, even loosely (Entradas & Bauer, 2012, *Public Understanding of Science*).

**Language laundering in science journalism.** The transformation of "transit signal" into "strange signal" is a specific example of what science communicators call semantic distortion — the replacement of precise technical language with evocative lay equivalents that shift the claim's meaning. "Strange" signals implies anomaly; "transit" signals implies routine observation. The transit is only described as a signal at all because it involves measuring a change in light flux over time, which can technically be called a signal in the information-theoretic sense. But colloquially, "signal" strongly implies a transmission — something sent, intentionally or not, by a source. The semantic conflation of "measured variation" with "received transmission" is at the heart of how the tabloid framing works.

**The epistemic novelty here: how "radius valley" terminology became sensationalised.** There is a specific and underappreciated pathway by which scientifically interesting but entirely conventional features of a discovery get re-read as mysterious. In the case of TOI-1846b, the radius valley — a statistical artefact of photoevaporation physics — was described in the paper and by NASA as "intriguing" and as something requiring "far better understanding." These honest scientific hedges about an area of active theoretical investigation were available to journalists looking for a hook. The language of scientific incompleteness ("we don't fully understand why the radius valley exists") was readily convertible into the language of mystery ("scientists say this planet sits in a 'strange gap' they can't explain"). The movement from "there is ongoing theoretical work on the physical mechanism" to "there are unexplained strange signals" is a condensed example of how scientific uncertainty — an epistemically healthy state — is processed by tabloid media into sensational mystification. This is not random or accidental: tabloid framing actively selects for words like "intriguing," "rare," "mysterious," and "unexplained" as journalistic affordances, regardless of whether the underlying science supports that framing.

**Social media's acceleration of the sensational version.** Once the Daily Mail published its "mysterious signal" framing, social media engaged the accelerating-rumour dynamic that consistently favours the emotional, surprising, or alarming version of a story. The genuine scientific finding — an interesting planet discovered using routine transit photometry — is accurate but undramatic. The tabloid version — NASA's mysterious planet emitting strange signals — is false but compelling. Research on information virality consistently demonstrates that content evoking surprise, awe, or anxiety spreads faster and further than neutral factual corrections (Berger & Milkman, 2012, *Journal of Marketing Research*). By the time the correction reaches audiences, the sensational version has already set the interpretive frame.

**The "super-Earth" label's popular resonance.** The term "super-Earth" is technically a mass-radius classification, but its popular reading is normative rather than taxonomic: a "super" Earth sounds like a better Earth, a more alive Earth, a bigger version of what we know. The scientific nomenclature was never intended to carry that normative weight, but public discourse has consistently loaded it with vitalist associations. Surveys of public science understanding find that "super-Earth" is one of the most recognisable exoplanet designations precisely because of these connotations (IAU public astronomy surveys, 2022). Combined with the "strange signals" framing, the label ensured maximum emotional engagement with the claim.


## 4. Verdict

**Mostly True with important correction — the discovery is real, the "strange signals" framing is fabricated.** The Soubkiou et al. (2025, arXiv:2506.18550) team's confirmation of TOI-1846b as a super-Earth exoplanet is a legitimate and verified scientific result, using the well-established transit photometry method via TESS data in combination with ground-based follow-up. The planet is real, its parameters are well-constrained, and the research is credible. The claim that NASA technology discovered a "super-Earth" is substantially correct.

The claim that the planet is sending "strange signals" is incorrect. The signals detected were transit photometry observations — predictable, mathematically describable, and physically understood measurements of starlight dimming as the planet crosses its host star. This is one of the most routine processes in modern exoplanet science. No scientist involved in the discovery described the signals as strange, mysterious, or anomalous. The phrase originated in tabloid and social media coverage, not from any scientific source.

**Snopes verdict: Mostly True.** The existence of the planet is true; the "strange signals" characterisation is false newspaper and social media embellishment.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The TOI-1846b misrepresentation is an instance of a recurring and well-documented pattern in science media: the legitimate discovery that gets distorted in translation. Exoplanetary science is particularly vulnerable to this because the field operates at the intersection of public fascination (space, life, other worlds) and technical inaccessibility. Readers who cannot personally evaluate a transit light curve are dependent on intermediaries — journalists, science communicators, social media aggregators — whose incentives do not always align with accuracy.

This specific case also illustrates the multi-step nature of distortion. The original Soubkiou et al. (2025) paper used appropriately hedged scientific language; NASA's public statements described transit photometry accurately; the pathway from accurate paper to sensational social media post passed through multiple transformations. The Daily Mail introduced "mysterious"; subsequent social media posts amplified and embellished; shares stripped away remaining context. By the fifth or sixth generation of sharing, the story bore little resemblance to the underlying science.

The radius valley deserves particular attention as the mechanism by which legitimate scientific uncertainty was converted into tabloid mystery. The Fulton gap (Fulton et al., 2017, *The Astronomical Journal*) is a genuine and interesting statistical feature of the known exoplanet population, currently explained by models involving photoevaporation (Owen & Wu, 2017, *The Astrophysical Journal*) or core-powered mass loss (Ginzburg et al., 2018, *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society*). Saying that it requires "far better understanding" is a standard scientific caveat about a model-dependent phenomenon — not an admission of inexplicable weirdness. The conflation of theoretical uncertainty with phenomenological mystery is a foundational error in science communication that recurs across disciplines.

TESS itself represents a triumph of systematic transit photometry at scale. Launched in April 2018, the satellite was explicitly designed to survey the entire sky and provide planet candidates for follow-up characterisation. The fact that it produces candidates including some with unusual properties is not a sign of cosmic strangeness but of the instrument doing exactly what it was engineered to do. Transit signals from TESS are not messages; they are measurements.

The broader context of UFO/UAP discourse and SETI (Search for Extra-Terrestrial Intelligence) in public culture creates a background radiation of expectation against which any space-signal story is read. Tarter et al. (2010, *Acta Astronautica*) noted that public perception frequently conflates planetary detection with intelligent life detection — a categorical error that exoplanet researchers have persistently attempted to correct. The TOI-1846b episode is a microcosm of this challenge.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes's investigation of the TOI-1846b story, published January 19, 2026 by Joey Esposito, followed a methodologically sound approach: source the original research paper (Soubkiou et al., 2025, arXiv preprint), contact NASA directly for a statement, and compare the scientific record against the claims in circulation. NASA's spokesperson provided an explicit and clear correction — transit signals are routine observations, not mysterious transmissions — that allowed the "Mostly True" rating to be grounded in authoritative primary source contact.

The verdict framework "Mostly True" is well applied here because one major component of the compound claim (a super-Earth exoplanet was discovered) is accurate, while the embellished element ("strange signals") is false. Compound claims with partially true antecedents are among the most challenging for fact-checkers because they require audiences to hold two evaluations simultaneously — "the discovery is real / the framing is false" — rather than a single binary verdict.

For science claims of this type, the fact-checker's toolkit benefits from these additional steps: (1) locating and reading the original academic paper rather than relying on press coverage; (2) identifying which specific technical terms have been replaced with colloquial equivalents that shift meaning; (3) checking whether the scientists themselves used any of the sensational language (in this case they did not); and (4) tracing the genealogy of the distortion — which outlet introduced the distortion and what amplified it.

The media literacy lesson from this case is that "signal" is a semantically loaded word in space science contexts. When science journalists use "signal" to describe a transit detection, they are using it in an information-theoretic sense (a measured variation in a physical quantity over time). When tabloids and social media users read "strange signals," they parse "signal" through the SETI/alien-contact template. Disambiguating these two senses — and noting when the shift between them is the source of the distortion — is a core competency for responsible science communication and fact-checking.
