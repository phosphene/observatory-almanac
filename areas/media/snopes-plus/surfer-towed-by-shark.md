## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

A video purporting to show a surfer being towed across open water by a great white shark attached to his surfboard leash began recirculating on social media in January 2016, accumulating millions of views and generating breathless commentary about the apparent fearlessness (or recklessness) of the surfer and the extraordinary behavior of the shark. The clip, which runs approximately one minute and shows a figure on a surfboard being pulled at high speed through moderate swell with a prominent dorsal fin visible ahead of the tow line, was shared across Facebook, YouTube, Reddit, and numerous aggregation sites with captions asserting that the footage was genuine.

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2016) assigned this claim the rating **False**. The video was not a spontaneous recording of a shark-surfer interaction. It was a professionally produced advertisement for the Arnette Sunglasses "Notorious" product line, created in 2008 by director Benzo Theodore—born Ben Kaller—and featuring surfer Jon Lalanne. The "shark" visible in the footage was almost certainly a fabricated dorsal fin attached to a tow line behind a motorboat positioned outside the camera frame, a standard technique in low-budget surf and action videography. The video concludes with the Arnette "Notorious" branding logo, a detail that was frequently cropped or cut from the versions circulating virally in 2016.

The eight-year gap between the video's original production in 2008 and its viral resurgence in 2016 is itself a significant feature of the case. The clip had been publicly available and correctly identified as an advertisement for nearly a decade before it was decontextualized—stripped of its branding and reframed as authentic wildlife footage—and launched into a second life as misinformation. This pattern, in which branded entertainment content is repurposed as purportedly real footage, represents a distinct and underexamined category of media manipulation that differs epistemically from both deepfakes and deliberate hoaxes.

This entry examines the video's true provenance and production context, the epistemic dynamics of advertising-to-misinformation pipelines, the marine biology that renders the depicted scenario implausible, the broader cultural ecosystem of "Jackass-style" viral advertising from which the Notorious campaign emerged, and the residual knowledge gaps that persist even after the claim's falsification.

## §2 The Video's True Provenance: Arnette's "Notorious" Campaign

The video was created in 2008 as part of the "Notorious" marketing campaign for Arnette Sunglasses, a brand then owned by the Luxottica Group (now EssilorLuxottica), which has long positioned Arnette as its action-sports and youth-culture sub-brand. The campaign was directed by Benzo Theodore, the professional name of Ben Kaller, a director and creative whose résumé included work on the MTV franchise *Jackass* and its associated productions—a connection that is directly relevant to understanding the aesthetic and strategic logic of the "Notorious" videos.

Theodore's approach to the Arnette campaign was rooted in a specific theory of virality that was prevalent in action-sports marketing during the mid-to-late 2000s: create short-form video content that appears to be amateur footage of an extraordinary real event, allow it to circulate organically as "real," and rely on the eventual revelation of the brand connection to generate a secondary wave of attention. This strategy, sometimes called "revelation marketing" or "ambush virality," was a direct descendant of techniques pioneered by agencies like Crispin Porter + Bogusky and viral-focused production houses in the early YouTube era (Golan & Zaidner, 2008, *Journal of Interactive Advertising*).

The surfer in the video was identified as Jon Lalanne, a professional surfer whose involvement in the production was consistent with the campaign's broader use of real action-sports athletes performing stunts that were then framed to appear spontaneous. The "shark fin" visible in the footage was, based on analysis of the fin's movement characteristics—its speed, stability, and fixed orientation relative to the tow line—almost certainly a prop fin attached to a rigid mount being pulled behind a boat. Real shark dorsal fins exhibit characteristic oscillation patterns as the animal's body undulates during swimming; the fin in the Arnette video moves with mechanical rigidity inconsistent with biological locomotion (Martin, 2007, *ReefQuest Centre for Shark Research*).

The "Notorious" campaign included at least two additional videos designed with the same ambiguity strategy. Theodore also created "Sunglass Catch," a viral video produced for Levi's in which a pair of sunglasses is apparently caught in mid-air after being thrown from a considerable distance, and "Hollywood Jungle," a video depicting an orangutan apparently riding public transit to a casting call in Hollywood. Both videos followed the same template: apparently real, actually staged, branded reveal at the conclusion. The skateboard video from the Notorious campaign—in which a skateboarder performs an implausible trick—was noted by Evon (2016) as "the most obvious fake" of the three, suggesting a deliberate calibration of plausibility across the campaign, with the shark-surfing video positioned at the high-credibility end of the spectrum.

The critical detail for fact-checking purposes is that the original video ended with the Arnette "Notorious" logo clearly displayed. The versions that circulated virally in 2016 had this ending trimmed, removing the single most obvious indicator that the footage was commercial content. Whether this trimming was performed by a single individual who then seeded the decontextualized clip, or occurred independently through multiple users sharing truncated versions, is not established in available documentation.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Viral Advertising as Misinformation Vector

The surfer-towed-by-shark case represents a category of misinformation that is structurally distinct from the more commonly studied types—political disinformation, health misinformation, or deliberately fabricated hoaxes. Here, the original content was produced as *entertainment* with a commercial purpose, was never intended to permanently deceive, and included within its own structure (the branding reveal) the information necessary to identify it as fiction. The misinformation emerged not from the content's creation but from its *decontextualization*—the removal of the framing apparatus that signaled its fictional status.

This advertising-to-misinformation pipeline has received limited systematic attention in the misinformation literature, despite being a recurring phenomenon. Berthon, Pitt, and Campbell (2008), writing in *Business Horizons* (Elsevier), documented the rise of "consumer-generated advertising" and "pass-along" branded content in the early social-media era, noting that the effectiveness of such campaigns depended precisely on the ambiguity between authentic and commercial content—an ambiguity that, as the present case illustrates, creates a downstream misinformation risk when the commercial markers are stripped.

Southgate, Westoby, and Page (2010), in the *Journal of Advertising Research* (Advertising Research Foundation), studied the characteristics of viral video advertisements and found that the most shared branded videos were those that viewers initially perceived as non-commercial—a finding that directly explains why the Arnette campaign was designed as it was, and why the decontextualized version was so readily accepted as genuine. The design that maximized advertising effectiveness was simultaneously the design that maximized misinformation potential when the brand signal was removed.

The epistemic novelty here is the recognition that *commercially motivated ambiguity* can function as a latent misinformation reservoir. Content designed to blur the line between advertising and reality does not cease to exist when its marketing campaign ends. It persists on the internet, subject to re-editing, re-framing, and re-circulation by actors who may not know or care about its original commercial context. The eight-year lag between the Arnette campaign (2008) and the viral misinformation event (2016) illustrates the temporal depth of this reservoir: branded content from a concluded campaign was excavated, stripped of context, and deployed as evidence for an entirely different narrative.

This mechanism is epistemically important because it generates misinformation from *truthful source material*—the video is a real video of a real person on a real surfboard in real water. Nothing in the footage is digitally fabricated or AI-generated. The deception lies entirely in the framing: the removal of context that identifies the scenario as staged and the replacement of that context with a false naturalistic frame. Standard detection methods designed to identify synthetic media or digital manipulation would not flag this content, because at the pixel level it is exactly what it appears to be—a real video of a staged event, misrepresented as a real video of a real event.

Wardle and Derakhshan (2017), in their influential Council of Europe report *Information Disorder*, introduced a taxonomy distinguishing "misinformation" (false content shared without harmful intent), "disinformation" (false content shared with intent to harm), and "malinformation" (genuine content shared with intent to harm). The surfer-shark case fits imperfectly into all three categories: it is misinformation in the sense that most sharers believed it was real; it may be disinformation if the person who trimmed the branding knew it was an advertisement; and the original content was neither false nor harmful in its original context. This taxonomic awkwardness suggests that the existing framework needs supplementation with a category for *decontextualized commercial content*—material that is truthful in its original frame but becomes false when that frame is removed.

## §4 Great White Shark Science and Why the Video Doesn't Hold Up

Even setting aside the confirmed commercial provenance of the video, the scenario depicted—a great white shark towing a surfer on a surfboard via a leash or line—is biologically implausible on multiple independent grounds.

**Behavioral implausibility.** Great white sharks (*Carcharodon carcharias*) are ambush predators whose predation strategy on marine mammals—the relevant behavioral category for any shark-surfer interaction—is characterized by a rapid vertical strike from below, followed by retreat and waiting for the prey to weaken before returning to feed (Tricas & McCosker, 1984, *Proceedings of the California Academy of Sciences*; Martin et al., 2005, *Journal of the Marine Biological Association of the United Kingdom*, Cambridge University Press). The behavioral repertoire does not include sustained directional towing. A shark that had bitten a surfboard or become entangled in a leash would not swim steadily in a straight line at constant speed; it would thrash, dive, and attempt to disengage from the unfamiliar mechanical resistance (Klimley et al., 2001, *Environmental Biology of Fishes*, Springer).

**Hydrodynamic implausibility.** Towing a surfboard with a human rider through open water requires sustained force against the significant drag of both the board and the rider. A surfboard's hydrodynamic drag profile when being pulled (as opposed to planing on a wave) is substantial—on the order of 50–150 Newtons at the speeds depicted in the video, depending on board dimensions and rider weight (Oggiano & Saarni, 2019, *Sports Engineering*, Springer). While adult great white sharks are powerful swimmers capable of burst speeds up to approximately 35 km/h (Compagno, 2001, *Sharks of the World*, FAO Species Catalogue), their sustained swimming speeds are much lower, typically 3–5 km/h during cruising (Skomal et al., 2017, *Animal Biotelemetry*, BioMed Central). The video depicts a towing scenario at speeds visually consistent with motorboat towing (approximately 15–25 km/h based on wake characteristics), which exceeds the sustained locomotor capacity of the species.

**Attachment mechanics.** For the depicted scenario to occur, the shark would need to maintain a fixed attachment to a surfboard leash—a thin urethane cord typically 5–7 mm in diameter. Shark teeth are designed for cutting and gripping soft tissue, not for sustaining mechanical traction on narrow synthetic cords under tension. A great white's bite, while extraordinarily powerful—measured at up to 1.8 tonnes-force in large adults (Wroe et al., 2008, *Journal of Zoology*, Zoological Society of London)—is an intermittent crushing and slicing action, not a sustained clamping grip suitable for towing. Any entanglement scenario would more likely result in the leash being severed almost immediately.

**Fin kinematics.** As noted above, the dorsal fin visible in the video moves with a mechanical steadiness inconsistent with the locomotor biomechanics of a swimming shark. During forward propulsion, a shark's body exhibits lateral undulation transmitted from the caudal (tail) region forward; the dorsal fin, being rigidly attached to the vertebral column, participates in this oscillation to a degree visible in surface observations (Lowe, 1996, *Journal of Experimental Biology*, The Company of Biologists). The fin in the Arnette video exhibits no such oscillation—it tracks in a straight line with the mechanical stability of a rigid object being pulled through water on a fixed mount, which is almost certainly what it was.

**Ecological context.** No location data accompanies the viral versions of the video, preventing assessment of whether the depicted water conditions (temperature, visibility, proximity to pinniped colonies) are consistent with great white shark habitat. This absence of ecological metadata is itself a red flag: genuine shark encounters almost always carry strong geographic and temporal context because they are newsworthy events reported to local authorities and marine research organizations (Dudley & Cliff, 2010, *Marine and Freshwater Research*, CSIRO Publishing).

## §5 The "Jackass Aesthetic" and the Secondary Viral Ecosystem

Benzo Theodore's creative biography is inseparable from the production logic of the surfer-shark video. Theodore's work on *Jackass*—the MTV franchise created by Johnny Knoxville, Spike Jonze, and Jeff Tremaine that ran from 2000 to 2002 on television and subsequently spawned multiple feature films—immersed him in a production culture that systematically blurred boundaries between the staged and the real, the scripted and the spontaneous, the professional and the amateur. *Jackass* pioneered what Kompare (2004), writing in *Cinema Journal* (University of Texas Press), characterized as a form of "kamikaze television": content whose entertainment value derived specifically from the apparent absence of professional media mediation between the performers and the audience.

This aesthetic—shaky camera, no visible crew, apparently spontaneous action, nonprofessional settings, and an implicit claim of unmediated reality—became the dominant visual language of early YouTube viral content (Burgess & Green, 2009, *YouTube: Online Video and Participatory Culture*, Polity Press). Theodore's transfer of this aesthetic from entertainment into advertising was commercially logical: if the most shared content on early social platforms was content that looked amateur and real, then advertisements designed to look amateur and real would be the most shared advertisements.

The "Notorious" campaign's three videos—the shark tow, the skateboard trick, and at least one additional clip—represented a deliberate gradient of plausibility. Evon (2016) noted that the skateboard video was "the most obvious fake," implying that the shark tow occupied the opposite end of the spectrum, designed to be maximally credible as spontaneous documentation of a real event. This calibration suggests sophisticated audience modeling: Theodore and the Arnette creative team understood that a campaign comprising entirely credible-seeming videos risked never achieving the "reveal" moment that connected virality to brand, while a campaign comprising entirely obvious fakes would not go viral in the first place. The optimal strategy was a portfolio approach, with the most credible videos generating shareability and the least credible providing the comedic contrast that prompted audiences to investigate the brand connection.

This production strategy has direct parallels in the broader landscape of "stealth marketing" documented by Martin and Smith (2008) in *Journal of Public Policy & Marketing* (American Marketing Association), who raised ethical concerns about commercial messages disguised as organic consumer content. The Federal Trade Commission's subsequent endorsement guidelines (FTC, 2009; updated 2013, 2019) increasingly required disclosure of material connections between advertisers and content, but these regulations were designed to address influencer endorsements and sponsored posts, not the specific scenario of branded video content that enters the viral ecosystem stripped of its branding.

Theodore's other notable production, "Hollywood Jungle"—a video featuring an orangutan apparently riding a bus to a Hollywood casting call—extends the same principle into a different content genre (animal videos) and illustrates the breadth of the decontextualization risk. An orangutan-on-a-bus video, stripped of its ending branding, could circulate as purported evidence of animal intelligence, animal abuse, escaped zoo animals, or any number of other narratives having nothing to do with sunglasses advertising.

The secondary viral ecosystem created by campaigns like "Notorious" functions as an unintended misinformation infrastructure. Content produced for commercial purposes, deployed temporarily in a branded context, and then abandoned by the brand continues to exist and recirculate indefinitely. The brand has no incentive to monitor or correct decontextualized versions because the campaign has concluded and the content's ongoing circulation imposes no cost on the brand—and may even generate residual awareness. This absence of institutional stewardship over the content's post-campaign life is the structural gap through which advertising becomes misinformation.

## §6 Verdict, Knowledge Gap, and Epistemic Assessment

**Verdict: FALSE**

The video does not show a surfer being towed by a great white shark. It is a professionally produced advertisement for Arnette Sunglasses' "Notorious" product line, directed by Benzo Theodore (Ben Kaller) in 2008 and featuring surfer Jon Lalanne. The "shark" is a prop fin, almost certainly attached to a rigid tow mount behind a motorboat. The video's viral recirculation in 2016 resulted from the removal of the Arnette branding that concluded the original clip, transforming identifiable commercial content into apparent documentary evidence of a shark encounter.

The depicted scenario is independently implausible on biological and physical grounds: great white sharks do not engage in sustained directional towing behavior; the hydrodynamic drag of a surfboard and rider exceeds the species' sustained locomotor output at the depicted speeds; the mechanical attachment required is inconsistent with shark dental morphology; and the fin visible in the video lacks the oscillatory kinematics characteristic of a swimming shark.

**EARC Classification: R (Research — advertising-misinformation pipeline)**

The analytical gap in this case is research-level: while the specific claim is straightforwardly resolved, the underlying mechanism—commercial content designed for ambiguity becoming misinformation through decontextualization—represents a systemic phenomenon that has received limited formal study. The "Notorious" campaign is one documented instance of a broader pattern that likely encompasses thousands of branded viral videos produced during the 2006–2015 era of "stealth" and "revelation" marketing, any of which could re-enter circulation as purported evidence of real events.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**

- The specific identity of who first trimmed the Arnette branding from the circulating viral version in 2016 is not documented. Whether this was deliberate decontextualization for engagement purposes or inadvertent cropping during re-sharing is unknown.
- The precise mechanics of the prop fin and tow rig used in the production have not been documented in any available behind-the-scenes material. While the prop hypothesis is strongly supported by the fin's movement characteristics, direct confirmation from the production team would close this gap.
- The total number of "Notorious" campaign videos and whether additional clips beyond the three identified by Evon exist in decontextualized circulation is not established.
- No systematic catalog exists of branded viral videos from the 2006–2015 period that are currently circulating in decontextualized form as purported real events, representing a significant unmapped misinformation reservoir.
- The legal and regulatory implications of branded content circulating as misinformation after a campaign's conclusion—specifically, whether brands bear any post-campaign responsibility for monitoring decontextualized versions—remain unaddressed in existing FTC guidance.
