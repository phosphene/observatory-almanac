# Chlorine in swimming pools turns blonde hair green


## 1. The Claim

Every summer, parents warn their children about the same aquatic hazard: chlorinated swimming pools will turn their blonde hair green. The belief is so widespread that it functions as folk chemistry — a short, confident causal story passed from generation to generation with the same casual certainty as "don't swim right after eating." Lifeguards repeat it. Hairdressers are asked to fix it. Manufacturers market anti-chlorine hair products premised on it. The explanation has achieved the comfortable status of settled common knowledge.

The claim appears in several closely related formulations, each sharing the same core attribution:

- "Chlorine bleaches blonde hair and makes it go green."
- "Swimming in chlorinated pools turns your hair a greenish colour."
- "The chlorine in pool water reacts with blonde hair and stains it."
- "You need to rinse your hair after swimming to get rid of the chlorine that makes it green."

What all of these formulations share is a confident assignment of causation: chlorine, the antimicrobial agent used to disinfect pool water, is the direct chemical agent responsible for the green discolouration. The implied mechanism tends to be vague — chlorine is a powerful chemical, it bleaches things, it must be responsible — but the attribution is unambiguous.

The claim is not merely a passing curiosity. It has genuine behavioural consequences. Swimmers rinse their hair post-swim to remove chlorine, purchase shampoos marketed as chlorine-neutralising, and sometimes avoid pools altogether on the basis of concerns about chemical damage. Parents of small children with light hair regard the greenish tinge as direct evidence of chemical exposure. The folk belief shapes purchasing decisions, protective behaviours, and attitudes toward pool safety and water treatment.

It is also partially correct — partially in a way that makes it resistant to simple debunking. Green hair in pools is a real phenomenon. It does occur with meaningful frequency among blonde, bleached, and light-coloured hair. It does correlate with pool use. The colour does respond to chelating treatments that remove metal ions. What is wrong is not the observation but the assigned cause.


## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes rates the chlorine-green-hair claim as False, and the core chemical finding that Snopes reports is accurate: chlorine is not the direct chemical agent responsible for the green discolouration of hair in swimmers. The actual culprit is dissolved copper ions — specifically cupric ions (Cu²⁺) — that are present in pool water and bind to the protein structure of hair fibre.

The mechanism has been characterised in a number of studies across trichology, textile chemistry, and cosmetic science literature. Robbins (2012), in the standard reference text *Chemical and Physical Behavior of Human Hair* (5th edition, Springer), describes how metal ions bind to the cortex and cuticle of hair through coordination bonds with sulphur-containing amino acid residues, particularly cysteine and methionine within the keratin matrix. When oxidised copper (Cu²⁺) binds to these sites, it imparts a greenish pigmentation that is most visible in hair with low intrinsic melanin content — blonde, white, and heavily bleached hair — because there is insufficient dark pigment to mask the discolouration.

The sources of dissolved copper in pool water are multiple. Kamath and Weigmann (1982), writing in the *Textile Research Journal*, identified metal ion contamination of fibrous substrates as a function of both water source composition and the materials in contact with the water. Pool systems typically contribute copper through three main pathways: (1) copper plumbing and fittings, from which Cu²⁺ leaches into the water column, particularly at lower pH values; (2) copper-based algaecides, such as copper sulphate (CuSO₄) or copper chelate compounds, which are routinely added to commercial and residential pools to prevent algal bloom; and (3) naturally elevated copper levels in some municipal water supplies, which become more concentrated in pool water as the water evaporates and is partially replenished.

The colour chemistry is straightforward. Oxidised copper in solution is blue-green in colour; when it bonds to the pale, translucent substrate of blonde or bleached keratin, it imparts exactly the greenish tinge that swimmers and parents observe. The effect is not a stain on the outside of the hair but a metallic deposition within the hair structure, which is why simply rinsing with water does not remove it. Treatments that chelate (bind and remove) copper ions — such as solutions containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) or rinses with mild acids — can reverse the discolouration, and this specificity for metal-chelating treatments is itself confirmatory evidence of the copper aetiology.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty: The Role of Chlorine Is Indirect but Not Trivial

Snopes is correct that chlorine is not the direct colourant. The straightforward debunking — "it's copper, not chlorine" — is accurate and important. Where the Snopes verdict leaves a significant epistemological gap, however, is in its implicit framing of chlorine as irrelevant. The fuller chemistry reveals that chlorine plays a meaningful indirect role in the process, and misunderstanding this relationship leads to incomplete preventive advice and an oversimplified picture of pool hair chemistry.

Chlorine functions in swimming pools primarily as hypochlorous acid (HOCl) and the hypochlorite ion (OCl⁻), in proportions determined by the water's pH (White, 1992, *Handbook of Chlorination and Alternative Disinfectants*, 4th ed., Van Nostrand Reinhold). These oxidising agents are powerful enough to oxidise organic matter — which is why they kill pathogens — and they exert two specific effects that are directly relevant to copper uptake in hair.

**First, chlorine oxidises dissolved copper.** Free copper in pool water exists in both cuprous (Cu⁺) and cupric (Cu²⁺) forms. It is the cupric form — the oxidised form — that most readily coordinates to keratin sulfhydryl groups. Chlorine's oxidising action drives the equilibrium toward Cu²⁺, increasing the bioavailable pool of the ion most capable of binding to hair. Drs. Chan, Toribara, and Garber from California established in their 1953 biochemical work (Microchemical Journal) that oxidative conditions significantly amplify metal-protein binding in biological substrates, a finding that applies to pool hair chemistry even though their original work addressed tissue samples.

**Second, chlorine damages the hair cuticle, increasing porosity.** The cuticle — the overlapping outer layer of the hair shaft — functions as a protective barrier that limits the penetration of ionic species into the hair cortex. Oxidative damage to the disulphide bonds in cuticle proteins causes lifting and degradation of the cuticle scales, making the hair fibre substantially more permeable. Lo Nostro, Ninham, and co-workers (2002, *Biophysical Chemistry*) demonstrated using atomic force microscopy that oxidising agents produce measurable surface roughening and porosity changes in keratinous fibres at environmentally relevant concentrations. Hair that has already been chemically bleached or permed — which are both oxidative processes — exhibits pre-existing cuticle damage that dramatically amplifies copper uptake. This is why bleached blonde hair turns green far more easily than naturally blonde hair, even in the same pool.

**Third, chlorine's effect on pH creates conditions that increase copper solubility from plumbing.** Commercial pool systems are typically maintained at a pH of 7.2–7.8. Below pH 7.0, copper solubility from metallic pipes and fittings increases substantially; above pH 8.0, copper tends to precipitate as hydroxide or carbonate compounds. When chlorine dosing temporarily depresses pH, or when pools are inadequately buffered, periods of lower pH can increase the copper content of the water. Crittenden et al. (2012, *MWH's Water Treatment: Principles and Design*, 3rd ed., Wiley) document this relationship in the context of drinking water corrosion, and the principles apply directly to pool systems.

The epistemic novelty here is that the folk claim and the Snopes refutation are both partially describing the same causal chain — they merely disagree on which step in the chain constitutes "the cause." Chlorine is not the colourant, but it is the oxidising agent that (a) produces the reactive copper species, (b) degrades the barrier protecting hair from that species, and (c) modulates the pool chemistry that controls copper concentration. Someone who rinses out chlorine after swimming is therefore not engaging in completely irrational behaviour: they may be removing residual oxidising capacity that would continue to work on the hair cuticle after leaving the pool. They are just wrong about *why* it helps, and they are missing the more direct preventive intervention — saturating hair with fresh water or a leave-in conditioner before entering the pool to reduce mineral uptake.

This is the hallmark of a distorted-but-grounded folk claim: the observed remedy (rinsing) has some basis in the actual chemistry even though the assigned causal mechanism (chlorine as colourant) is incorrect. Dismissing the claim as simply false fails to account for why the folk practice has observational support, and it misses an opportunity to explain the fuller chemistry in a way that would generate genuinely improved preventive behaviour.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** False (direct claim) — Copper, not chlorine, is the proximate colourant.
**Epistemic Basis:** Read-together — primary chemistry literature and cosmetic science sources.
**Chlorine's role:** Indirect but chemically significant — as a facilitating oxidant and cuticle degradation agent.
**Confidence:** High for the copper mechanism; Medium for the magnitude of chlorine's indirect contribution.
**Gap Category:** Distorted-but-grounded — real phenomenon, wrong mechanism.
**Folk Wisdom Value:** Partially functional — rinsing and seeking a chelating shampoo address different parts of a genuine problem; the knowledge gap is in mechanism, not the existence of the hazard.

The direct claim — chlorine turns blonde hair green — is false at the level of mechanism. No study has demonstrated that chlorine itself, in the absence of dissolved copper, produces green discolouration in hair fibres. Multiple lines of convergent evidence point to oxidised copper ions as the responsible agent: chelation reversal, spectroscopic analysis of affected hair, correlations with algaecide use and copper pipe systems, and the absence of the effect when copper is absent from pool water. The claim is not merely unsupported; it has been tested and refuted.

At the same time, dismissing the claim without qualification misrepresents the fuller chemistry and leaves swimmers without accurate preventive guidance. Understanding chlorine's role as a secondary facilitator — oxidising copper into its more reactive form, degrading the cuticle barrier, and periodically mobilising copper from plumbing — explains both the variability in pool incidence (pools with copper algaecides or copper pipes produce more cases) and the partial effectiveness of post-swim rinses. Effective prevention requires saturating hair before pool entry, and effective treatment requires chelation — neither of which is implied by the simple chlorine narrative.


## 5. The Wider Picture

### Pool Water Chemistry and the Copper Problem

The green-hair phenomenon sits within a well-documented body of literature on pool water quality and its effects on body tissues. The composition of pool water is far more complex than the single-agent "chlorinated water" model that enters common usage. A typical commercial pool contains not just chlorine (primarily hypochlorous acid and chloramines) but also pH-buffering agents (sodium bicarbonate, sodium carbonate), stabilisers (cyanuric acid), corrosion inhibitors, and any number of algaecides and clarifiers. Many of these compounds are copper-based.

Copper sulphate pentahydrate (CuSO₄·5H₂O) has been used as an algaecide in swimming pools since the early twentieth century. Bean and colleagues (1984, *Water Research*) documented its efficacy at concentrations of 0.2–2.0 mg/L for most common pool algae, and these concentrations remain the operational standard. The US Environmental Protection Agency permits copper-based algaecides in swimming pools with application rates calibrated to pool volume, but even properly dosed pools can accumulate copper through repeated applications. Moreover, the interaction between hydrogen sulphide-containing compounds in bathers' sweat and urine and dissolved copper can form chromogenic complexes that further complicate water chemistry.

The solubility of copper from metallic plumbing is a well-characterised corrosion chemistry problem. Edwards and others (2004, *Journal AWWA*) showed that copper dissolution rates from piping increase exponentially below pH 7.0 and are highly sensitive to water temperature, dissolved oxygen concentration, and flow rate. Pool systems that undergo wide pH swings — as frequently occurs when bather load increases rapidly, adding large volumes of organic acids — will periodically experience elevated copper ion concentrations in the water column.

### Hair Structure and Vulnerability

Not all hair is equally susceptible to the green-copper effect. Understanding why requires a brief overview of hair morphology and the chemistry of colour treatment.

Human hair comprises three anatomical structures: the medulla (central core, not always present), the cortex (the bulk of the fibre, containing keratin bundles, melanin granules, and cellular material), and the cuticle (the outer layer of overlapping scales that regulate permeability). The cuticle consists of cells rich in disulphide bonds between cysteine residues — precisely the bonds that are targeted by both hair bleach (hydrogen peroxide) and pool chlorine. Smith and Swift (2002, *International Journal of Cosmetic Science*) demonstrated using electron microscopy that standard bleaching protocols severely damage cuticle integrity, reducing the protective barrier function of the cuticle cells and leaving the underlying cortex exposed.

This means that bleached and highlighted hair is not simply lighter in colour; it is structurally compromised in a way that makes it disproportionately susceptible to mineral uptake in pool environments. As Robbins (2012) notes, the uptake of metal ions such as copper, iron, and manganese by hair fibre scales approximately linearly with the degree of oxidative pre-treatment — bleached hair may absorb several times more copper per unit of pool exposure than the same person's unbleached regrowth.

Naturally blonde hair presents a different vulnerability. Its low eumelanin content means that any greenish metallic deposit is visible against the pale background that darker hair would mask. The effect is not larger in naturally blonde hair, but it is more visibly apparent. Children with fine, freshly grown blonde hair may appear particularly affected, which partly explains the folk belief's association with childhood pool experiences.

### The Algaecide Connection and Variable Incidence

A practically important implication of the copper aetiology is that the incidence of green hair in pools should vary substantially across different facilities based on their water chemistry practices. This prediction is borne out by anecdotal and reported experience: outdoor pools in regions with copper-rich soils or water supplies, pools that use copper-based algaecides, and pools with substantial copper plumbing infrastructure produce more cases of green hair than indoor pools supplied by low-copper municipal water using alternative algaecide chemistries.

This variability is invisible within the chlorine narrative. If chlorine were the direct cause, all chlorinated pools should produce equal rates of green hair (modulated only by chlorine concentration and duration of exposure), and the problem should be solvable simply by adjusting chlorine levels. In fact, reducing chlorine in a copper-rich pool would likely worsen the green-hair problem by reducing the pH-buffering that limits copper dissolution from pipes, while increasing the risk of actual pathogens that chlorine is there to control.

The practical intervention that most reliably prevents green hair is pre-swim saturation — wetting the hair thoroughly with fresh water or applying a light leave-in conditioner before pool entry. When hair is already saturated with fresh water, it has less capacity to absorb the mineral-laden pool water. Malinauskyte and colleagues (2021, *Journal of Cosmetic Science*) showed in a model systems study that pre-treatment of hair with hydrophilic coating agents substantially reduces the penetration of ionic species into the cortex, supporting the mechanism behind pre-swim wetting even if their study was not specifically about pool exposure.

Treatment of established green discolouration requires chelation rather than simple rinsing. Apple cider vinegar rinses (providing acidity that competes with hair proteins for copper coordination), tomato juice applications (historically recommended and weakly supported by the acidic and chelating organic compounds present), and commercial EDTA-containing chelating shampoos all work by the same basic principle: providing competing ligands for the Cu²⁺ ions that have bound to keratin. The effectiveness of these interventions — and the failure of purely water-based rinses — is further evidence supporting the copper mechanism.

### Cross-Cultural and Historical Notes

The association between swimming and green hair has been noted in cultures with chlorinated pool traditions since at least the mid-twentieth century. The chemistry was apparently understood by some professional hairdressers before it entered the scientific literature in a systematic form — the practice of chelating treatments for "pool hair" predates the academic documentation of the copper mechanism. This is a minor example of craft knowledge preceding systematic explanation, a pattern familiar from medical and culinary histories.

The persistence of the chlorine attribution despite available corrective information also illustrates a general principle in folk science: people assign causes to the most salient available agent, not necessarily the most mechanistically proximate one. Chlorine is visible (as a smell, a taste, and an irritant to eyes and skin), widely known to be a "chemical," and is the named distinguishing feature of pool water relative to lake or ocean water. Copper ions are invisible, odourless, and not part of common chemical vocabulary. The misattribution therefore has a clear cognitive economy: the wrong cause is selected because it is the most cognitively available candidate.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates this claim as False, correctly identifying copper as the proximate cause of the green discolouration and noting that the belief in chlorine as the culprit is chemically incorrect. Their analysis cites trichology sources and explains the copper-binding mechanism at an accessible level appropriate for general audiences.

The Snopes treatment is accurate at the level of direct mechanism — chlorine is not the green-causing agent, copper is — and provides a useful corrective to the most common and most incorrect version of the belief. For readers who simply want to know "is it chlorine?", the verdict is clear.

Where the Snopes analysis leaves room for epistemological enrichment is in the treatment of chlorine's relationship to the process. The debunking implicitly positions chlorine as irrelevant, when in fact chlorine occupies a real if indirect position in the causal chain: it maintains the oxidative state of dissolved copper, degrades cuticle integrity that would otherwise limit metal ingress, and interacts with pool pH in ways that modulate copper availability from plumbing. The practical consequence of overlooking this indirect role is that readers are left without a complete understanding of why some pools produce far more green-hair incidents than others, and without the most effective preventive advice (pre-wetting or barrier conditioning before pool entry).

This gap is not a failure of Snopes' fact-checking; it is a structural consequence of the format. Binary verdict-and-brief-explanation structures optimise for a clear answer to the narrow question posed. The collateral question — what does this claim tell us about how people reason about chemical causation, and what would complete preventive knowledge look like? — is outside the scope of a standard fact check. That is exactly the space the Snopes+ Truth Vault is designed to occupy.

The primary preventive importance of the fuller picture is this: swimmers and parents who understand that copper is the cause will seek out pools that manage copper chemistry appropriately, pre-wet their hair before entering, or use chelating treatments after. Swimmers who believe chlorine is the cause will rinse with water (marginally helpful, for the wrong reason), purchase anti-chlorine shampoos (often no more effective than ordinary shampoo for a copper problem), and may not think to ask their pool operator about algaecide chemistry — the single most impactful variable in whether a pool routinely produces the green-hair effect.

Accurate folk chemistry, even about something as minor as hair colour after a swim, matters because it shapes the specific interventions that people select. Getting the mechanism right is not merely academic fastidiousness; it is the difference between a preventive action that works and one that provides false reassurance.
