# Taco Bell Was Not Closing in 2020 — It Was Growing, and the Ads That Said Otherwise Were the Real Story

## 1. The Claim

In late December 2020, online advertisements began appearing on social media platforms and across the web's advertising networks with thumbnails and headlines suggesting that Taco Bell was going out of business. Clicking these ads led to a 50-page Money Pop article that, on social media, carried the headline "These Restaurant Chains Are Turning Off Their Burners And Closing Up Shop." The actual headline on the Money Pop website was somewhat different: "These Popular Restaurant Chains Are Losing Money Fast." This discrepancy between the social media headline and the actual article headline — a form of bait-and-switch that is endemic to the clickbait ecosystem — was itself revealing of the incentive structures at work.

Snopes fact-checker Jordan Liles investigated the claim and published his findings on December 24, 2020, rating it **FALSE**. The investigation found that not only was Taco Bell not going out of business, but the chain had actually expanded during 2020 — adding over 200 net new restaurants during a year when much of the restaurant industry was contracting. The Money Pop article, when read carefully, did not actually claim Taco Bell was closing; it mentioned that "there will be some cutbacks in locations in 2020." But the advertising and social media framing created a strong implication of imminent closure that the article's actual content did not support.

This was not the first time Taco Bell had been targeted by false closure claims. Snopes noted that a troll website had published a similar hoax in 2016, suggesting that the chain was a recurring target for this type of manufactured rumor — a pattern that raised questions not just about the specific claim but about the economic and psychological dynamics that make restaurant-closure rumors a reliably profitable content category.

## 2. What's Actually True

The empirical reality of Taco Bell's business performance in 2020 was not merely "not closing" — it was a story of active expansion and relative financial strength during one of the most challenging periods in the modern history of the American restaurant industry.

Yum! Brands, the parent company of Taco Bell, KFC, and Pizza Hut, reported in its third-quarter 2020 earnings that Taco Bell operated 7,400 restaurants as of Q3 2020, up from 7,191 restaurants in Q3 2019. This represented a net addition of 209 locations during a period when the National Restaurant Association estimated that over 110,000 restaurants and bars had closed either permanently or on a long-term basis across the United States (National Restaurant Association, 2020). Taco Bell was not just surviving the pandemic; it was growing through it.

The financial performance was equally robust. Yum! Brands' Q3 2020 earnings report showed that Taco Bell's division recorded higher core operating profits than either the KFC or Pizza Hut divisions during the same quarter. This performance reflected several structural advantages that insulated Taco Bell from the worst impacts of the pandemic: a business model built around drive-through service (which continued operating when dine-in was restricted), a price point that attracted budget-conscious consumers during an economic downturn, and a menu format that translated well to takeout and delivery.

Far from retrenching, Taco Bell announced an aggressive expansion initiative in August 2020. The "Go Mobile" concept represented a new restaurant format designed specifically for the post-pandemic environment: smaller buildings with reduced dining room space, dedicated drive-through lanes, "bellhops" equipped with tablets to take orders from cars waiting in line, and integration with the Taco Bell mobile app for contactless ordering and pickup. The company announced that Go Mobile locations would begin opening in 2021, signaling confidence in continued growth rather than preparation for closure.

The Money Pop article that generated the closure rumor occupies an interesting position in the information ecosystem. Its actual text was carefully hedged — mentioning "cutbacks" rather than closure — but the framing through which it reached most readers (the social media headline, the advertising thumbnails, the listicle format grouping Taco Bell with chains that genuinely were struggling) created an impression far more dramatic than the article's content supported. This gap between what the article actually said and what its distribution framework implied is a structural feature of the digital advertising ecosystem that the article itself was designed to exploit.

Taco Bell's history provides useful context. The chain was founded in 1962 by Glen Bell in Downey, California. Bell, who had previously operated a hot dog stand, was reportedly inspired by observing the long lines at a Mexican restaurant operating near his stand. He initially opened a restaurant called Taco Tia before establishing the Taco Bell brand. The chain grew rapidly through franchising and was acquired by PepsiCo in 1978, then spun off as part of Tricon Global Restaurants (later renamed Yum! Brands) in 1997. By 2020, it had become one of the largest quick-service restaurant chains in the United States, with a cultural footprint that extended well beyond its menu through viral marketing campaigns, brand collaborations, and a deliberately cultivated reputation for irreverent innovation.

## 3. Why People Believe This

### The Attention Economics of Manufactured Panic

The Taco Bell closure rumor was not a spontaneous eruption of public misunderstanding. It was a manufactured information product, designed and distributed through a specific economic system that makes the creation and dissemination of false restaurant-closure headlines reliably profitable. Understanding why people believed the claim requires understanding the attention economy that produced it — an economy whose dynamics have been extensively studied but remain poorly understood by most of the public who participate in it as both consumers and unwitting raw material.

The foundational concept is what Snopes itself identified in its investigation: advertising "arbitrage." In its simplest form, the model works as follows: an advertiser (in this case, the entity placing the Taco Bell closure ads) pays a relatively small amount to a social media platform or advertising network for ad placements. The ad — designed to provoke curiosity, alarm, or both — drives clicks to a content site (Money Pop). The content site is structured as a multi-page slideshow, where each "page" requires a separate click and loads a separate set of advertisements. A reader who clicks through all 50 pages generates 50 pageviews, each carrying its own advertising revenue. If the total advertising revenue generated by the reader's 50 pageviews exceeds the cost of the initial ad placement that brought the reader to the site, the arbitrage is profitable.

This model has been documented by digital advertising researchers as a significant driver of low-quality content production. Braun and Eklund (2019, *Digital Journalism*) analyzed the economic incentive structures of slideshow content farms and found that the format's profitability depends on two key variables: the cost-per-click of the initial ad placement (which must be low) and the click-through rate within the slideshow (which must be high enough to generate sufficient pageviews per visitor). Restaurant-closure headlines perform exceptionally well on both dimensions: they generate high click-through rates because they trigger loss aversion — the well-documented psychological tendency to weight potential losses more heavily than equivalent gains (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979, *Econometrica*) — and they can be targeted affordably because the subject matter does not compete for premium advertising inventory.

The attention economics framework developed by Herbert Simon (1971, *Computers, Communications, and the Public Interest*) and subsequently elaborated by scholars including Tim Wu (2016, *The Attention Merchants*) and James Williams (2018, *Stand Out of Our Light*) provides the theoretical scaffolding for understanding why these operations work. Simon's foundational insight — "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention" — describes the fundamental condition of the digital media environment. In a landscape where virtually unlimited content competes for finite human attention, the content that captures attention most efficiently wins, regardless of its truthfulness or social value.

The Taco Bell closure ad was optimized for attention capture. It combined several well-established psychological triggers: threat (a beloved brand is disappearing), specificity (it named a recognizable chain, not an abstract category), and temporal urgency (the framing implied imminent closure, demanding immediate attention). These triggers correspond to what Berger (2013, *Contagious: Why Things Catch On*) identified as "high-arousal" emotional content — content that activates the sympathetic nervous system and motivates sharing behavior. The arousal response does not distinguish between accurate and inaccurate stimuli; a false threat activates the same attentional and emotional systems as a real one.

The slideshow format itself exploits what behavioral economists call the "sunk cost fallacy" in a micro-interaction context. Having clicked on the initial ad and invested attention in the first few pages, readers are psychologically inclined to continue clicking through additional pages rather than abandoning their "investment" — even when the content on each successive page delivers minimal new information. Ariely (2008, *Predictably Irrational*) documented this pattern across multiple decision domains, and the multi-page slideshow is perhaps its purest commercial application: each click represents a micro-commitment that makes the next click marginally more likely.

The broader phenomenon of manufactured restaurant-closure rumors can be understood through the lens of what Zuboff (2019, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*) termed the "behavioral futures market." In this framework, human attention and behavioral responses are not merely resources to be captured but prediction products to be manufactured and sold. The advertiser who places a Taco Bell closure ad is not primarily in the business of informing readers about Taco Bell; they are in the business of manufacturing a predictable behavioral response (clicking, scrolling, viewing ads) from a predictable audience segment (people who care about Taco Bell, fast food, or American cultural institutions).

The recurrence of the Taco Bell closure claim — appearing both in 2016 (via a troll website) and in 2020 (via the Money Pop advertising arbitrage ecosystem) — supports the interpretation that certain brands serve as reliable "attention assets" in the misinformation economy. Brands with high emotional salience, broad consumer familiarity, and strong cultural identity generate the most efficient attention returns when paired with threat narratives. Taco Bell, with its distinctive brand personality and loyal consumer base, fits this profile precisely.

Research on "information pollution" — the deliberate introduction of false or misleading content into information ecosystems for economic gain — has increasingly focused on the supply-side economics of misinformation production. Tandoc et al. (2018, *Digital Journalism*) argued that understanding misinformation requires analysis not just of why people believe false claims but of why false claims are produced in the first place. The Taco Bell case provides a clean illustration: the claim was produced because producing it was profitable, and it was profitable because the digital advertising ecosystem rewards attention capture without regard to informational accuracy.

The psychological vulnerability that the arbitrage model exploits is not unique to restaurant-closure rumors. It extends to any category where established brands face plausible but exaggerated threats: retail chains, automotive brands, technology companies, and entertainment franchises have all been subjects of similar manufactured-panic content. But the restaurant category is particularly fertile ground because of the emotional dimensions of food culture — the associations between specific restaurants and personal memories, social rituals, and community identity that make the prospect of closure feel not just commercially significant but personally threatening.

The mismatch between the Money Pop social media headline ("These Restaurant Chains Are Turning Off Their Burners And Closing Up Shop") and the actual article headline ("These Popular Restaurant Chains Are Losing Money Fast") illustrates a specific technique in the attention-arbitrage toolkit. The social media headline is optimized for click-through — it is dramatic, categorical, and alarming. The article headline is moderated — still attention-grabbing, but hedged enough to provide legal and editorial cover. This dual-headline strategy allows the content producer to distribute maximally provocative framing through advertising channels while maintaining a more defensible position in the actual content, a practice that Pickard (2020, *Democracy Without Journalism?*) identified as characteristic of the "post-industrial" digital media economy where the economic incentives of distribution are decoupled from the editorial standards of production.

## 4. Verdict

**False.** Taco Bell was not going out of business in 2020. The chain grew from 7,191 to 7,400 restaurants between Q3 2019 and Q3 2020, posted higher core operating profits than its Yum! Brands sibling divisions (KFC and Pizza Hut), and announced an expansive new "Go Mobile" restaurant format for 2021 launch. The Money Pop article that generated the closure rumor did not actually claim Taco Bell was closing — it mentioned "cutbacks" — but its distribution through advertising channels used headlines implying imminent closure to drive traffic to a 50-page slideshow designed to maximize advertising revenue through reader engagement.

The claim was a product of the digital advertising arbitrage ecosystem, in which the manufacturing of emotionally provocative but factually false headlines is a profitable business model. Taco Bell had been the target of similar false closure claims as recently as 2016, suggesting that the chain's high brand recognition and emotional salience make it a reliable "attention asset" for misinformation producers operating in the attention economy.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The Taco Bell closure rumor exists within a broader ecosystem of manufactured misinformation that has become a significant feature of the digital media landscape. Understanding this ecosystem — its economic incentives, its distribution mechanisms, and its relationship to legitimate journalism — is essential for evaluating not just this specific claim but the entire category of sensationalized business-failure content that proliferates across social media and advertising networks.

The digital advertising arbitrage model that produced the Taco Bell rumor is not a marginal or exotic phenomenon. It represents a substantial economic sector within the digital media economy. Research by the Global Disinformation Index estimated that the advertising revenue flowing to misinformation and disinformation websites globally exceeded $235 million annually as of 2019, with a significant portion generated through exactly the kind of slideshow-driven arbitrage that the Money Pop article exemplified (Global Disinformation Index, 2019, *The Quarter Billion Dollar Question*). The programmatic advertising systems that power most digital ad placement operate largely without human editorial oversight, matching ads to audience segments based on demographic and behavioral data rather than content quality or factual accuracy.

This creates what economists call a "market failure": the private incentives of content producers (maximize attention and pageviews) diverge from the social interest (accurate information about the economy and specific businesses). The market failure is structural, not the result of individual bad actors. Money Pop and similar operations are rational economic agents responding to the incentive structures created by the digital advertising ecosystem. Changing their behavior requires changing the incentives — a challenge that has proven resistant to both market self-regulation and government intervention.

The impact of false closure rumors on the businesses they target is a dimension that receives less attention than it deserves. While Taco Bell's strong market position likely insulated it from any measurable economic harm, smaller or more vulnerable businesses targeted by similar rumors may experience real consequences. Research on "information cascades" in financial markets (Bikhchandani et al., 1992, *Journal of Political Economy*) has demonstrated that false information about a company's viability can become self-fulfilling under certain conditions: if enough customers, suppliers, or investors believe a business is failing, their behavioral responses (avoiding the business, demanding accelerated payment terms, selling equity) can contribute to the very failure that was falsely predicted.

The Taco Bell case also illuminates the geography of digital misinformation. The Money Pop article was distributed through programmatic advertising networks that operate across geographic boundaries, meaning that the false closure claim could reach readers regardless of whether they lived near a Taco Bell location or had any direct relationship with the brand. This geographic decontextualization is a distinctive feature of digital misinformation compared to its pre-digital antecedents: whereas a false rumor about a local restaurant's closure would historically have been bounded by the geographic reach of word-of-mouth communication, the same rumor distributed through digital advertising networks can reach millions of people simultaneously, generating attention (and advertising revenue) at scale.

Taco Bell's actual strategic trajectory in 2020 — the Go Mobile concept, the continued expansion of its restaurant network, the strong financial performance relative to competitors — represents a story that is in some ways more interesting than the false closure narrative but that lacks the emotional urgency that drives viral sharing. Research on "information asymmetry" in media consumption (Hamilton, 2004, *All the News That's Fit to Sell*) has documented a persistent pattern in which negative, threatening, or alarming information receives disproportionate attention relative to positive or neutral information of equivalent significance. Taco Bell's expansion is a good-news story about a successful business; Taco Bell's closure would be a bad-news story about economic failure and cultural loss. The attention economy systematically privileges the latter over the former.

The Yum! Brands corporate structure that encompasses Taco Bell, KFC, and Pizza Hut provides an interesting comparative lens. Within the same parent company, three brands experienced the pandemic differently: Taco Bell thrived, KFC managed adequately, and Pizza Hut faced more significant challenges (though it also benefited from increased delivery demand). These divergent outcomes within a single corporate family underscore the importance of business model specifics — particularly the presence or absence of drive-through infrastructure, the price point relative to consumer budgets during a recession, and the adaptability of the menu to takeout and delivery formats — in determining pandemic resilience.

The founding story of Taco Bell, which Snopes recounted, carries its own cultural significance. Glen Bell's origin story — watching the lines at a Mexican restaurant from his hot dog stand and deciding to enter the market — is a classic American entrepreneurship narrative. The chain's growth from a single location in Downey, California, to a global network of over 7,400 restaurants represents one of the more dramatic scaling stories in restaurant industry history. The false closure narrative implicitly threatens this arc of growth and success, which may partly explain why it generates such strong emotional responses: it contradicts not just a brand's current status but the narrative of American entrepreneurial success that the brand symbolizes.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Taco Bell claim presents a fact-checking scenario that is simultaneously straightforward and deeply instructive. The factual question — is Taco Bell going out of business? — has a clear, verifiable answer (no), supported by publicly available corporate earnings data, restaurant count figures, and strategic announcements. But the simplicity of the factual rebuttal belies the complexity of the informational environment that produced the false claim and the methodological challenges that environment creates for fact-checking practice.

Jordan Liles's investigation for Snopes involved several distinct analytical steps. First, he traced the claim to its source — the Money Pop slideshow article — and identified the discrepancy between how the article was distributed (with alarming headlines suggesting closure) and what it actually said (more modestly, that there would be "cutbacks"). This source analysis revealed that the misinformation was primarily a function of distribution framing rather than content fabrication: the article's text was hedged, but its advertising and social media presentation was not.

Second, Liles obtained and cited concrete financial data from Yum! Brands' quarterly earnings reports, establishing not merely that Taco Bell was not closing but that it was actively growing. This approach — going beyond the negation of a false claim to provide positive evidence of the actual situation — represents best practice in fact-checking methodology. Research by Nyhan and Reifler (2010, *Political Behavior*) on the "backfire effect" (now understood to be more limited than originally hypothesized but still relevant in certain contexts) suggested that simple negations of false beliefs can be less effective than providing alternative, accurate narratives that fill the informational void left by the debunked claim.

Third, Liles contextualized the claim within the broader advertising arbitrage ecosystem, explaining the economic incentives that produced it. This structural analysis distinguishes journalistic fact-checking from simple binary correction. By explaining not just that the claim was false but why it existed and how it reached audiences, the fact-check provided readers with diagnostic tools — frameworks for recognizing similar claims in the future — rather than merely adjudicating a single instance.

The identification of the 2016 precedent — a troll website that had published a similar Taco Bell closure hoax — added historical depth to the analysis. The recurrence of the same false claim about the same brand, separated by four years and produced through different mechanisms (troll site vs. advertising arbitrage slideshow), illustrated a pattern that individual fact-checks, evaluated in isolation, might not reveal. This longitudinal perspective is a strength of Snopes' accumulated database of fact-checked claims, which allows individual investigations to draw on historical precedent.

The Money Pop article's structure — 50 pages aggregating information about multiple restaurant chains — created a specific challenge for the fact-checking process. The article did not make a single, easily quotable false claim about Taco Bell; instead, it embedded a relatively innocuous mention of the chain within a vast structure of content whose overall framing, combined with the advertising used to drive traffic to it, created a false impression. Fact-checking organizations must navigate the distinction between "this article contains a false claim" and "this article's distribution creates a false impression" — a distinction that maps onto broader debates in media literacy about the relative importance of content accuracy versus contextual integrity.

The programmatic advertising dimension of the claim raises questions about the scope of fact-checking responsibility. The false impression about Taco Bell was created primarily by advertisements — the promotional thumbnails and headlines that circulated on social media and advertising networks — rather than by the article itself. These advertisements were generated and distributed through automated systems that operate largely outside the purview of traditional editorial fact-checking. Liles addressed this dimension by analyzing the advertising arbitrage model, but the systemic question — whose responsibility is it to ensure that advertising for content does not create false impressions that the content itself does not explicitly make? — remains open.

The Taco Bell case also illustrates the challenge of fact-checking claims that are "false by implication" rather than "false by assertion." The Money Pop article did not state that Taco Bell was closing. It stated that there would be "cutbacks." But the article's distribution framework — the headlines, the structure, the context created by grouping Taco Bell with chains that were genuinely struggling — created a strong implication of closure that many readers absorbed. Fact-checkers typically evaluate claims rather than implications, but the Taco Bell case demonstrates that implications can be more consequential than claims when the implication reaches a wider audience than the claim and when the distribution mechanism is designed to maximize the gap between implication and claim.

The broader question of how fact-checking organizations should prioritize their resources in an environment of manufactured misinformation is raised by cases like this one. The Taco Bell closure rumor was not a unique, artisanal piece of misinformation — it was an industrial product, one instance of a content category (false business-failure claims) generated at scale by operations optimized for advertising revenue. Fact-checking each individual instance is necessary but insufficient: addressing the phenomenon requires structural analysis of the incentive systems that produce it, combined with advocacy for platform policies and advertising standards that would reduce the profitability of misinformation production. Liles's fact-check contributed to both dimensions — correcting the specific claim while illuminating the system that produced it — and in doing so exemplified the dual role of fact-checking as both immediate correction and structural diagnosis.
