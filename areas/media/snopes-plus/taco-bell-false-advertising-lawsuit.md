## §1 — Claim & Origin

On July 31, 2023, a Reddit post in the r/ExpectationVsReality subreddit uploaded by user u/dylantherabbit2016 displayed a pair of photographs with the caption "A portion from the lawsuit suing Taco Bell for false advertising." The images showed side-by-side comparisons of two Taco Bell menu items — the Crunchwrap Supreme and the Grande Crunchwrap — as they appeared in promotional advertising versus what customers allegedly received in practice. The advertised versions depicted plump, ingredient-rich wraps with visible layers of beef, vegetables, and sauces protruding evenly to the edges. The received versions allegedly appeared substantially deflated, with ingredients bunched toward the center and much of the tortilla left unfilled.

Within hours, the images had propagated beyond Reddit to Facebook, Twitter (now X), and TikTok, where a video by user @haseeblegal accumulated significant view counts. The virality was propelled by the visceral "expectation vs. reality" framing, a genre of food-disappointment content that reliably resonates with social media audiences conditioned to distrust fast-food marketing imagery. However, no post in the initial wave substantiated the claim with a direct link to any legal filing, leaving open the question of whether the images were fabricated, repurposed from a prior viral moment, or authentic documents from a real lawsuit.

Snopes investigator Izz Scott LaMagdeleine verified the claim on August 2, 2023, using the Public Access to Court Electronic Records (PACER) federal court records database, which hosts all filings in U.S. federal district courts. The photographs appeared verbatim on page two of the formal complaint filed in the case *Siragusa v. Taco Bell Corp.*, United States District Court for the Eastern District of New York, filed July 31, 2023 — the same day the Reddit post appeared. The simultaneous posting of the complaint and the Reddit image is consistent with either the plaintiff's legal team or an observer with early access to the PACER filing distributing court exhibit images to social media as part of a publicity strategy.

## §2 — Verdict & Evidence Base

**Verdict: True.**

The evidence base for this verdict is unambiguous and independently verifiable. The federal court filing is a matter of public record accessible to anyone with a PACER account. The core evidential chain is:

1. **Filing identity**: *Siragusa v. Taco Bell Corp.*, U.S. District Court, E.D.N.Y., filed July 31, 2023. Plaintiff: Frank Siragusa.
2. **Class definition**: Siragusa filed on behalf of himself and all other "similarly situated individuals" who purchased a Crunchwrap Supreme, Grande Crunchwrap, Vegan Crunchwrap, Mexican Pizza, or Veggie Mexican Pizza between July 31, 2020, and the final resolution of the case.
3. **Allegation**: The complaint accused Taco Bell of "unfair and deceptive trade practices for falsely advertising the amount of beef and/or ingredients" in the four specified products.
4. **Exhibit location**: The side-by-side photographs circulated on social media were embedded directly within the complaint, appearing on page two, making them part of the formal legal record rather than supplementary materials or media-fabricated comparisons.
5. **Relief sought**: (a) monetary damages fully compensating all class members who purchased an "Overstated Menu Item"; (b) injunctive relief requiring corrected advertising or discontinuation of the offending products; (c) such other relief as the Court deems appropriate.

The additional exhibits in the complaint included similar comparison photographs for the Mexican Pizza and Vegan Crunchwrap, both showing analogous disparities between promotional images and alleged real-world portions. These additional images were not prominently featured in the viral social media posts, suggesting the public focused on the most visually striking Crunchwrap comparisons.

The claim as stated on social media — that the circulated photographs were from a lawsuit against Taco Bell for alleged false advertising — is therefore fully confirmed. No material element of the claim was false or exaggerated.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: The Documentary Capture Problem in Food Advertising Litigation

The extraordinary speed at which these lawsuit exhibits propagated on social media illuminates a relatively underexplored epistemic phenomenon: the *documentary capture moment* in consumer-protection litigation. Lawsuits against major fast-food corporations for portion misrepresentation are not new — Wendy's, McDonald's, and Burger King have all faced analogous complaints in various jurisdictions — but the viral mechanism by which a federal court exhibit can simultaneously function as a legal submission and a social media content object within hours of filing represents a qualitatively novel information dynamic.

This matters epistemically because courts and social media infrastructures operate under radically different standards of evidentiary weight. A photograph appearing in a federal complaint is not, by itself, proof that Taco Bell falsely advertised its products. The images were curated by plaintiff's counsel to support a legal narrative; the complaint has not been adjudicated; and the defendant is entitled to contest both the representativeness of the comparison photographs and the methodology by which they were obtained. In legal terms, the images demonstrate that a claimed discrepancy was alleged under oath and documented — nothing more.

Yet on social media, the framing collapsed this distinction entirely. Users treated the court-exhibit photographs as authoritative evidence of proven wrongdoing, conflating "images used in a lawsuit" with "images proving Taco Bell committed fraud." This epistemic collapse is structurally analogous to what Lewandowsky et al. (2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) described in the context of correction effects — audiences anchor on vivid initial representations and resist updating toward more nuanced evidentiary assessments.

The food-advertising domain has its own distinct literature on expectation-reality gaps. Wansink and Chandon (2006, *Journal of Marketing Research*) documented systematic overestimation of portion sizes in fast-food marketing imagery relative to prepared items, finding that these disparities were not merely cosmetic but reliably influenced consumer caloric expectations and, downstream, actual consumption behavior. While Wansink's subsequent retractions on unrelated methodological grounds have complicated his broader corpus, the core observational finding in that specific study — that fast-food promotional images predictably exceed actual portion dimensions — has been replicated in independent consumer research (Chandon & Wansink, 2012, *Journal of Consumer Psychology*).

What the Taco Bell lawsuit adds, epistemically, is the formalization of this documented phenomenon into a legal claim of deceptive trade practice. The gap between what the *Siragusa* complaint terms "Advertised" and "Received" items exists in a field where a structured body of social science evidence could theoretically support the economic harm element of the plaintiff's case. Whether that potential was actualized in the litigation is beyond the scope of Snopes' verification — but it represents a genuine epistemic frontier where food marketing research, consumer psychology, and consumer protection law intersect without a well-established evidential bridge.

## §4 — Broader Context & Related Claims

This lawsuit exists within a longer pattern of fast-food litigation over portion and ingredient misrepresentation in the United States. Several closely related cases provide useful context:

**McDonald's burger size litigation (2022)**: A class-action filed in the Southern District of New York in 2022 similarly alleged that McDonald's promotional photographs of burgers depicted items significantly larger and more ingredient-rich than those served to consumers. The complaint relied on photographic comparisons structurally identical to those used in *Siragusa*. As of the time of writing, the outcome remained uncertain.

**Subway "footlong" settlements**: In 2013, class-action plaintiffs alleged that Subway's "footlong" sandwiches routinely measured less than 12 inches. Subway reached a settlement that required franchisees to use measuring tools, though a federal appeals court later vacated the settlement on grounds that the class had suffered no legally cognizable injury (Subway Footlong Sandwich Litigation, 7th Cir. 2017). This precedent is legally significant for *Siragusa* because it highlights the challenge plaintiffs face in demonstrating concrete economic harm when products are sold by name rather than by a specific stated weight or dimension.

**Regulation of food advertising imagery**: The FDA and FTC share jurisdiction over food marketing claims in the United States. Cheftel et al. (2019, *Food Policy*) conducted a comparative analysis of regulatory frameworks governing food imagery in the EU, US, and Australia, finding that US regulations are the least prescriptive regarding the relationship between promotional photographs and served portions — a regulatory gap that may facilitate the practices alleged in lawsuits like *Siragusa*.

The Taco Bell lawsuit also invites comparison with analogous consumer protection actions in other industries. The pharmaceutical advertising domain, for instance, has evolved strict regulatory requirements through the FDA's Division of Drug Information imposing substantive evidentiary standards on "before and after" type comparisons. No equivalent standard governs how fast-food companies photograph their products for promotional purposes.

## §5 — Counterfactual Analysis & Gaps

Several important questions are not resolved by the Snopes verdict:

**Gap 1: Outcome of the lawsuit.** The *Snopes* check simply verifies that the photographs were authentic court exhibits — it does not track the disposition of the case. Class action certification, merits adjudication, and any settlement or dismissal are subsequent questions of equal or greater significance for understanding whether the underlying claims about Taco Bell's practices are substantiated.

**Gap 2: Methodological validity of comparison photographs.** The exhibits in the complaint do not specify how the comparison photographs were obtained — whether by professional food stylists under controlled conditions for the advertising images, or whether the "received" images were cherry-picked from disproportionately bad preparations. Plaintiff's counsel has a professional incentive to select the most extreme comparisons. Rigorous empirical assessment would require a systematic sampling methodology across multiple locations and preparation conditions, analogous to what Chandon and Wansink (2007, *Journal of Consumer Research*) employed in their controlled studies of calorie estimation in fast-food contexts.

**Gap 3: Economic harm quantification.** The complaint alleges compensatory damages without specifying a methodology for calculating how much individual consumers were harmed. Economic literature on "overstated" food products generally models harm as the difference between willingness-to-pay for the advertised product and willingness-to-pay for the received product — a hedonic price gap. Without this analysis, the damages claim is legally vulnerable.

**Gap 4: Geographic and temporal scope.** The class period (July 31, 2020 onward) spans the COVID-19 pandemic, during which supply chain disruptions may have independently affected ingredient quantities at fast-food locations. Whether this constitutes an affirmative defense for Taco Bell is an open empirical and legal question.

**Gap 5: Representativeness of viral images.** The photographs that went viral showed the Crunchwrap Supreme and Grande Crunchwrap. The complaint also included exhibits for the Mexican Pizza and Vegan Crunchwrap. The differential virality of the Crunchwrap images versus the others suggests that public engagement was shaped as much by visual salience and meme-readiness as by the comprehensiveness of the legal allegations.

## §6 — Significance & Takeaways

The Taco Bell false-advertising lawsuit claim is significant at multiple overlapping levels.

**At the verification level**, it demonstrates how quickly authentic legal documents can travel across social media ecosystems in forms that simultaneously preserve their documentary origin and strip their evidentiary context. The photographs were real court exhibits — this is the true and important finding — but their virality also cultivated widespread public misreadings of what an authentic court exhibit proves.

**At the consumer protection level**, the case surfaces a structural tension in the regulatory framework governing fast-food advertising. The FTC's general prohibition on deceptive advertising practices applies in principle, but enforcement in the fast-food sector has historically been sparse. Plaintiff-driven class actions like *Siragusa* therefore function as private proxies for regulatory enforcement.

**At the media literacy level**, this case exemplifies the recurring pattern wherein official-looking documents — court filings, government reports, scientific papers — are shared on social media with the implicit claim that their official status constitutes proof of their contents. Teaching audiences to distinguish between "this document exists and its existence has been verified" and "the claims within this document have been legally or scientifically established" is a fundamental competency for navigating an information environment in which institutional documents routinely circulate as viral content objects.

**At the epistemic policy level**, the case raises questions about whether PACER's model of open access to federal court filings — a genuine transparency achievement — requires complementary public education infrastructure to prevent routine misreadings of what complaints, as distinct from judgments, actually establish.

The Snopes rating of **True** is correct, narrow, and appropriately scoped: the photographs were indeed from the lawsuit. The broader questions the case raises remain open, institutionally, legally, and scientifically.
