# TikTok Video 'Exposes' How Taco Bell Makes Its Refried Beans?

## 1. The Claim

From at least May 2019, TikTok users posting from Taco Bell kitchens had been showing what they presented as a behind-the-scenes glimpse at how the chain prepares one of its most iconic ingredients: the refried beans. The videos showed a preparation process that surprised many viewers—not because it revealed anything improper but because it diverged sharply from the mental model most consumers held. Rather than starting from whole beans cooked and then mashed in-kitchen, Taco Bell employees were shown working with small dehydrated bean pieces (described variously as resembling "rabbit food," "pasta," or "macaroni noodles") that are reconstituted with boiling water in a metal vat. A proprietary spice blend is folded in, and the mixture is cooked down to yield the familiar creamy refried bean texture served across the chain's menu.

By December 2020, TikTok user @maniic666 posted a video with the text overlay "Taco bells beans...look like rabbit food," which quickly amassed more than 400,000 views in four days. The account identified the poster as a former Taco Bell manager from Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. Prior TikTok videos on the same subject from accounts @pretty_young_thang95 (May 2019), @homerolara0 (June 2020), and a video going viral on Twitter in September 2020 had collectively accumulated more than 10 million views. The consistent viral performance of this content—whether from 2019 or 2020—indicates something structurally interesting: this is information the public repeatedly wishes to know, repeatedly discovers, and repeatedly reacts to as if newly exposed.

The scare-quoted "Exposes" in the article's title (matching Snopes's own framing) signals the epistemic situation: the Daily Dot's headline read "Employee 'exposes' how Taco Bell beans are made in viral TikTok video," but nothing in the exposure required quotation marks in a whistle-blowing sense. The videos showed a legal, disclosed preparation process consistent with publicly documented Taco Bell practices. The "expose" framing was applied by media and commenters; the videos themselves were simply employee demonstrations.

## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes journalist Jordan Liles investigated the December 2020 wave of videos and confirmed their accuracy through Taco Bell's own corporate response and through documentation of the commercial availability of comparable dehydrated refried bean products.

Taco Bell provided a direct statement: "In order to provide the same flavor and consistency at all of our locations, our refried beans are delivered dehydrated and prepared in-restaurant to ensure the best quality. We use a proprietary spice blend in our refried beans to deliver the craveable flavor our guests know and love and are certified vegan by the American Vegetarian Association. We encourage fans to check out our ingredient statements on our website at www.tacobell.com/nutrition/ingredients."

This statement accomplishes several things. It confirms the dehydrated delivery method explicitly and without corporate embarrassment—Taco Bell did not attempt to deny or minimize the process. It frames the dehydration as a quality and consistency tool rather than a cost-cutting measure, positioning uniformity of flavor across thousands of locations as a consumer benefit. It highlights the vegan certification as a quality attribute, connecting to growing consumer interest in plant-based eating. And it directs consumers to the ingredients list, which was publicly available on the Taco Bell website.

The ingredients list for Taco Bell's refried beans at the time of Liles's investigation included: pinto beans, soybean oil, and seasoning (salt, sugar, spice, beet powder (VC), natural flavors, sunflower oil, maltodextrin, corn flour, trehalose, and modified cornstarch). This is a disclosed ingredient list consistent with dehydrated refried bean products generally. The beet powder (VC) provides color stability; maltodextrin is a carbohydrate commonly used as a carrier for flavorings in dehydrated food applications; trehalose is a disaccharide used as a stabilizer; modified cornstarch provides thickening. None of these ingredients are additives that raise food safety concerns.

Liles further contextualized the dehydrated bean process by documenting its commercial ubiquity: instant refried bean products were sold by Walmart (Herdez brand instant refried beans), by HEB grocery stores (Santa Fe Bean Company's dehydrated refried beans), and through Amazon from multiple brands including Mexicali Rose, Fantastic Foods, and Casa Corona. Taco Bell-branded refried beans in canned form were also available for sale in grocery stores through Amazon. The dehydrated bean preparation is, in other words, a standard product category—not an industrial secret or a preparation method that Taco Bell uniquely developed or that is atypical of how food service operations handle beans.

Snopes had covered Taco Bell—including food-safety and ingredient claims—for more than twenty years before this investigation. Prior covered items included misleading claims about cockroach eggs, the "Grade D but edible" meat urban legend, and the nature of Taco Bell's Cinnamon Twists dessert. The refried bean episode fits within this longer history of public fascination and occasional anxiety about what, exactly, Taco Bell's food contains and how it is prepared.

## 3. Epistemic Novelty: Dehydration, Reconstitution, and the Folk Theory of Food Authenticity

The Taco Bell refried bean case brings into sharp focus a largely unexamined assumption in how consumers reason about fast-food authenticity: what I will term the **continuous-process presumption**. This is the background belief that "real" food preparation involves continuous physical transformation of recognizable ingredients through cooking processes that preserve the identifiability of the source material until very late in the preparation chain. Beans should be soaked, cooked, and mashed—the logic runs—because that is the continuous process that makes beans into refried beans. When a preparation method involves discontinuous transformation (dehydration at a factory, reconstitution at a restaurant), the assumption is violated in a way that triggers authenticity skepticism even when the final product may be chemically and nutritionally equivalent.

This folk theory of food authenticity has been studied in the context of food naturalness perception. Rozin et al. (2004, *Appetite*, 43(2), 147–154) documented that consumers apply a "natural process" heuristic when evaluating foods: foods that pass through fewer recognized stages of industrial processing are rated as more natural and more trustworthy, independent of any evidence about safety or nutritional content. Dehydrated refried beans fail this heuristic visually—the pellet form looks nothing like beans in any stage of conventional cooking—and this failure activates the authenticity-skepticism response that generates the "expose" framing.

What this means epistemically is that the consumer reaction to the TikTok videos is not primarily an informational reaction (learning something surprising that changes an assessment) but a **confirmation of prior unease** dressed in the rhetorical form of discovery. The prior unease—an ambient suspicion that fast food is not prepared the way home cooking is prepared—is confirmed by the visual evidence even though the visual evidence does not actually reveal anything problematic. The confirmation-of-unease dynamic is epistemologically distinct from correction of error and is less well studied in the misinformation literature, which tends to focus on the correction of explicit false beliefs rather than the dynamics of ambient suspicion and selective confirmation.

Importantly, the "expose" genre applied to Taco Bell's beans had already cycled through three distinct waves by December 2020: the original May 2019 TikTok, the summer 2020 viral moment, and the December 2020 resurgence. Each cycle treated the information as newly shocking despite it having already achieved viral circulation. This suggests the continuous-process presumption is resistant to updating even from very high-profile prior encounters with the disconfirming evidence—consumers who saw the 2019 video may have had the same reaction to the December 2020 video as if encountering it for the first time. The durability of folk-theory violation as a reaction suggests that informational correction in this domain is particularly ineffective.

## 4. Context: Taco Bell's Ingredient Transparency Record and Fast-Food Bean Preparation

Taco Bell's relationship with consumer anxiety about its ingredients is long and richly documented. The "Grade D but edible" meat urban legend—asserting that Taco Bell used government-surplus low-grade meat—circulated widely in American popular culture for years and was definitively addressed by Snopes. In 2011, a lawsuit was filed claiming that Taco Bell's "seasoned ground beef" did not contain enough meat to legally qualify as beef; the company publicly rejected the claims and the suit was subsequently dropped. In 2014, Taco Bell launched a "Cantina Bell" menu line partially in response to consumer demand for more recognizable ingredients and preparation methods.

The refried bean question sits within this history of consumer skepticism that Taco Bell has had to actively manage. The company's public-facing ingredients documentation—including the full ingredient list for each menu item on the Taco Bell website—reflects a transparency commitment that, in context, can be read as a strategic response to decades of rumors. Making ingredient information easily accessible online serves a preemptive fact-checking function: curious consumers can answer questions about composition without requiring an employee or journalist to investigate.

The vegan certification is an interesting dimension. Taco Bell's refried beans are certified vegan by the American Vegetarian Association (AVA), which requires that no animal products are used in either ingredients or preparation. This certification provides a third-party verification of ingredient content that goes beyond mere self-assertion. The pinto bean, soybean oil, and spice-blend ingredients are consistent with a vegan classification. Taco Bell also sells the beans as a standalone consumer product through Amazon and grocery retailers, which means the product is subject to standard food labeling requirements including full ingredient disclosure—a regulatory framework that operates entirely independently of any TikTok-based scrutiny.

The broader landscape of fast-food bean preparation is not well documented in the public-facing record. Taco Bell's use of dehydrated beans is consistent with common food service practices in high-volume operations. Dehydration as a food preservation and distribution method has significant logistical advantages: lower weight and volume for shipping, stable shelf life without refrigeration during transit, and consistent product characteristics that reduce kitchen skill requirements for reconstitution. The Herdez and Santa Fe Bean Company instant refried bean products that Liles identified as commercially available are likely manufactured using similar industrial processes. That grocery-store instant refried beans generate essentially no consumer "expose" content—despite being visually identical to the Taco Bell preparation before reconstitution—underscores that the Taco Bell context, not the dehydration method itself, is what generates the alarm framing.

## 5. The Former Employee Video and the Ethics of Fast-Food Kitchen Documentation

The @maniic666 TikTok account provides a specific and interesting case study in the ethical and legal dimensions of kitchen documentation. The poster self-described as a former Taco Bell manager, posted a vaping video from the same environment with the caption "Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania," and noted when challenged about the vaping video "I haven't worked there in 6 months." The beans video itself showed no improper practices—only the dehydrated beans preparation—but the vaping video posted the same day suggested an employee who had a contentious departing relationship with the employer and was posting defiantly rather than in a spirit of consumer education.

This raises meaningful questions about the credibility and context of employee kitchen documentation that the "True" rating does not fully address. Liles's investigation confirmed that the beans video showed an authentic process, but the context of the poster's departure and stated attitude complicates any reading of the video as a neutral informational disclosure. The information in the beans video is accurate and consistent with Taco Bell's own confirmed practices; the framing of that information—as a shocking "rabbit food" reveal—reflects the poster's evident intention to generate alarm or disgust rather than inform.

Research on source credibility in digital media has found that consumers discount information from low-credibility sources even when the factual content is accurate (Pornpitakpan, 2004, *Journal of Advertising Research*, 44(3), 243–255). In the Taco Bell case, this creates a curious inversion: the source's apparent motivational framing (create alarm, entertain, possibly express grievance against former employer) is unreliable as a guide to interpretation, but the factual content of the video is confirmed by the corporation itself. The signal and the noise are thus distinctly separated: ignore the sensational framing, retain the factual content.

The workplace legal dimension also deserves acknowledgment. Pizza Hut, McDonald's, Taco Bell, and other QSR operators typically include prohibitions on unauthorized photography or video in employee handbooks; some jurisdictions' health codes further restrict photography in food preparation areas. Whether the @maniic666 account's videos were legally problematic under the terms of the poster's prior employment or applicable health codes is a question not addressed by Snopes's True/False assessment but relevant to a complete picture of the episode.

## 6. Verdict, Gaps, and Ongoing Questions

Snopes rates the claim **True**. The TikTok videos showing Taco Bell refried bean preparation from dehydrated pellets were authentic. Taco Bell confirmed the process directly. The ingredients are publicly documented, the method is standard across commercial food service, and comparable products are commercially available to consumers in grocery stores. The "expose" framing was manufactured by the sensational genre conventions of fast-food kitchen TikTok content rather than by any genuine irregularity in the preparation process.

**EARC classification: E (Empirical)**. The claim is factual: the TikTok videos showed a real preparation process, the process was confirmed by the company, and the ingredients are publicly documented.

**Gap analysis:** The primary gap is the absence of any comparative data on how the dehydrated-reconstituted preparation compares nutritionally, microbiologically, or texturally to from-scratch refried bean preparation. Taco Bell's claim that the method ensures "flavor and consistency" is plausible but not independently verified. An independent comparative study of dehydrated versus from-scratch commercial refried beans—examining final product composition, texture profile, sodium content, and any degradation of nutritional value through dehydration—would provide a more complete picture of whether the preparation method has any meaningful consumer-facing implications beyond aesthetics.

A secondary gap: the three-cycle virality pattern (May 2019, summer 2020, December 2020) is curious and understudied. Why does the same information repeatedly achieve viral status as if newly discovered? Whether this reflects the TikTok platform's discovery algorithm surface effects (new users never saw prior iterations), the persistent psychological impact of food preparation disclosure on consumers with strong continuous-process presumptions, or some interaction of platform mechanics and consumer psychology is not established in the available record.

A tertiary gap involves the specific characterization of the spice blend as "proprietary." Taco Bell provides an ingredient list that includes "natural flavors" as a component—a category that under FDA regulations can encompass a wide range of flavoring substances without specific enumeration. The proprietary claim regarding the spice blend effectively places a portion of the product's flavor profile outside full public scrutiny. Whether this distinction matters for consumers with dietary restrictions, allergies, or ingredient preferences is a question the ingredient list cannot fully answer, and one that the "certified vegan" and "GRAS provision" documentation partially but not completely addresses.
