# "Tapping the Top of a Soda Can Prevents It from Fizzing Over"


## 1. The Claim

The claim under examination is that tapping the top (or sides) of a freshly shaken carbonated beverage can will prevent or significantly reduce the explosive fizzing that occurs when the can is opened. This practice is nearly universal in Western culture, passed down through generations as practical wisdom. The claim encompasses several variants: some practitioners tap the top of the can, others tap the sides, and still others perform a combination of both actions. The underlying belief is consistent across all variants—that mechanical agitation of the can's exterior will somehow calm the pressurized carbonated liquid within.

This belief extends beyond casual consumption into professional settings. Bartenders routinely tap shaken cocktail mixers, and even some beverage industry workers employ this technique when handling carbonated products. The practice has become so embedded in cultural consciousness that it appears in popular media, instructional videos, and informal educational content as accepted fact.

The claim carries implicit assumptions about carbonation physics, bubble nucleation, and the relationship between external mechanical forces and internal liquid dynamics. It suggests that brief, targeted impacts on the container can meaningfully alter the behavior of dissolved carbon dioxide gas within the liquid, reducing the likelihood of rapid degassing upon pressure release.


## 2. What's Actually True

To properly evaluate this claim, we must first understand the physics of carbonation and bubble formation. Carbonated beverages contain carbon dioxide gas dissolved in liquid under pressure. The solubility of CO2 in water follows Henry's Law—it increases with pressure and decreases with temperature. When a carbonated container is opened, the sudden pressure drop creates supersaturation, where more CO2 is dissolved than can remain stable at atmospheric pressure.

The dramatic fizzing that sometimes occurs results from rapid degassing—the CO2 comes out of solution to form gas bubbles. The rate and intensity of this process depend on nucleation sites—microscopic irregularities or particles where bubble formation can begin. In a perfectly smooth container with perfectly pure liquid, degassing would be much slower and less dramatic.

Shaking a carbonated container creates numerous small bubbles throughout the liquid. These bubbles don't immediately rise to the surface; many become trapped against the container walls or remain suspended in the liquid. When the container is opened, these pre-existing bubbles serve as nucleation sites for rapid degassing, creating the explosive fizzing effect.

The tapping hypothesis suggests that mechanical impacts can dislodge wall-adhered bubbles, allowing them to rise to the surface and coalesce in the headspace before opening. This would theoretically reduce the number of nucleation sites available for rapid degassing when pressure is released.

From a purely theoretical standpoint, the mechanism has plausibility. Vibration can indeed dislodge adhered particles or bubbles from surfaces—this principle is used in industrial applications like ultrasonic cleaning. The question is whether the brief, low-intensity tapping typically applied to beverage containers generates sufficient force to meaningfully affect bubble behavior.


The epistemological landscape around this claim reveals several important considerations about folk science and practical knowledge. The persistence of the tapping practice despite limited scientific support illustrates how cultural transmission of techniques can occur independently of empirical validation.

From an experiential standpoint, many practitioners report subjective success with tapping. This creates an interesting epistemological tension—personal experience suggests efficacy while controlled studies indicate minimal effect. Several factors could explain this discrepancy:

Confirmation bias plays a significant role. People who practice tapping may selectively remember instances where it seemed to work while forgetting occasions when it didn't. The variable nature of carbonation fizzing—which depends on factors like temperature, agitation history, and time since shaking—provides ample opportunity for spurious correlation.

The placebo effect extends beyond medical contexts into everyday practices. The act of tapping may provide psychological reassurance that leads to more careful opening technique, inadvertently reducing fizzing through behavioral rather than physical mechanisms.

Selective attention to tapping technique might also matter. Practitioners who are more careful about their tapping approach may also be more careful about their opening approach, conflating technique refinement with tapping efficacy.

The waiting period inherent in most tapping practices—the time spent tapping is time not opening—may account for apparent success. Simply waiting 10-30 seconds after shaking allows natural bubble coalescence and migration processes to occur, reducing fizzing regardless of tapping.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The most rigorous scientific examination of this practice appears in a 2017 study by Sopina and Sander published in Royal Society Open Science. Their research specifically tested whether tapping carbonated containers affects degassing behavior upon opening. Using controlled experimental conditions, they compared the fizzing intensity of tapped versus untapped carbonated water bottles after shaking.

Their methodology involved standardized shaking protocols, consistent tapping procedures, and quantitative measurement of degassing rates. The results showed no statistically significant difference between tapped and untapped containers in terms of fizzing intensity or duration. The study concluded that tapping, as commonly practiced, does not meaningfully reduce carbonation fizzing.

However, the study also revealed interesting nuances. While top-tapping showed no effect, some evidence suggested that side-tapping might have minimal impact under certain conditions. The researchers noted that the location and intensity of tapping could theoretically matter, as different surfaces of the container have different relationships to bubble adhesion patterns.

Additional physics research provides context for understanding why the effect might be minimal. The adhesive forces holding small bubbles to container walls are quite strong relative to the mechanical energy delivered by casual tapping. Most tapping generates vibrations that propagate through the container material but transfer relatively little energy directly to the liquid-wall interface where bubbles are adhered.

The time factor also matters. Even if tapping does dislodge some bubbles, the brief interval between tapping and opening may not provide sufficient time for meaningful bubble coalescence and migration to the headspace. The dynamic process of bubble behavior in supersaturated liquids operates on timescales that may not align with typical human tapping-to-opening intervals.


## 4. Verdict

**Classification: CONTESTED**

**Gap Category: Phenomenologically Real**

The claim that tapping prevents soda fizzing represents a genuinely contested area where folk practice and scientific evidence don't fully align. While controlled studies suggest minimal practical effect from typical tapping techniques, the practice persists because it addresses a real phenomenon (carbonation fizzing) through a mechanism that has theoretical plausibility (bubble dislodging) even if practical efficacy is limited.

The "phenomenologically real" gap designation reflects the authentic experiential basis for the belief. People do observe variable fizzing behavior, tapping does involve real physical forces, and the outcomes do sometimes appear to correlate with the practice. The gap isn't between truth and falsehood but between folk understanding and scientific precision about mechanism and efficacy.

The practice isn't harmful and may provide marginal benefit under specific conditions, particularly when tapping involves waiting periods that allow natural degassing processes to occur. However, more reliable approaches exist for those who need predictable carbonation control: temperature management (colder liquids hold more CO2), gentle opening technique (gradual pressure release), and time (allowing natural settling after agitation).

For casual consumers, the choice to tap or not tap involves personal preference rather than scientific necessity. The practice represents harmless folk physics that provides psychological comfort and occasionally practical benefit, even if the causal relationship isn't as strong or consistent as commonly believed.

The most accurate statement about tapping would be: "Tapping may provide minimal benefit under some conditions, primarily through timing effects rather than direct bubble manipulation, but more reliable methods exist for controlling carbonation fizzing." This nuanced conclusion respects both scientific evidence and lived experience while providing practical guidance for those seeking effective carbonation management techniques.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The tapping phenomenon exists within a broader context of folk physics knowledge—intuitive beliefs about how physical systems behave that may or may not align with scientific understanding. These beliefs often emerge from pattern recognition in complex systems where multiple variables interact in ways that aren't immediately apparent to casual observation.

Similar practices exist across cultures and domains. Wine enthusiasts tap champagne bottles before opening, believing it reduces cork-popping violence. Beer servers sometimes tap kegs or taps before pouring, following traditions that may or may not have practical basis. These practices share common elements: they're low-cost, low-risk interventions that provide a sense of control over unpredictable physical processes.

The carbonation industry itself has developed various techniques for managing degassing in production and service contexts. Professional approaches typically involve temperature control, pressure management, and specialized dispensing equipment rather than mechanical tapping. while bubble behavior is indeed manageable through physical interventions, the casual tapping approach isn't among the most effective methods.

Research into bubble dynamics has applications beyond beverages, extending into materials science, chemical engineering, and even medical imaging where bubble behavior affects ultrasound quality. This broader scientific context provides validated principles for understanding carbonation physics, even when specific applications like can-tapping haven't been exhaustively studied.

The persistence of tapping practices also reflects broader human tendencies toward ritual behavior in uncertain situations. When facing unpredictable outcomes—like whether a shaken soda will fizz dramatically—people often develop ritualistic responses that provide psychological comfort even when practical benefit is unclear.

Educational implications emerge from this analysis. The gap between folk practice and scientific understanding suggests opportunities for science communication that acknowledge the intuitive appeal of certain beliefs while providing evidence-based alternatives. Rather than simply debunking tapping, effective education might explain why the impulse makes sense while demonstrating more reliable approaches to carbonation management.

The environmental context also matters. In professional or controlled settings where carbonation behavior is critical—such as beverage production, scientific experimentation, or culinary applications—understanding the limitations of tapping becomes important for developing more effective techniques.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Notably, Snopes.com has not directly addressed this specific claim in their fact-checking database. This absence is significant given the widespread nature of the belief and Snopes' typical coverage of common myths and folk practices. The lack of coverage may reflect the claim's position in a gray area between verifiable science and cultural practice—it's neither obviously false nor dramatically consequential enough to warrant urgent debunking.

The absence of mainstream fact-checking attention has allowed the practice to persist without systematic scrutiny. Unlike more sensational claims that attract immediate scientific or journalistic investigation, this everyday practice operates below the threshold of formal verification while remaining universally practiced.
