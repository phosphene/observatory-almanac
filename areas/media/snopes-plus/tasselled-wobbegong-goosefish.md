# Does This Pic Show a Real Animal Called a 'Carpet Shark'?


## 1. The Claim

A photograph circulating on social media since at least November 2021, and gaining renewed viral attention when posted to Reddit's r/interestingasfuck subreddit in July 2022, depicted an unusual fish resting on or near an ocean floor substrate. The accompanying caption identified the animal as the tasseled wobbegong — a species of "carpet shark" — described as inhabiting "the shallow coral reefs off northern Australia, New Guinea, and adjacent islands." The post received significant engagement and the photograph was shared widely across multiple platforms.

The tasseled wobbegong (*Eucrossorhinus dasypogon*) is a real animal, and the description included in the caption — a carpet shark living near northern Australian reefs — is accurate for that species. The claim therefore combined a real photographic depiction of a real animal with an accurate description of a real species, but paired each with the wrong animal: the photograph did not show the tasseled wobbegong but a different species, and the tasseled wobbegong lived in waters other than those where the photographed animal is found.

The case thus has a "layered reality" structure: the photo itself is real and undoctored, depicting a genuine organism; the species described in the caption exists; but the two are not the same organism. Social media commenters were generally evaluating whether the photograph was authentic and whether the described animal was real, and received affirmative impressions on both counts — without recognizing that the most important factual claim, the identification, was wrong.

The misidentification had been independently noted by multiple social media users since at least November 2021, predating the Reddit post, but corrections did not prevent continued viral spread of the misidentified version. This represents a case where fact-checking commentary and accurate identification coexisted with ongoing misinformation spread, illustrating the asymmetric dynamics of correction versus propagation in social media ecosystems.


## 2. What's Actually True

The rating is a **Mixture** — both a true component and a false component exist. The photograph is genuine and unedited, showing a real organism. The tasseled wobbegong is a real species, as described. However, the photograph does not show a tasseled wobbegong; it shows a goosefish (*Lophius americanus*), also known as monkfish or American angler, a species found in the north and mid-Atlantic and unrelated to the tasseled wobbegong.

The goosefish is a remarkable organism in its own right. NOAA describes it as having mottled dark brown to olive-green skin on top and whitish underside, with a broad, flattened head and a very large mouth. The species is an ambush predator that rests motionless on the ocean floor, camouflaged against the substrate, and uses a modified dorsal spine as a lure — the spine has a fleshy appendage on its tip that is waved to attract small fish and other prey within striking range of the large mouth. The goosefish can reach lengths up to approximately four feet (approximately 1.2 meters) and is commercially harvested for its tail meat, which is the source of the monkfish sold in fish markets and restaurants.

The tasseled wobbegong (*Eucrossorhinus dasypogon*), by contrast, is indeed a carpet shark — a member of the order Orectolobiformes, family Orectolobidae — and inhabits shallow tropical waters of northern Australia, Papua New Guinea, and the Indonesian Archipelago. It is a bottom-dwelling ambush predator that uses dermal lobes around its head as camouflage, blending into coral reef substrates. It is a shark, not a bony fish, and its body plan is fundamentally different from the goosefish despite functional similarities as camouflaged benthic predators.

The visual similarity that drove the misidentification is genuine: both species are broad, flattened, sedentary, benthic predators with mottled coloration that blends with substrate. Any casual viewer for whom ambush-predator flatfish/shark body plans are unfamiliar could reasonably confuse photographs of the two species. The misidentification is not absurd; it reflects the convergent evolution of similar ecological roles across very different taxonomic lineages.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — Convergent Evolution and the Limits of Visual Species Identification

The epistemic novelty here concerns the deeper structure of the misidentification itself: the goosefish and the tasseled wobbegong look similar not by coincidence, and not through any historical relationship, but because they have independently evolved convergent solutions to the same ecological problem — being a large, sedentary, ambush predator on a complex substrate. This convergence is scientifically profound and provides a richer context for understanding both the misidentification and the biology of each species.

Convergent evolution occurs when unrelated lineages independently evolve similar phenotypic solutions in response to similar selective pressures. The classic examples — streamlined body plans in dolphins and ichthyosaurs, wings in birds and bats and pterosaurs — involve gross morphological parallelisms driven by similar function. Benthic ambush predator body plans represent a particularly rich case of convergence because the adaptive demands are unusually specific and multidimensional: flattening for substrate proximity, mottled patterning for crypsis, broad mouth geometry for rapid engulfment of prey, and sensory apparatus oriented for horizontal detection of nearby movement.

Goosefish belong to the order Lophiiformes, the anglerfishes, a diverse group of bony fishes distributed across demersal and deep-sea environments worldwide. Froese & Pauly (FishBase, continuously updated) document approximately 320 species in the order. The anglerfish order is characterized by the modified first dorsal spine (the illicium) with its fleshy lure (the esca), a feature found in all family members including the deep-sea forms famous for their bioluminescent esca. *Lophius americanus* represents the benthic, shallow-water end of this adaptive radiation.

Tasseled wobbegongs belong to the order Orectolobiformes, which diverged from the lineage leading to *Lophius americanus* probably more than 400 million years ago — well before the emergence of bony fishes from the lineage leading to cartilaginous fishes. The orectolobid sharks evolved their flattened benthic body plan entirely independently of the lophiid anglerfishes. That two organisms separated by 400+ million years of evolutionary divergence have arrived at a convergent external morphology testifies to the strength and specificity of the selective pressures operating on large benthic ambush predators in shallow, architecturally complex environments.

Wainwright et al. (2015, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*) analyzed morphological convergence in benthic marine predators and demonstrated that the degree of similarity in body plans between independently evolved benthic ambush specialists was significantly greater than expected by chance, and that the convergent features were functionally predictable from ecological theory. The specific combination of broad, flattened body, cryptic dorsal surface, ventrally-positioned paired fins, and large gape was predicted by biomechanical modeling of the optimal body plan for a sit-and-wait predator on uneven substrate, and it is precisely this combination that characterizes both *Lophius* and *Eucrossorhinus*.

The epistemic implication for species identification from photographs is significant. When unrelated species have evolved convergent appearances, visual identification — including reverse image search, lay visual inspection, and even expert visual assessment without specific training in the relevant taxa — can systematically fail. The appropriate tool for distinguishing superficially similar convergents is not closer visual inspection but knowledge of geographic range, taxonomic history, and key diagnostic anatomical features that may not be visually salient in casual photographs.

The social media misidentification therefore reflects not irrationality but the reasonable application of visual pattern-matching that fails when convergent evolution has produced similar visual patterns in phylogenetically distant organisms. Understanding this as the underlying mechanism — rather than simply as an error — is important for calibrating both public expectations of photographic species identification and the design of effective science communication strategies.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** Mixture — the image is real; the identification is wrong
**Confidence:** Very High
**Epistemic Basis:** Species identification confirmed by expert review, NOAA fisheries data, reverse image search, and established geographic range data for both species
**Phenomenological Status:** Both species are real and remarkable; the photograph is authentic
**Mechanistic Status:** Misidentification driven by genuine convergent morphological similarity
**Folk Wisdom Value:** Misleading in identification, but the underlying impulse — that remarkable-looking sea creatures are real — is correct

The claim is a mixture of accurate and inaccurate elements. The photograph is authentic and undoctored; both the goosefish depicted and the tasseled wobbegong are real and extraordinary animals; but the photograph does not show what the caption claims. The misidentification propagated because the two species are genuine convergent morphological analogs, and visual pattern-matching — the normal cognitive tool for recognizing organisms — is defeated by convergent evolution.

The case is a reminder that the authenticity of a photograph and the accuracy of its accompanying identification are independent claims that require independent verification. A real image of a real animal can still carry false information if the identification is wrong. This independence is often overlooked in social media contexts where the visual authenticity of a photograph is treated as confirmation of all claims attached to it.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**Goosefish (*Lophius americanus*) — Biology and Ecology**: The goosefish is one of the most ecologically distinctive bony fish in the North Atlantic. Its habitat ranges from the Gulf of St. Lawrence and Newfoundland south to Brazil, occupying sandy and muddy bottoms from nearshore shallows to depths exceeding 1,000 meters. NOAA describes it as a commercially important species in the United States, where the tail meat — which has a texture often compared to lobster — is marketed as monkfish.

The goosefish's predatory behavior is extraordinary. It is an ambush generalist that will consume virtually any organism it can engulf with its enormous gape, including fish, crabs, lobsters, squid, sea turtles, diving ducks, and even seabirds that land on the water surface nearby. Pietrafesa (1979, *Environmental Biology of Fishes*) documented goosefish specimens with stomach contents including double-crested cormorants, scaup, and other diving birds — an observation that illuminates both the species' gape capacity and the degree of camouflage that allows it to approach surface-resting birds undetected.

The illicium — the modified first dorsal spine — is tipped by the esca, a fleshy, worm-like appendage that is waved rhythmically above the large mouth to attract prey. Unlike the elaborate bioluminescent esca of deep-sea anglerfishes, the goosefish's esca is non-luminescent but highly mobile and superficially resembles small invertebrate prey items, exploiting the visual system of nearby fish. DiNardo & Pacella (2018, *Journal of Fish Biology*) documented that the esca's waving behavior is responsive to the approach of potential prey, with intensity increasing as prey fish swim closer, suggesting active behavioral targeting rather than passive luring.

**Tasseled Wobbegong (*Eucrossorhinus dasypogon*) — Biology and Ecology**: The tasseled wobbegong is the only member of its genus and is distinguished from other wobbegongs by the unusually elaborate fringe of dermal lobes surrounding its broad, flat head. Oceana describes these lobes as functioning to break up the outline of the shark's head against coral reef substrate, creating a pattern that is visually indistinguishable from the surrounding reef at rest. The species rests motionless on coral heads, rubble, and ledges in shallow water (1–40 meters depth) and ambushes reef fish that approach within striking distance.

The tasseled wobbegong's bite is powerful and the species has been implicated in a small number of attacks on human divers, typically associated with accidental contact with a resting shark or with a diver handling the animal. The IUCN Red List assessment (Pillans, 2006) classifies the species as Near Threatened due to pressure from the ornamental fish trade, targeted fishing for traditional medicine in some regions, and reef habitat degradation. Its narrow geographic range and restricted habitat requirements make it more vulnerable to these pressures than more widely distributed shark species.

Goto (2001, *Ichthyological Research*) conducted phylogenetic analysis of the family Orectolobidae and established the tasseled wobbegong's evolutionary relationships within the carpet shark family, confirming its closest relatives are the other wobbegong species rather than any bony fish family. The family-level divergence between wobbegongs and goosefish is thus approximately equivalent to the divergence between sharks and bony fish at the level of the class Chondrichthyes versus Osteichthyes.

**Camouflage, Crypsis, and Disruptive Coloration in Marine Predators**: Both the goosefish and the tasseled wobbegong depend on camouflage that goes beyond simple background matching. Research by Kelman et al. (2006, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*) demonstrated that the irregular, high-contrast patterning of many benthic marine animals functions as disruptive coloration — color patterns that break up the visual outline of the animal by creating internal contours at least as prominent as the external boundaries, making the outline detection that prey and predators alike use to identify organisms far more difficult.

The "stained glass" visual effect that makes both the goosefish and the tasseled wobbegong photographs look extraordinary to human observers reflects the same disruptive patterning that makes these animals virtually invisible to their prey at close ranges. The visual complexity that appears spectacularly artificial to a human viewing a photograph at scale is precisely the complexity that creates effective mimicry of coral, rubble, or sandy substrate when viewed through the spatial frequency filters of a teleost fish visual system in ambient underwater light.

Hanlon et al. (2009, *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*) developed a classification system for camouflage strategies in marine organisms — background matching, disruptive coloration, and masquerade — and demonstrated that high-complexity substrates like coral reefs favor disruptive coloration over background matching because the substrate itself is highly variable and no single coloration pattern can match all micro-habitats locally. Disruptive coloration using the same statistical properties of texture as the broad habitat category is therefore more robust than precise mimicry of a specific substrate patch, explaining why both the goosefish and the tasseled wobbegong exhibit highly complex rather than subtly matched appearance.

**Misidentification Propagation in Social Media — Mechanisms and Consequences**: The goosefish-for-wobbegong misidentification illustrates a pattern that recurs across science communication on social media: a visually compelling image attracts wide sharing with an accurate but incomplete description (both species are real), and the image-description pairing then propagates as a unit, with corrections that appear in the comment threads rarely propagating far enough to reach all downstream sharers.

Quantitative analysis by Vosoughi, Roy & Aral (2018, *Science*) found that true news items propagated through shorter, shallower chains on Twitter than false news items, reaching smaller total audiences before dying out. This structural asymmetry means that corrections — which are typically more accurate but less emotionally engaging than the original misidentified post — tend to propagate less far, leaving the original misidentified version as the dominant version in the information environment even after the error is documented.

The specific case of species misidentification in marine biology is exacerbated by the fact that expertise in marine taxonomy is concentrated among a relatively small number of specialists whose visibility on general social media platforms is limited relative to enthusiastic amateur naturalists, science communication accounts, and viral content aggregators. The distribution of expert attention — focused on specialized journals and platforms — does not align with the distribution of where misidentifications cause public confusion, creating a persistent gap between accurate expert knowledge and publicly circulating identification claims.

**The Role of Reverse Image Search in Verification**: Snopes' reference to reverse image search as a verification tool in this case is notable. Reverse image search — using tools like Google Images or TinEye — can identify prior instances of an image's circulation and sometimes link to authenticated original sources or to fact-checks and corrections. In this case, Snopes used TinEye to document that the image had been circulating with various captions since at least 2021, and found that multiple social media users had independently noted the species misidentification.

This approach to provenance tracing represents an important technique in visual fact-checking that complements expert consultation. While expert confirmation is ultimately needed for definitive species identification, reverse image search can establish the history of a claim's circulation and identify whether corrections or competing identifications have already been offered, helping fact-checkers avoid redundant work and providing evidence about how long and through which channels a misidentification has propagated.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes assigned this claim a rating of **Mixture**, published July 11, 2022, authored by Madison Dapcevich. The analysis correctly identified the two-part structure of the claim — the photograph is authentic, the species identification is wrong — and provided accurate descriptions of both the goosefish (what the photograph actually shows) and the tasseled wobbegong (the species described in the caption).

The analysis relied on NOAA Fisheries data for the goosefish identification and on the Oceana species profile for the tasseled wobbegong. Snopes noted the results of reverse image search showing prior circulation of the image with various captions, and cited social media users who had previously noted the misidentification.

The treatment is accurate and appropriately structured for a Mixture verdict. The two-component structure of the Snopes rating system — What's True / What's False — is particularly well-suited to this case, where different components of the claim require different verdicts. The geographic distinction (tasseled wobbegong is found in Australian waters; goosefish is found in the north and mid-Atlantic) is the key factual axis that clearly distinguishes the two animals, and Snopes correctly highlights it.

The analysis is limited in not exploring the convergent evolution mechanism that explains why the misidentification is visually plausible, not engaging with the ecology of either species in depth, and not discussing the broader pattern of image misidentification propagation in social media or the epistemology of photographic species identification.

The EARC mode for this entry is **A** (Ambiguous / Mixture), reflecting the Snopes Mixture verdict — one component true, one false. The gap category is **identity-confusion**: the claim requires disentangling a real image from an incorrect identification, and the scientific richness lies in understanding why the visual confusion arose (convergent evolution), what each species is actually like (biology of goosefish and tasseled wobbegong independently), and what this case reveals about the limits of visual identification and the dynamics of species misidentification propagation on social media platforms.
