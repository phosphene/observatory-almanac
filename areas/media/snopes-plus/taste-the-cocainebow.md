## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

The claim in question holds that the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) conducted inspections of Skittles candy manufacturing facilities and discovered that multiple factories had been secretly incorporating cocaine into their products. The rumor circulated primarily through Facebook shares following the May 2015 Huzlers article, which was written in a mock news style closely mimicking legitimate journalism. The headline, "Multiple Skittles Factories Busted After It Was Discovered They Have Been Using Cocaine to Make Their Skittles," promised a bombshell revelation of widespread federal law-enforcement action against one of the most recognizable candy brands in the United States.

Snopes investigator David Mikkelson (2015) assigned this claim the rating **Labeled Satire** — a category the platform uses when source material explicitly identifies itself as satirical or fictional, but whose presentation leads large numbers of readers to treat it as factual reporting. The verdict is unambiguous: there is no truth to the FDA cocaine-in-Skittles story, not at the time of publication, not at any subsequent point, and not from any independent corroboration. No FDA press release, no criminal prosecution, no recall notice, and no independent laboratory analysis has ever supported the claim.

The manufactured article is a textbook specimen of the "product contamination hoax" genre — a recurring species of viral falsehood that Huzlers employed repeatedly against beverage and food brands throughout the mid-2010s. The site published substantively identical fabrications involving cocaine found in Coors Light beer and semen in Starbucks drinks, and urine in AriZona Iced Tea — all false, all sharing the same formal features, and all generating real-world alarm, media inquiries, and brand-reputation damage before being identified as satirical fiction.

The epistemic verdict is therefore simple: the claim is entirely without factual foundation, the source is a known fabricator, and the regulatory body named in the story (the FDA) had no involvement. What makes the entry worth extended analysis is not the factual question — which is closed — but the social, psychological, and infrastructural conditions that allowed thousands of people to share the story as credible.

## §2 Origin, Propagation, and Source Assay

The article was published on Huzlers.com on or around May 18, 2015. Huzlers had been operating as a satirical website since at least 2013, positioning itself explicitly — in its own site disclaimer — as "the most notorious urban satirical website with the most shocking headlines and articles." The disclaimer was present on the website but was either not noticed or actively disregarded by the vast majority of readers who encountered the article in its shared form on social media, stripped of the disclaimer's contextualizing language.

Huzlers' product-contamination subgenre reliably exploited a particular affective circuit: the combination of brand recognition, physical intimacy (the article concerns something people physically ingest), and implicit third-party authority (the invocation of the FDA as the discovering body). The article did not pretend that a random blogger had made a discovery; it claimed that federal regulators had caught a major consumer-goods company. This framing borrowed credibility from the FDA's genuine status as a powerful, trusted, and well-known agency.

The propagation mechanism was straightforwardly the Facebook news feed algorithm of 2015, which at that time gave significant weight to "high engagement" content without content-type discrimination. The article was formatted to look identical to a legitimate news story; its URL (huzlers.com/multiple-skittles-factories-busted/) read like a plausible wire service or local news address without domain-level scrutiny. Users whose network of friends included even one share were exposed to it as an undifferentiated item in their news feed alongside actual news.

The social dynamics of sharing reinforced propagation. Studies of social media misinformation propagation — notably Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral (2018) in *Science*, who analyzed 126,000 rumor cascades on Twitter — found that false stories diffuse faster and broader than true ones, partly because novelty detection drives human attention and engagement. A story about cocaine in Skittles is maximally novel; a story saying Skittles are normal candy is not. The cognitive work required to dismiss it is greater than the cognitive work required to share it.

## §3 Epistemic Analysis — The Knowledge Gap

**Epistemic novelty:** The Skittles-cocaine story illuminates a specific and underappreciated gap in public understanding of what the FDA actually does and what kinds of product-contamination events are structurally possible. Most Americans have only a vague model of how FDA inspections work, which makes the scenario imaginable: they can conceive of inspectors visiting factories, finding something wrong, issuing a report. The fictional story fills that abstract template with sensational content, and because the template itself is borrowed from real FDA enforcement activity, the story acquires a false plausibility.

The genuine FDA regulatory framework for food safety operates under the Food Safety Modernization Act of 2011 (FSMA), which shifted the FDA's approach from reactive to preventive. Under the FSMA framework, the FDA does conduct facility inspections, issue warning letters, and initiate recalls — but these activities and their outcomes are documented in publicly accessible federal registers and FDA databases. The absence of any such record for the Skittles cocaine story is itself dispositive: it is not merely that the story "seems unlikely"; it is that the mechanism by which it would become true involves federal record-keeping that does not exist.

This represents a structural epistemic gap: citizens who believed the story lacked not just the factual conclusion (no cocaine in Skittles) but the procedural knowledge necessary to evaluate the claim. Had they known that FDA enforcement actions are publicly documented and searchable, they would have had a simple verification pathway. The gap is not one of intelligence or education in any straightforward sense; it is a gap in *regulatory procedural literacy* — knowledge of how government agencies act and leave paper trails.

There is a secondary gap in media-source literacy: the capacity to identify a URL, check a domain's reputation, locate a site's about/disclaimer page, and treat the presence or absence of an editor's byline, date of correction policy, and publishing history as quality signals. Research by Pennycook and Rand (2019) published in *Psychological Science* found that brief analytical-reflection prompts significantly reduced participants' tendency to rate false headlines as accurate, suggesting that the issue is less one of irreversible cognitive bias than of attentional defaults.

The EARC classification is **E (Empirical)**: this claim is factually false and verifiably so. The knowledge gap is primarily an infrastructural-literacy gap, not a genuine scientific uncertainty.

## §4 Scientific and Empirical Context

The chemistry and pharmacology underlying the claim deserve brief examination, both because they clarify why the scenario is implausible at a physical level and because the actual pharmacology of cocaine is worth public understanding independent of this rumor.

Cocaine hydrochloride (the form present in illicit powder cocaine) is sensitive to heat, oxidation, and pH changes. Hartung et al. (2009), writing in the *Journal of Analytical Toxicology*, examined degradation of cocaine under various thermal conditions and found significant decomposition above 90°C — conditions routinely exceeded in commercial candy manufacturing, which typically involves temperatures of 110–150°C during gelatinization, mixing, and coating phases. Even if cocaine were somehow introduced at the factory level, a substantial fraction would be chemically degraded by the manufacturing process itself before reaching consumers.

Secondly, the economic and logistical absurdity of the claim merits acknowledgment. At 2015 prices, the quantity of cocaine required to lace even a modest production run of Skittles at any pharmacologically meaningful dose would represent an extraordinary manufacturing cost and would require a supply chain that would be extraordinarily difficult to conceal. The Mars/Wrigley manufacturing facilities that produce Skittles in the United States are large, heavily regulated operations subject to ongoing FDA oversight, including inspections that assess ingredient sourcing, manufacturing processes, and finished-product composition.

The FDA's actual analytical chemistry capabilities are relevant here. The FDA's Center for Food Safety and Applied Nutrition (CFSAN) operates laboratories that can detect trace contaminants at parts-per-billion concentrations using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). If cocaine were present in Skittles at pharmacologically relevant levels, it would be detectable. And because detection would constitute a major regulatory and criminal event — triggering mandatory recall, public health notification, and federal prosecution — it would be impossible to suppress from public record.

The broader context of food-safety contamination findings is also instructive. Real FDA contamination findings are publicized through a well-established protocol: the FDA issues a press release, updates its recall database, identifies affected lot numbers, and coordinates with retailers on removal from shelves. None of these events occurred in association with Skittles. The absence of evidence here is genuinely strong evidence of absence, precisely because the evidentiary infrastructure is so thoroughly documented.

## §5 Expert Responses and Regulatory Context

Wrigley (now Mars Wrigley Confectionery), the manufacturer of Skittles, did not issue a formal response to the Huzlers story, presumably because the fabricated nature of the piece was clear enough that corporate engagement would be counterproductive — amplifying the story rather than neutralizing it. This non-response itself became a subsequent vector for confusion: some commenters on social media in 2015 interpreted the absence of a corporate denial as suggestive of culpability, applying a version of the "no denial = implicit confirmation" cognitive error.

The FDA similarly did not issue any statement regarding cocaine in Skittles, for the obvious reason that no such investigation occurred. The silence of a federal regulator on a satirical fabrication is informationally correct but rhetorically vulnerable: in an environment of diffuse institutional distrust, silence from the agency named as the discovering body reads, to some audiences, as cover-up behavior.

Media coverage of the story was dominated by fact-checking outlets — Snopes, FactCheck.org, and subsequent entrants in the fact-checking space — rather than by mainstream news organizations. This is itself characteristic of the mid-2010s hoax news ecosystem, in which legitimate journalism had limited appetite for "debunking" stories that directly acknowledging would have amplified. The asymmetry between the speed of viral propagation and the reach of subsequent debunking was noted by Benkler, Faris, and Roberts (2018) in *Network Propaganda* (Oxford University Press), who analyzed misinformation asymmetry as a constitutive feature of the post-2012 social media information environment.

## §6 Broader Significance and Lessons

The Skittles-cocaine story, taken as an isolated incident of 2015-era fake news, might appear merely quirky — a piece of frivolous satire that briefly amused some audiences and alarmed others. Its significance within the Truth Vault project lies in its exemplary status as a specimen of a durable and recurring genre.

The product-contamination hoax is one of the oldest forms of folk legend and urban rumor studied in folklore scholarship. Jan Harold Brunvand, in his landmark collection *The Vanishing Hitchhiker* (1981, W.W. Norton), documented an earlier generation of contamination legends involving soft drink cans, fast food products, and candy bars, demonstrating that anxieties about industrially produced food with its invisible supply chains and corporate opacity have generated contamination fears across at least the twentieth century. Huzlers and its successors did not invent this genre; they digitized and accelerated it.

What changed in the social media era is the feedback loop between creation, propagation, and brand damage. A 1980s contamination rumor spread through word of mouth at the pace of human social networks; a 2015 contamination story achieves the same diffusion in hours. The debunking cycle, even when rapid by historical standards, consistently lags behind initial propagation because the information architecture of social platforms was not designed to route corrections alongside shares.

The epistemic lesson is compact but consequential: when evaluating a claim about a specific federal regulatory action — a recall, a ban, an investigation, a prosecution — the first verification step is to check the relevant regulatory database directly. FDA recalls are at fda.gov/safety/recalls-market-withdrawals-safety-alerts. No entry in that database requires a second opinion. The absence of a record there for the Skittles cocaine claim is cleanly and publicly accessible. Fact-checking confidence is possible without expert chemistry knowledge or legal training; it requires only the knowledge that the database exists and how to read it.

That regulatory-procedural literacy is itself a learnable, teachable, transferable skill — applicable not just to confection-contamination hoaxes but to the entire domain of claims about what government agencies have or have not done. This is the substantive addition the Truth Vault can make: not just cataloguing false claims but teaching the verification muscle that makes future claims easier to assess.
