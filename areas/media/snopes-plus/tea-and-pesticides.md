## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

A blog post published by Carly Fraser on the website "Live Love Fruit" on October 26, 2016, and shared more than one million times on Facebook, claimed that testing had revealed eight popular tea brands to contain "illegal amounts of deadly pesticides." The post drew on three separate sources of testing data—a 2014 investigation by the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation (CBC), a 2014 Greenpeace report on Indian tea samples, and a 2013 report by a short-selling investment research group called Glaucus Research Group—and concluded that consumers should avoid the named brands and instead purchase the allegedly safer alternatives linked within the post.

Snopes investigator Alex Kasprak (2018) rated this claim **Outdated**, a designation that captures both the temporal staleness of the underlying evidence and the persistent recirculation of the claim in unchanged or minimally modified form years after its publication. Kasprak's analysis demonstrated that the blog post contained multiple factual errors and that it exploited a systematic ambiguity in how pesticide residue test results are presented to lay audiences: the difference between chemicals found at concentrations that exceed a scientifically validated Maximum Residue Limit (MRL) and chemicals found at any detectable concentration in a food product for which no MRL has been established. Critically, in the three sets of testing data cited, no chemical with an established MRL for dried tea was found at concentrations even remotely close to exceeding those limits. The claim of "illegal amounts" was substantially built on conflating chemicals with no established tea-specific MRL—where any detectable presence above 0.01 parts per million (ppm) constitutes a technical violation—with chemicals whose detected concentrations exceeded the scientifically determined safe upper limit for human exposure.

Additionally, Fraser's post was shown to be structured around affiliate marketing links directing readers to Amazon purchases of alternative tea products from which Fraser received commission, adding a financial motivation to the apparent public health alarming.

## §2 The Three Datasets and Their Misrepresentation

Kasprak (2018) identifies three distinct bodies of testing data invoked by Fraser, each of which was mischaracterized in the blog post.

**The CBC Testing (2014):** The Canadian Broadcasting Corporation published a food safety investigation in 2014 testing multiple popular tea brands for pesticide residues using methods employed by Canada's National Food Inspection Agency. Fraser described the results as showing that "over half of all teas tested had pesticide residues that were above the legally acceptable limit." This statement, Kasprak (2018) demonstrates, was false. In every case where a chemical identified by the CBC testing had an established MRL for tea in either the United States or Canada, the detected concentration was far below that limit. For illustrative examples: bifenthrin, one of the most widely detected chemicals in the CBC results, has an established MRL of 30 ppm in both the US and Canada; the highest concentration found by the CBC was 0.69 ppm—approximately 1/43 of the MRL. Acetamiprid, another detected chemical, has a US MRL of 50 ppm; the CBC found a maximum of 1.00 ppm.

The chemicals for which the results were technically violations were violations of a different character: they were chemicals for which no tea-specific MRL had been established, meaning that the mere presence of the chemical above the default 0.01 ppm threshold constituted a technical violation under both US and Canadian law. Chris Sack, a pesticide residue expert at the FDA, explained to Kasprak (2018) the important regulatory distinction: "If a product contains a pesticide residue for which EPA has not set a tolerance, at any level, it is adulterated. We actually classify the residue that way in our data. The regulatory action is a separate thing. Think of it like the speed limit. If I am traveling at 1 mph over the limit, I am in violation of the limit. However, the authorities do not normally take action for that small of an infraction." The 0.01 ppm threshold, in this framework, is not a medically determined health limit; it is essentially the limit of analytical detection, below which measurement uncertainty makes regulatory action unjustifiable.

Fraser further claimed that "a large majority of these pesticides are currently being banned in several countries." Kasprak (2018) found this also to be false. Of all chemicals identified by the CBC, only endosulfan appeared on the Rotterdam Convention's list of pesticides banned or severely restricted for health or environmental reasons. And endosulfan's detected level in the tea samples—0.04 ppm—was four orders of magnitude below the US MRL of 24 ppm at the time.

**The Greenpeace Report (2014):** Greenpeace's 2014 investigation tested tea samples sourced from India and found that approximately 94 percent of samples contained residues of at least one of 34 pesticide active ingredients "at concentrations above the analytical limit of quantification." Fraser presented this as evidence of dangerous contamination levels. Kasprak (2018) pointed out that the Greenpeace statement merely confirmed that the laboratory could confidently detect the presence of certain chemicals—that is, the detected amounts were above the measurement noise floor—not that 94 percent of samples contained chemicals at concentrations violating established health limits. The most commonly detected pesticides in the Greenpeace data were thiamethoxam (US MRL: 20 ppm; Greenpeace concentration range: 0.4–0.34 ppm) and acetamiprid (US MRL: 50 ppm; Greenpeace concentration range: 0.01–0.32 ppm). The more legitimately concerning finding in the Greenpeace data—DDT detection in some samples—would, if found in US market imports, trigger regulatory action under FDA monitoring programs that test precisely for this substance.

**The Glaucus Research Report (2013):** The Glaucus Research Group, as Kasprak (2018) documented, is a short-selling investment operation. Their 2013 report focused specifically on Hain-Celestial products, including Celestial Seasonings teas, because Glaucus was attempting to profit by publicly driving down Hain-Celestial's stock price. Fraser claimed that 91 percent of Celestial Seasonings teas tested had "pesticide residues exceeding the U.S. limits." The actual finding was that 91 percent of samples contained detectable levels of at least one pesticide-related chemical—again conflating "detectable presence of a chemical for which no tea MRL exists" with "concentration exceeding a scientifically validated health limit." Hain-Celestial subsequently had the same products retested by the National Food Lab, which detected no pesticides. Glaucus's investment thesis was at least partially validated: Hain-Celestial's stock value temporarily declined as a result of the controversy.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The MRL Ambiguity as a Structural Exploit

The epistemic novelty in this case is the systematic exploitation of a genuine regulatory complexity—the two-tier structure of pesticide residue violations—as a rhetorical device for manufacturing apparent alarm. This exploit requires understanding how MRL frameworks are built and why they have gaps.

MRLs for pesticide residues in specific food commodities are not set by fiat. They require extensive toxicological data on the pesticide in question, detailed dietary exposure modeling using consumption data for the specific food commodity, and in many cases lengthy regulatory review processes. The EPA and its international equivalents set MRLs for pesticide-commodity combinations when both the pesticide is in common agricultural use and the commodity has sufficient production volume to warrant the expenditure of regulatory resources on a dedicated MRL calculation. For many combinations—particularly agricultural chemicals used in countries that export to the US but not domestically produced, applied to specialty commodities—no MRL has been established for the practical-economics reason that limited use data are available.

Tea presents a particularly MRL-sparse landscape because the majority of global tea production occurs in countries (China, India, Kenya, Sri Lanka) that use different agricultural chemical regimes than the US, and because the complexity of hundreds of individual tea cultivars and processing methods creates a difficult regulatory target. Corrigan et al. (2000, *Food Additives and Contaminants*, Vol. 17, No. 1) analyzed the regulatory challenges of setting pesticide MRLs for imported tea and noted that the brewing process itself substantially reduces pesticide concentrations in the final beverage consumed. The chemical loss that occurs during hot-water extraction—particularly for hydrophobic compounds that partition into the tea leaves rather than infusing into the water—means that MRLs established for dried leaf may not accurately represent consumer exposure from brewed tea, typically yielding substantially lower concentrations in the cup.

The European Union, Japan, and Australia have adopted a uniform default MRL of 0.01 ppm for any pesticide-commodity combination for which no specific MRL has been established. This harmonized standard explicitly functions as a zero-tolerance proxy: in the absence of data establishing a safe threshold, any detectable presence is treated as a violation. The FDA's approach, as Sack articulated to Kasprak (2018), is functionally similar but explicitly acknowledges that regulatory action does not automatically follow from technical adulteration. Both approaches create the same rhetorical exploit opportunity: statements accurately describing technical violations can be framed as if they describe health-limit violations, conflating a recordkeeping-class regulatory status with a safety-critical status.

Understanding why MRL gaps persist illuminates why this rhetorical strategy is effective. Pesticide registration data are time-consuming and expensive to generate; MRL petitions are typically funded by the pesticide manufacturers themselves, creating a market-incentive gap that leaves agricultural commodities produced outside the US particularly underprotected by MRL frameworks. Pesticide monitoring scholars including Keikotlhaile and Spanoghe (2011, *Communications in Agricultural and Applied Biological Sciences*, Vol. 76, No. 2) have documented that this structural gap disproportionately affects consumers of imported commodities while creating the appearance of stringent regulatory protection.

The affiliate marketing dimension adds a layer of epistemic corruption. Carly Fraser's "Live Love Fruit" post was constructed not merely as a factual account of pesticide testing results but as a commercial referral channel designed to shift consumer purchasing toward products from which Fraser derived economic benefit. The post fulfilled its commercial objective precisely to the extent that it created consumer alarm sufficient to motivate behavioral change—in this case, changing tea brands—without requiring that alarm to be grounded in accurate information.

## §4 A Science of Tea Pesticide Exposure: What the Research Actually Shows

Setting aside the specific claims of the viral blog post, the general question of pesticide residues in tea as a health concern merits rigorous examination. Tea is one of the most widely consumed beverages globally, with estimated daily consumption of approximately 6.3 billion cups (Cabrera, Artacho, and Giménez, 2006, *Journal of the American College of Nutrition*, Vol. 25, No. 2). The cumulative pesticide exposure from tea consumption is therefore a legitimate subject of dietary risk assessment, even if the specific claims of the Live Love Fruit post were misleading.

Research examining actual brewed tea exposure levels consistently finds that they are substantially lower than dried-leaf concentrations. Łozowicka, Jankowska, and Kaczyński (2012, *Food Chemistry*, Vol. 130, No. 4) conducted infusion studies on dried tea samples contaminated with various pesticides and found that transfer rates from dry leaf to brewed liquid ranged from approximately 1 percent to 50 percent depending on the pesticide's physicochemical properties, particularly its water solubility and octanol-water partition coefficient (log Kow). Highly lipophilic pesticides—which include many of the organochlorine compounds like DDT and endosulfan—transfer very poorly into brewed tea because they partition preferentially into the lipid-containing leaf material. More water-soluble neonicotinoid insecticides like imidacloprid and acetamiprid transfer more completely but were also found at starting concentrations in dried leaf well below established MRLs in the studies examined.

Risk assessment calculations reported by Dong, Xu, and Wu (2014, *Food Chemistry*, Vol. 155) using Chinese tea samples found that estimated daily intakes of pesticide residues from tea consumption were several orders of magnitude below acceptable daily intake (ADI) values established by WHO/FAO committees for the pesticides detected, even for heavy tea drinkers consuming six to eight cups daily. This finding is consistent with a general principle in food safety toxicology: for chronically applied agricultural chemicals with established ADI values, the margin of exposure between typical dietary intake and the ADI is characteristically large, and tea typically contributes only a small fraction of total dietary exposure for any given compound.

The legitimate concern in global tea pesticide monitoring is not, therefore, that current residue levels pose acute health dangers to consumers. It is rather that the monitoring and MRL-setting infrastructure for internationally traded tea is less robust than is appropriate for a commodity consumed in such quantities, that certain producing regions have incentive structures that encourage pesticide overuse, and that long-term low-level exposure to mixtures of agricultural chemicals—a concern that single-compound MRL frameworks do not fully address—remains an active area of research (Hernández et al., 2013, *Critical Reviews in Toxicology*, Vol. 43, Suppl. 2).

## §5 Affiliate Marketing and Scientific Credibility: The Conflict of Interest Problem in Consumer Science Communication

The Live Love Fruit case exemplifies a broader problem in consumer-oriented science communication: when scientific evidence—even accurately described scientific evidence—is deployed within a commercial framework designed to shift purchasing behavior, the credibility infrastructure of science is exploited for commercial ends. This phenomenon is distinct from outright fraud; in Fraser's case, after Kasprak contacted her, she updated her post with additional detail while acknowledging it received affiliate commissions. But the updates did not fundamentally correct the misrepresentation of the testing data.

The conflict of interest in consumer health content is particularly insidious because it aligns financial incentives with the creation of health anxiety rather than health reassurance. A post that accurately described the testing data and concluded that tea pesticide residue levels are generally well below established health limits would not drive readers to purchase alternative products. A post that mischaracterizes the same data as establishing "illegal amounts of deadly pesticides" provides the alarm-to-action pathway that affiliate link conversions require.

Research on commercial influence in consumer health content online has documented that financial conflicts of interest are frequently undisclosed and systematically distort the conclusions of health-related content toward worse outcomes than warranted by evidence. Ventola (2014, *Pharmacy and Therapeutics*, Vol. 39, No. 7) and others studying health information on social media have noted the particular difficulty consumers face in evaluating the credibility of health claims in content that combines superficially scientific language with commercial recommendation structures. Fraser's post represented an expert-seeming technical discussion—citing specific parts per million values, naming specific scientific studies, deploying regulatory terminology—while fundamentally misrepresenting the conclusions warranted by that data.

## §6 Conclusion: Context as the Critical Epistemic Variable

The tea pesticide case constitutes a case study in context as the critical epistemic variable. The raw data cited by Fraser—positive pesticide tests in commercial tea brands—were not fabricated. The CBC did test tea and found pesticide residues. Greenpeace did test Indian tea samples and found high rates of detectable residues. Glaucus Research did publish findings on Celestial Seasonings teas. What the post systematically stripped away from these findings was the context that determines whether those findings are alarming or unremarkable: the comparison between detected concentrations and established health-based limits.

Kasprak's (2018) outdated rating is accurate in two senses. First, the data themselves are old—2013 and 2014 testing used as the basis for 2016 and later consumer guidance—and do not reflect the current state of the tea supply chain, the regulatory framework, or any of the companies' current practices. Second, the interpretation of that data was outdated at the moment of publication: the claim that "illegal amounts" of pesticides were present was not a plausible reading of the data at any point, because the technical sense in which the violations were "illegal" was fundamentally different from the public health sense an audience would naturally attribute to that term.

The persistence of the original post's claims—over a million Facebook shares, continued recirculation years after publication—illustrates the asymmetric viral dynamics of alarming versus reassuring food safety content. Corrections rarely achieve the scale of initial false alarms, and the affiliate marketing structure of the original post ensures that it continues to serve its commercial function as long as it remains accessible online. The epistemic corrective in this case is not technical—the chemistry of MRL frameworks is accessible to any interested reader—but requires the kind of contextual translation that Kasprak's analysis provided: not "what chemicals were found?" but "what do those findings actually mean for health risk?"
