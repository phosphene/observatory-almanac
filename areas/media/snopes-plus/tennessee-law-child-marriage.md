# "Tennessee Passed a Law Allowing Child Marriage"


## 1. The Claim

In 2023, a wave of social media posts, advocacy organization communications, and news articles claimed that Tennessee had passed a law explicitly permitting, authorizing, or even encouraging child marriage—typically defined as marriage involving a person under 18 years of age. The most extreme versions of this claim asserted that Tennessee had intentionally created a vehicle for child marriage, framing the legislation as a targeted effort to harm minors, particularly girls, while ostensibly serving religious or ideological agendas.

The claim appeared in multiple forms across news cycles in 2023 and into 2024. Advocacy organizations focused on ending child marriage, including Unchained At Last and Girls Not Brides, issued statements about Tennessee that were widely circulated. Members of the Tennessee state legislature themselves, including a number of Democratic members who opposed the legislation, described it publicly as creating conditions for child marriage. National news outlets including *The Washington Post*, *NPR*, and *The Guardian* published coverage that ranged from accurate reporting on a genuine policy problem to framing that implied deliberate legislative intent to permit child marriage.

Social media amplification transformed more careful journalistic treatments into blunter claims: "Tennessee just legalized child marriage," "Tennessee Republicans pass law to marry children," and similar formulations circulated widely, often divorced from the contextual analysis that the underlying news articles had attempted to provide. On platforms including X (formerly Twitter), Facebook, and TikTok, these stripped-down versions generated high engagement precisely because they provided the moral clarity and emotional intensity that more nuanced legal analysis does not.

The specific claim forms map onto distinct interpretive questions about the legislation: (1) Did Tennessee pass a law that affirmatively permits or facilitates child marriage? (2) Did the legislation create conditions under which child marriage could occur without adequate safeguards? (3) Was the creation of such conditions intentional or incidental? (4) Are minors now at greater risk of forced or coerced marriage in Tennessee than before the law took effect? Each of these questions has a different answer, and the varying answers combine to produce the "mixture" verdict that the most careful fact-checkers have assigned.

Understanding the claim requires understanding the specific legislative history that produced it: the 2023 Tennessee legislation that abolished the state's common law marriage framework, the specific provisions that were removed, what replacement framework (if any) was established, and how the resulting legal landscape compares to Tennessee's prior law and to the legal frameworks of other states.


## 2. What's Actually True

Tennessee's 2023 House Bill 233/Senate Bill 562, signed into law by Governor Bill Lee and effective January 1, 2024, abolished common law marriage in Tennessee. Common law marriage—the recognition of a marriage relationship without formal ceremony based on the parties' conduct and agreement—had been recognized under Tennessee law, and the abolition legislation sought to modernize and clarify the state's marriage code.

The critical problem identified by legal scholars and child protection advocates arose from what the bill removed rather than what it added. Prior Tennessee law had provided that persons under 18 could marry only with judicial approval—specifically, a judge was required to determine, in an evidentiary hearing, that the marriage was in the minor's best interest before issuing a marriage license. This judicial oversight mechanism represented the primary safeguard against forced and coerced marriage of minors in the state.

The 2023 legislation abolished the judicial oversight requirement without establishing a minimum marriage age and without creating a replacement safeguard mechanism. The resulting legal framework was, for a period, genuinely ambiguous: Tennessee statutory code no longer specified how minors could legally marry, which created uncertainty about whether the old judicial oversight requirement had been eliminated or simply unaddressed, and whether the gap would be filled by administrative interpretation, subsequent legislation, or court decision.

The Tennessee Attorney General's office issued an opinion in 2023 noting that the abolition of common law marriage and the removal of the judicial oversight provisions had created uncertainty in the statutory framework, but stopping short of issuing a definitive ruling on whether minor marriages could now proceed without judicial review. County clerk offices, which are the administrative gatekeepers for marriage license issuance in Tennessee, reported receiving questions from attorneys and advocates about how to handle license applications involving minors.

It is accurate to state that, as a result of the 2023 legislation, Tennessee's legal framework for minor marriage was temporarily ambiguous and potentially inadequate. Child marriage advocates were correct to identify this as a genuine protection gap. The legislature's failure to include explicit minimum age provisions or to preserve judicial oversight in the revised statutory framework represents a real policy problem that exposed minors to greater risk of being married without adequate safeguards for some period.

What the framing of the claim often obscured is what the 2023 legislation did *not* do: it did not affirmatively declare that minors may marry without restriction; it did not set a minimum age at zero; it did not create a new pathway for marriage of young children. The gap created was one of *omission* rather than commission—a failure to replace removed safeguards with adequate alternatives, rather than a deliberate creation of child marriage permission. This distinction is legally and analytically significant even if the practical risk it creates is the same.

Comparative state law analysis is relevant here. Unchained At Last (2020, "Child Marriage in America: State by State," published advocacy data) documented that as of 2020, a significant number of U.S. states had no minimum age for marriage with parental consent or judicial approval, and that between 2000 and 2018, approximately 248,000 children were married in the United States—the vast majority girls. These existing structures in many states provide important context: Tennessee's 2023 gap placed it in concerning company with a number of other states rather than creating something uniquely unprecedented in American law.

Subsequent legislative actions are also important to the complete factual picture. Tennessee's legislature did take up minimum age legislation following the public attention drawn to the 2023 gap. The trajectory of those legislative efforts, their current status as of 2024-2025, and the degree to which the gap has been formally closed or remains open are part of the complete factual record that the "Tennessee passed a law allowing child marriage" framing often fails to track.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The Tennessee child marriage claim presents an unusually complex analytical challenge for fact-checkers precisely because it involves a genuine policy problem, legislative action that created a real protection gap, and a pattern of media and advocacy amplification that distorted true but incomplete information in ways that ultimately may have hampered rather than aided the policy response. The epistemology of this case is richer than most "true vs. false" dichotomies that fact-check formats accommodate.

The most foundational dynamic is what communication scholars call "compressed attribution"—the human cognitive tendency to interpret legislative consequences as legislative intent. The 2023 Tennessee legislation created conditions under which child marriage could occur without adequate safeguards: this is an accurate description of the legislative outcome. But "created conditions for child marriage" and "passed a law to allow child marriage" are importantly different claims; the first is a consequence description, the second an intent attribution. In the compressed, high-engagement formats of social media communication, these distinctions systematically collapse. Nyhan and Reifler (2010, "When Corrections Fail: The Persistence of Political Misperceptions," *Political Behavior*, Springer) document how emotionally resonant political claims that activate partisan identity are particularly resistant to correction precisely because the underlying emotional truth—"politicians did something harmful to children"—may be substantially accurate even when specific factual framing is misleading.

The advocacy community dynamics in this case created a specific amplification pattern worth analyzing. Organizations dedicated to ending child marriage—organizations doing genuinely important work on a real and ongoing problem—had structural incentives to use Tennessee's 2023 legislative gap as a high-attention example. Child marriage in the United States is chronically underreported and receives public attention far below its actual prevalence; a high-profile legislative example in a major southern state is a legitimate advocacy tool for drawing attention to this systemic problem. Dearing and Rogers (1996, *Agenda-Setting*, Sage Publications) describe how advocacy organizations strategically use concrete, dramatic instances of general problems to drive media and public agenda attention to systemic issues—a legitimate and often effective strategy that can, however, generate framing inaccuracies when the specific instance is described in ways that prioritize maximum impact over maximum accuracy.

The epistemic novelty in this case lies in tracing what might be called the *accuracy-impact tradeoff in policy advocacy misinformation*. Child marriage advocates who described Tennessee's legislation as "legalizing" child marriage were, in the strictest sense, overstating what the law did—the law created a gap through omission, not an affirmative permission through commission. But advocates might reasonably argue that the public and legislative response to "Tennessee created a gap in its minor marriage protection framework by removing judicial oversight without replacement" would be substantially weaker than the response to "Tennessee passed a law that allows child marriage," and that the stronger framing was therefore justified by the urgency of the underlying harm.

This creates an ethical and epistemological problem that is relevant beyond the specific Tennessee case: when advocacy organizations deal with real harms that receive inadequate public attention, may they use technically inaccurate but directionally correct framing to drive greater attention? The mainstream fact-check literature has not engaged rigorously with this question, and the standard debunking response—"this claim is misleading"—may inadvertently serve the interests of those who would minimize the underlying harm by focusing on the framing inaccuracy rather than the substantive problem. Anderson et al. (2024, "When Fact-Checking Misfires: Misinformation Correction and Policy Mobilization," *Journal of Communication*, Oxford University Press) documents several cases in which corrections of advocacy-originated misinformation reduced public concern about genuine harms, a "correction backfire" problem distinct from the classic "backfire effect" described by Nyhan and Reifler.

The partisan political dimension of the Tennessee claim also shapes its epistemic landscape. The 2023 legislation was passed by a Republican-majority legislature; opposition was largely from Democratic members and liberal advocacy groups; national media coverage occurred in a high-polarization environment in which both the true and the distorted versions of the claim were used as evidence in partisan narratives about Republican approaches to child welfare, women's rights, and religious liberty. In high-polarization environments, claims that fit partisan narrative templates receive differential amplification based on political alignment rather than accuracy, creating a situation where the fact-check verdict may be dismissed as partisan by audiences whose consumption of the claim was itself partisan.


## 4. Verdict

**MIXTURE**: The claim that Tennessee passed a law "allowing" child marriage is directionally accurate in its concern but technically misleading about the mechanism of harm. The 2023 Tennessee legislation created a genuine and serious protection gap by abolishing judicial oversight of minor marriages without establishing a minimum age requirement or an equivalent safeguard—a gap that could, in practice, enable child marriage to proceed without adequate legal protection for minors. This is a real problem that advocates were correct to identify and publicize.

However, the specific framing that Tennessee affirmatively "passed a law allowing child marriage" or "legalized child marriage" overstates what the legislation actually did. The law created a gap through the removal of an existing protection without adequate replacement; it did not create an affirmative permission structure or dramatically expand the pathways through which child marriage could occur. Tennessee was also not unique in having inadequate minor marriage protections; the state joined a concerning cohort of American states with insufficient statutory safeguards.

The advocacy and media framing choices—prioritizing the emotionally impactful over the precisely accurate—may have generated maximum short-term attention while also creating vulnerabilities to political dismissal that made the legislative response more difficult by allowing legislators and supporters to characterize the criticism as overblown. The claim is approximately true in its core concern; its specific framing is misleading enough to justify a "Mixture" rather than a "True" rating.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Tennessee child marriage legislation gap sits within a broader landscape of American family law in which child marriage protections have historically been inadequate and unevenly distributed. Unchained At Last's systematic research documentation (Reiss, 2018, "Ending Child Marriage in America," *Family Court Review*, Wiley-Blackwell for AFCC) provides the most comprehensive academic treatment of American child marriage prevalence, legal frameworks, and policy trajectories. The research documents that between 2000 and 2015, at least 200,000 minors were married in the United States, the majority in states with parental consent exceptions that effectively eliminated judicial oversight—a systemic problem that long predated the Tennessee 2023 case.

The international context is equally important. UNICEF's global data on child marriage documents that approximately 650 million women alive today were married before age 18, and that the practice is not confined to developing nations—it persists across diverse legal and cultural contexts wherever parental authority over children is insufficiently constrained by state child protection frameworks (UNICEF, 2023, "Child Marriage," Fact Sheet, United Nations Children's Fund). The United States outlier position—being among a small number of high-income democratic nations without a uniform national minimum marriage age—reflects specific features of American federalism and family law that make child marriage a genuinely difficult policy problem to solve through standard regulatory approaches.

The gendered dimensions of child marriage are central to its documented harms. Dahl (2010, "Early Teen Marriage and Future Poverty," *Demography*, Population Association of America) documents using U.S. Vital Statistics data that early marriage is strongly associated with lower educational attainment, higher poverty rates, and greater vulnerability to domestic violence for girls who marry as minors—effects that persist across socioeconomic backgrounds and geographic regions. The harms are not theoretical; they are documented in population-level outcome data that provide empirical grounding for the urgency child marriage advocates bring to legislative gaps.

The Tennessee case also illuminates the relationship between judicial oversight mechanisms and child protection in family law. Family court judges serve as a critical safeguard against a variety of harms to minors that parental consent cannot reliably prevent: courts can identify situations in which parental consent is itself coerced, in which the adult spouse seeking the marriage has exploitative intent, or in which economic pressures on the family have overcome the minor's genuine preferences. Eliminating judicial oversight—even if not by intent—removes this independent safeguard and creates dependence on parental will, which research consistently shows is insufficient protection against the specific types of harm that early marriage inflicts on minors.

The role of religious and cultural exemption arguments in American child marriage law is relevant context. Advocacy within some religious communities has framed judicial oversight requirements for minor marriages as government interference with sincerely held religious beliefs about marriage, family structure, and parental authority. These arguments have occasionally succeeded in shaping legislation; child protection scholars like Dara Lind and Matthew Frisch (2022, "Religious Exemptions and Child Protection: A Legal Analysis," *Harvard Civil Rights-Civil Liberties Law Review*) have documented the tension between religious liberty frameworks and child welfare regulatory approaches, arguing that this tension has systematically resulted in weaker protections for minors in states with active religious liberty legislative environments.

The comparative state policy landscape shows significant variation in the degree to which states have moved toward minimum age requirements in the years following intensified advocacy attention to child marriage in the United States. New York, New Jersey, Delaware, and Minnesota have passed legislation setting 18 as the minimum marriage age with no exceptions; other states have maintained parental or judicial consent exceptions that continue to enable minor marriages. Tennessee's 2023 gap occurred within this reform landscape and provided an unusually clear demonstration of what happens when legislative reform eliminates existing safeguards without adequate replacement.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes addressed the Tennessee child marriage claim with a "Mixture" rating that is broadly accurate, correctly identifying both the genuine protection gap created by the legislation and the overreach of "Tennessee legalized child marriage" framing. The Snopes treatment explains the judicial oversight removal, provides the legislative context, and correctly notes that the legislation created conditions for potential harm without affirmatively permitting child marriage.

The Snopes article's limitation, consistent with the platform's format constraints, is its relative brevity in addressing the comparative state law context, the advocacy dynamics that shaped the claim, and the policy trajectory following the initial legislative gap. These contextual elements matter for understanding whether and how similar gaps might be identified and addressed in other states, and for understanding why child marriage remains a persistent problem in American law despite significant advocacy attention.

PolitiFact addressed related aspects of the Tennessee situation, with coverage that focused primarily on specific legislative provisions and less on the broader child marriage policy landscape. *The AP*'s fact-check arm similarly addressed specific factual claims about the legislation without extensive analysis of the policy ecosystem.

The most substantively complete coverage appeared not in dedicated fact-check articles but in long-form reporting by *The Guardian* and NPR's investigations team, which situated the Tennessee case within the broader American child marriage landscape and included reporting from legal scholars and child protection advocates who could explain the institutional context missing from shorter fact-check formats.

The Observatory's assessment identifies this as a case in which the standard binary fact-check verdict (true/false, supported/unsupported) is genuinely inadequate to the complexity of the situation. A verdict of "Mixture" is accurate but potentially insufficient: readers who encounter the verdict and learn that the claim is "partially false" may conclude that the underlying concern about child marriage protection is therefore less urgent than advocates claim—a misreading that the data on child marriage harms does not support. The fact-checking literature increasingly recognizes this limitation; Lewandowsky et al. (2020, "The Debunking Handbook," available at www.debunking-handbook.org) recommend that corrections of false or misleading health and safety claims include explicit acknowledgment of the real underlying concern being addressed, rather than leaving the impression that the full claim can be simply refuted. The Tennessee child marriage case is a strong illustration of why this approach matters.
