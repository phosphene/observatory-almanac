# Study Claimed Tesla Had Highest Fatality Rate of Any Car Brand – But Is It True?


## 1. The Claim

Beginning in November 2024 and continuing into early 2025, a claim circulated intensively across Reddit, TikTok, Instagram, X (formerly Twitter), and Bluesky stating that a study had found Tesla to have the highest fatal accident rate of any car brand. The claim was based on a published analysis by iSeeCars, a car search-engine website, which reported a finding of 5.6 fatal crashes per billion vehicle miles for Tesla vehicles — the highest rate across all car brands examined. The iSeeCars study analysed data from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration's Fatality Analysis Reporting System (FARS), looking at model years 2018 to 2022 in crashes resulting in at least one occupant fatality.

The claim was picked up and reported by a range of outlets including *Rolling Stone*, *MotorTrend*, and *CarPro News*. On Reddit, a post titled "Tesla Has the Highest Fatal Accident Rate of All Car Brands, Study Finds" generated substantial engagement with many commenters debating whether the finding reflected vehicle design flaws, driver behaviour, or both. The iSeeCars report itself acknowledged this ambiguity, noting that "it's possible these high fatal accident rates reflect driver behavior as much or more than vehicle design."

The claim entered the media environment at a politically and culturally charged moment. Tesla and its CEO Elon Musk had become increasingly polarising figures in public discourse, and the fatality rate study was framed by many sharers as damning evidence against both the company and, implicitly, its leadership. Conversely, Tesla supporters framed media coverage of the study as politically motivated. The existence of a named study gave the claim a veneer of scientific authority that straightforward assertions about Tesla's safety record would not have possessed, making it more resistant to dismissal while its methodological foundations remained largely unexamined.


## 2. What's Actually True

**A study does exist and does make the claimed finding — with a critical caveat.** The iSeeCars study is a real document that was publicly available at the time of reporting. It used FARS data — a legitimate, government-maintained dataset that is publicly accessible through NHTSA — to identify fatal crashes involving vehicles from model years 2018 to 2022. On that raw measure, Tesla vehicles were represented in crash data at a rate that, after iSeeCars' normalization process, yielded a figure of 5.6 fatal crashes per billion vehicle miles — the highest among the brands examined.

However, the normalization step is where the study's verifiability collapses. To calculate "fatal crashes per billion vehicle miles," you need both a numerator (fatal crashes, from FARS data — publicly available) and a denominator (vehicle miles driven — not from any public dataset). FARS data does not include vehicle miles information. iSeeCars supplied this denominator from its own proprietary dataset of "more than 8 million vehicles on the road in 2022," estimating total vehicle miles driven for the makes and models in question from data it had collected internally. This data is not publicly available. iSeeCars did not respond to Snopes' multiple inquiries — sent in December 2024 and January 2025 — asking how the vehicle miles data was collected, how its accuracy was ensured, or whether the underlying data could be made available for independent review.

**The denominator problem is decisive.** In any comparative rate calculation, the choice of denominator shapes the result at least as much as the numerator. "Deaths per miles driven" is the correct normalisation metric for comparing vehicle safety across brands — raw crash counts favour brands with more vehicles on the road. But producing a valid "miles driven" denominator for a specific make/model combination is methodologically demanding. Variables that could affect the result include: how iSeeCars defined "on the road in 2022," how it handled vehicles that were registered but not regularly driven, how it treated vehicles with uncertain mileage histories, and what assumptions it made about typical annual mileage for Tesla vehicles relative to other brands.

Each of these choices can produce large differences in the output rate, and none of them can be evaluated by any external party because the data and methodology are proprietary. The study's conclusion may well be accurate; it also may contain systematic methodological errors that inflate Tesla's apparent rate. Without the underlying data, there is no way to distinguish between these possibilities.

**What independent, transparent analyses show.** The Insurance Institute for Highway Safety (IIHS), which is funded by automobile insurers and conducts rigorous independent crash analysis with published and transparent methodologies, presents a substantially different picture. In its most recent analysis of driver fatality rates — specifically for 2020 model year vehicles — the IIHS analysed three Tesla models. None ranked in the top 20 cars with the highest rate of driver deaths. Furthermore, the IIHS rated the Tesla Model Y as its top safety pick for 2024 — its highest safety designation. IIHS Director of Media Relations Joe Young confirmed to Snopes that the IIHS does not conduct overall fatality rate analyses by brand (only by model), and that IIHS conclusions are reached through the institute's own internal crash analyses using transparent methodologies.

The IIHS also noted that fatality rates for drivers of *other* vehicles in crashes involving the three Tesla models it analysed were all below average — meaning that when Teslas are involved in fatal crashes, the other-party casualty rate is lower than baseline, which is relevant information about vehicle safety that the iSeeCars study's single-metric framing obscures.

**Why Tesla vehicles might show confounding factors in fatality rate analyses.** There are plausible structural reasons why Tesla vehicles might appear to have elevated fatality rates in some analyses even if the vehicles themselves are not unusually dangerous. Tesla is predominantly a luxury/performance vehicle brand, and performance vehicles are driven at higher average speeds, more frequently on highways, and by drivers who may exhibit different behavioural risk profiles than drivers of comparable-segment non-performance vehicles. The iSeeCars study itself acknowledged that high fatal accident rates for Tesla "reflect driver behavior as much or more than vehicle design." If the vehicle miles denominator does not adequately control for how those miles were driven — highway versus city, night versus day, speed distribution — then a rate comparison that is technically valid on its own terms may be misleading as an indicator of vehicle safety.

Additionally, Tesla's Autopilot and Full Self-Driving technologies have been the subject of formal NHTSA investigations into multiple fatal crashes, and the visibility of those crashes in media coverage may create a feedback loop in which Tesla crash data is more thoroughly reported and catalogued than comparable crashes in conventional vehicles. If Tesla fatalities are subject to higher reporting rates in FARS or in the source data iSeeCars used, that would inflate the apparent numerator without reflecting a genuine difference in underlying crash frequency.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The study format as epistemic authority.** A "study" or "report" from a named organisation has a significantly higher credibility signal than an unsourced assertion. Most readers who encountered headlines saying "study finds Tesla has highest fatality rate" did not investigate the methodology of the study or the credibility of iSeeCars as a research institution. The existence of a named study functions as a credibility shortcut that bypasses methodological scrutiny. This epistemic shortcut is particularly effective when the conclusion aligns with pre-existing beliefs or dispositions — in this case, scepticism about Tesla as a company, concerns about self-driving technology, or broader anxieties about the safety of electric vehicles.

**Confirmation loops in polarised media environments.** The Tesla fatality rate claim entered a media ecosystem in which Tesla and Elon Musk are among the most politically polarising topics in American public discourse. In that environment, information that appears to confirm negative assessments of Tesla is shared enthusiastically by one side of the polarisation and dismissively denied by the other. Neither reflexive sharing nor reflexive denial is methodologically sound, but the polarisation itself inhibits the methodological inquiry that would be necessary to evaluate the claim properly. People who are predisposed to distrust Tesla share the study because its conclusion is congenial; Tesla supporters dismiss it as biased without engaging with the methodological concerns; and the intellectually appropriate response — "the study makes a specific claim but its methodology cannot be verified because it relies on proprietary data" — does not generate the same engagement as either affirmation or denial.

**The genuine and unresolved safety questions about autonomous driving technology.** Separate from the iSeeCars study's verifiability problems, there are genuine and ongoing questions about the safety of Tesla's Autopilot and Full Self-Driving technologies. NHTSA has launched investigations into multiple fatal crashes involving Autopilot engagement. There have been documented cases in which Tesla vehicles operating in assisted-driving modes have failed to detect stationary objects. The existence of these genuine, legitimately investigated safety concerns creates a background of plausibility against which the iSeeCars claim seems credible, even though the specific study relies on unverifiable proprietary data. The genuine concerns and the methodologically problematic study become conflated in public discourse, with the study lending numerical specificity to concerns that are otherwise difficult to quantify.

This is the epistemic novelty in this case: the claim does not exist in isolation but in a context populated by real, documented safety questions. The conflation of independently-grounded concerns with a methodologically questionable specific claim is not random; it reflects the cognitive tendency to treat claims that are consistent with a broader pattern as more credible than claims that are anomalous. The appropriate analytical response is to hold these two things separately: "there are legitimate and documented questions about Tesla's autonomous driving safety" (true) and "the iSeeCars study showing Tesla as #1 in brand-level fatal accident rates is independently verifiable and methodologically sound" (not demonstrated).


## 4. Verdict

**Unverified — the study exists and makes the claimed finding, but relies on proprietary data that prevents independent verification.** iSeeCars found and published a finding of 5.6 fatal crashes per billion vehicle miles for Tesla, which it ranked highest among manufacturer brands. However, the normalisation step that makes this a meaningful rate — the vehicle miles denominator — comes from iSeeCars' own dataset, which is not publicly available. Since iSeeCars did not respond to requests for methodological clarification, no independent evaluation of the study's accuracy is possible. The IIHS, using transparent and publicly documented methodologies, does not rank Tesla among the most dangerous car brands and gave the Tesla Model Y its highest safety designation for 2024. The claim that a study showed this is correct; the claim that the finding is accurate and reliable cannot be independently assessed with currently available information.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The iSeeCars Tesla fatality rate case is a useful template for understanding a category of misinformation that is not simply false but epistemically underspecified. A claim can be factually accurate at the level of "a study does say this" while being epistemically inadequate at the level of "the study's findings are verified and reliable." Much of the public discourse around research findings treats these two levels as equivalent — if a study says it, it happened — which skips the methodological scrutiny that distinguishes reliable from unreliable research.

This pattern is especially prevalent in analyses conducted by commercial entities — businesses, think tanks, advocacy groups, research arms of industry associations — that want to generate media-relevant findings and have structural reasons to control what data they share. Academic and government research is not immune to methodological problems, but it typically operates under norms of data transparency and peer review that are absent from commercially motivated research reports. The iSeeCars situation — a car search-engine site releasing a finding about car safety using its own proprietary data — is structurally opaque in ways that an IIHS analysis is not, and that opacity should trigger proportionally greater scrutiny.

For policy audiences, the case also highlights a structural gap in vehicle safety data infrastructure. The absence of a public, comprehensive, methodologically standardised dataset for vehicle miles driven by make/model/year means that any rate calculation involving miles driven must rely either on proprietary commercial data (iSeeCars) or on proxy estimates (as IIHS uses). Closing this data gap — through, for example, NHTSA-led collection of odometer data from vehicle inspections or registration records — would enable more rigorous and verifiable vehicle safety comparisons that currently exist only in forms that cannot be independently checked.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes published a detailed analysis of this claim in January 2025, written by Taija PerryCook, which noted both that the study exists and that its methodology relies on inaccessible proprietary data. The Snopes article's contribution was less a verdict than a methodological autopsy: examining what the study actually claimed, identifying the normalisation step where independent verification becomes impossible, and contrasting the iSeeCars findings with the IIHS analysis, which used transparent methodologies and reached qualitatively different conclusions about Tesla's safety relative to other brands.

For fact-checkers encountering similar claims based on studies from commercial research entities, standard methodological due diligence includes: (1) identifying what dataset was used as the denominator in any rate calculation, and whether that denominator is publicly available; (2) checking whether the producing organisation is primarily a research institution or a commercial entity with interests that may shape its research priorities and findings; (3) seeking the response of the organisation to methodological questions — as Snopes did, and iSeeCars declined to provide; (4) consulting independent organisations that conduct research on the same question using transparent methodologies; and (5) resisting the conflation of "a study says this" with "this finding has been independently verified." The discipline of distinguishing what a study claims from what can be independently confirmed is foundational media literacy for an information environment in which commercially produced research documents are routinely cited with the same authority as peer-reviewed academic work or government data.
