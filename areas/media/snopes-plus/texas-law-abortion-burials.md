# Texas passed a law requiring burial or cremation for aborted fetuses


## 1. The Claim

A recurring political claim holds that the state of Texas passed a law requiring that all aborted fetuses be given formal burial or cremation, treating fetal remains as equivalent to human remains that require dignified final disposition. The claim circulates in several forms. In some iterations it appears as straightforward reporting — "Texas passed a law requiring this" — presented as a statement of current or recent law. In others it functions as a political critique of anti-abortion governance: evidence that the state legislature has attempted to enforce a moral or religious view of fetal personhood through the regulatory machinery of public health law. In a third variant it appears in contexts of disbelief: "did you know Texas requires you to bury or cremate an aborted fetus?" — a rhetorical framing that treats the claim as demonstrating the extreme reach of Texas abortion legislation.

The claim typically references the 2017 Texas legislative session and Senate Bill 8, which did indeed contain a provision mandating dignified disposal of fetal remains. The bill was signed by Governor Greg Abbott and became a central exhibit in arguments about the scope of Texas anti-abortion policy. For many people who encountered this claim in 2017 or 2018, there was genuine factual basis for it — the Texas legislature had passed the law, it had received press coverage, and the governor had signed it.

What tends to be absent from the circulating claim is any mention of what happened next. The burial provision of Senate Bill 8 was challenged immediately in federal court, blocked before it could take effect, and ultimately struck down as unconstitutional. This means the claim is simultaneously true in one register (a law was passed) and misleading in another (that law was never enforced and was ruled unconstitutional). The relationship between a bill being signed and a rule being operative is collapsed into a single assertion that misleads by omission.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The 2016 administrative rules.** The story of Texas fetal remains legislation begins with administrative action, not legislation. In July 2016, the Texas Department of State Health Services (DSHS) proposed rules requiring that healthcare facilities dispose of fetal remains from abortions, miscarriages, and ectopic pregnancies through burial, cremation, or the spreading of ashes — specifically prohibiting disposal through the sewer system or incineration with remains going to a medical waste landfill, which were the two methods then in common use by abortion providers. The rules were announced quietly during a public health comment period and drew significant attention from reproductive rights organisations, who argued they imposed new costs and logistical burdens on abortion clinics without any corresponding public health benefit.

In December 2016, the Center for Reproductive Rights and Whole Woman's Health filed suit to block the rules. In January 2017, U.S. District Judge Sam Sparks issued a temporary injunction preventing them from taking effect. Judge Sparks found the rules were unconstitutionally vague, would impose material burdens on abortion providers and their patients, and that the stated benefits were too uncertain to justify those burdens under the constitutional standard then governing abortion regulations. The state initially appealed but later dropped the appeal when Senate Bill 8 was introduced.

**Senate Bill 8, 2017.** During the 85th Texas Legislative Session, Senate Bill 8 was introduced as a sweeping anti-abortion measure. It contained multiple provisions: a ban on so-called "dismemberment abortions" (a term used in the bill for the dilation and evacuation procedure); a prohibition on the donation of fetal tissue from abortions to medical research; and — crucially — the codification of the fetal remains disposal requirement that had previously been attempted through administrative rules. By embedding the requirement in statute rather than administrative regulation, supporters hoped to give it a firmer legal foundation.

The bill passed the Texas legislature and was signed by Governor Abbott in June 2017. The fetal remains burial provision required all healthcare facilities — not just abortion providers — to dispose of embryonic or fetal tissue from abortions, miscarriages, ectopic pregnancies, and other products of conception through burial (in-ground or entombment), cremation, or the spreading of cremated remains. The provision explicitly applied to tissue resulting from procedures at any gestational stage, including very early first-trimester procedures where the "remains" might consist of a small amount of tissue.

Healthcare providers, patients, and rights organisations saw several problems with the provision. First, it imposed costs on abortion clinics that would likely be passed on to patients — individual cremation or burial arrangements for tissue from early abortions added per-procedure expense that had no precedent in standard medical waste disposal. Second, it overrode patients' own preferences and, in some cases, religious or cultural beliefs about bodily disposal. Third, opponents argued the requirement served no legitimate public health purpose — standard medical waste disposal methods for fetal tissue were already regulated and posed no public health risk — and was instead a mechanism for expressing a moral or theological view of fetal personhood through healthcare regulation.

**The federal injunctions.** In January 2018, United States District Judge David Alan Ezra temporarily blocked the fetal remains burial provision of SB8. Judge Ezra applied the "undue burden" standard from the Supreme Court's ruling in *Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey* (1992) and from *Whole Woman's Health v. Hellerstedt* (2016), which required courts to weigh the burdens a regulation imposes on abortion access against the state's asserted benefits. Judge Ezra found that the state's arguments in support of the provision "lack merit," that the burdens were real and material, and that the asserted benefits were minimal. He also noted evidence suggesting that the stated purpose — expressing "profound respect for the life of the unborn" — was a pretext for restricting abortion access rather than a genuine public health justification.

In September 2018, Judge Ezra issued a permanent injunction blocking the provision, finding it unconstitutional under the *Hellerstedt* framework (Planned Parenthood of Greater Texas Surgical Health Services v. Kauffman, W.D. Tex. 2018). The permanent injunction meant the law could not be enforced. Texas appealed to the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals, which heard arguments in September 2019.

**The Indiana parallel and Supreme Court context.** In May 2019, while the Texas fetal remains case was working through the courts, the Supreme Court issued a ruling in *Box v. Planned Parenthood of Indiana and Kentucky* that addressed a related Indiana statute. The Court upheld the portion of an Indiana law requiring dignified disposal of fetal remains (as distinct from portions of the Indiana law that were at issue for other reasons), concluding that states have a "legitimate interest in the proper disposal of fetal remains." This ruling significantly altered the legal landscape for the Texas case, as it suggested federal courts should be more deferential to state fetal remains disposal requirements.

The Fifth Circuit's subsequent handling of the Texas case was complicated by this Supreme Court guidance. In August 2021, the Fifth Circuit vacated the district court's permanent injunction as to one component of SB8 related to alternative fetal-death-certificate methods, indicating a willingness to allow parts of the state's fetal remains regulatory framework to proceed — though the principal burial/cremation requirement's status remained in legal flux.

**Post-Dobbs shift.** In June 2022, the Supreme Court's ruling in *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* overturned *Roe v. Wade* and *Planned Parenthood v. Casey*, eliminating the federal constitutional right to abortion. This fundamentally altered the legal basis on which the Texas burial provision had been challenged — the "undue burden on abortion access" framework no longer had constitutional grounding as a federal matter. Texas's Human Life Protection Act, a pre-positioned trigger law, immediately took effect following *Dobbs*, effectively banning most abortions in the state except in narrow medical emergency situations. With legal abortion in Texas now severely restricted, the practical relevance of the fetal remains disposal rules changed: there are now far fewer abortions performed in the state to which the rules would apply, though the provisions continue to apply to fetal remains from miscarriages and ectopic pregnancies treated at healthcare facilities.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The law was real.** Unlike many viral claims that rest on fabrication, this one begins with a genuine legislative event. Senate Bill 8 was passed by the Texas legislature and signed by the governor in June 2017. The fetal remains burial provision was real text in a real statute. People who say "Texas passed a law requiring this" are describing something that actually happened in the legislative record. The error is not fabrication but the omission of subsequent events — specifically the fact that the law was immediately blocked and never took effect in the form the claim implies.

**Confirmation of political priors.** The claim functions differently in different political communities. For people who believe Texas anti-abortion legislation has gone to extraordinary lengths, the claim confirms a pre-existing mental model of politically motivated reproductive health regulation. For people who believe such claims are anti-Texas propaganda, the claim triggers dismissal. Both political valences reduce the likelihood that either group will seek out the full legal history, which is more complicated and less useful for either position than the simplified claim.

**Mechanism: legislation-as-fact conflation.** A specific epistemic error operates here: the routine social media conflation of a law being passed with a law being in effect and enforced. Legal challenges to legislation are a standard feature of legislative activity at the state level, particularly for abortion-related laws, but the existence and outcome of legal challenges is rarely part of the news cycle that non-specialist audiences follow. Most people who saw a 2017 headline saying "Texas passes law requiring burial of aborted fetuses" did not subsequently track the January 2018 temporary injunction, the September 2018 permanent injunction, the Fifth Circuit appeal, the Indiana Supreme Court ruling, or the post-Dobbs legal landscape. The passage of the law is a memorable, narratively coherent event; the legal aftermath is diffuse, technical, and procedurally complex. This asymmetry of memorability and shareability means the claim persists in the population long after the operative legal situation has changed.

**Religious and moral salience.** Burial and cremation carry deep religious and cultural significance in most traditions. A law requiring burial or cremation for aborted fetuses directly invokes that significance, triggering strong moral intuitions about bodily respect, personhood, and the state's role in enforcing moral views through law. Claims with high moral and emotional salience are more memorable and more likely to be shared than procedurally more accurate but emotionally neutral accounts of administrative litigation.


## 4. Verdict

**Recontextualised: true as a legislative fact, misleading as a description of operative law.** Texas did pass Senate Bill 8 in 2017, which included a provision requiring burial or cremation of fetal remains from abortions and other pregnancy-related procedures. This is factually accurate. However, the burial provision was immediately challenged in federal court, blocked by injunction before it could take effect, and permanently enjoined as unconstitutional in September 2018 under the undue burden framework. The law as described in the popular claim never operated as active enforced law. After *Dobbs*, the constitutional landscape that formed the basis for the injunctions shifted, and the legal status of the provision became more uncertain — but the context of drastically reduced legal abortion in Texas means the practical implications are fundamentally different from what the pre-2022 claim implied.

The verdict: the claim is accurate as a legislative record but requires substantial contextualisation to be accurate as a description of the legal and regulatory reality as it applied or applied at any given time.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Texas fetal remains legislation is part of a broader pattern of state-level regulation that uses healthcare administrative requirements to express moral or political views about abortion without directly prohibiting it. Legal scholars — including Priscilla Smith (2018, *Yale Journal of Law & Feminism*) and Robin Fretwell Wilson — have documented the expansion of this regulatory approach across multiple states in the decade following *Gonzales v. Carhart* (2007), in which the Supreme Court upheld a federal ban on one abortion method and signalled greater deference to Congress and state legislatures in regulating abortion procedure.

The specific strategy of requiring dignified fetal remains disposal has parallels in a number of other states. Indiana, Georgia, and Louisiana each passed related measures, and the Supreme Court's 2019 ruling in *Box v. Planned Parenthood* specifically upheld Indiana's version while declining to reach the constitutional questions on that state's grounds for banning abortions based on race or disability of the fetus. The *Box* ruling's endorsement of state interest in "proper disposal of fetal remains" became a key citation in subsequent lower court proceedings involving similar laws.

What makes the Texas case distinctively complex is the dual SB8 confusion. Texas used the same bill designation — SB8 — for two significantly different pieces of legislation, one in 2017 focused on fetal remains disposal and fetal tissue donation, and another in 2021 that became nationally famous as the Texas Heartbeat Act, establishing a private civil enforcement mechanism for a six-week abortion ban. Media coverage of the 2021 SB8 and its novel enforcement structure occasionally collided with prior coverage of the 2017 SB8, creating additional opportunities for claims about one law to be interpreted as claims about the other.

The post-*Dobbs* environment in Texas means that many pre-2022 claims about specific Texas abortion regulations now exist in a context where the constitutional framework that governed them has been substantially altered. Fact-checkers and media literacy educators working with these claims need to be attentive to the temporal dimension: a claim that was accurately described as "law passed but blocked" in 2018 may need to be updated or re-evaluated in the 2022 and later environment where the grounds for the block have been legally transformed.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim has not been directly addressed on major general fact-checking platforms in the form most commonly shared. The absence of a dedicated Snopes or PolitiFact entry likely reflects the claim's status as "complicated true" — fact-checking formats that produce clean True/False/Mixture verdicts find partial claims more difficult to handle, and the version of this claim that does the most work (Texas passed a law, full stop) is technically true at the legislative level, making the appropriate verdict something like "True with critical context," a category that many platforms find awkward to present and which generates less engagement than clean debunks.

Reliable primary sources include reporting by The Texas Tribune on the bill's passage, the injunctions, and the Fifth Circuit proceedings; the U.S. District Court opinions in *Planned Parenthood of Greater Texas Surgical Health Services v. Kauffman* (W.D. Tex. 2018); and the U.S. Supreme Court opinion in *Box v. Planned Parenthood of Indiana and Kentucky*, 587 U.S. ___ (2019). The Health and Safety Code of Texas (Tex. Health & Safety Code § 697.005) contains the current statutory text.

People encountering this claim in political discourse should ask: Is the speaker asserting that the law passed (true), that the law is currently in effect and enforceable (legally complicated), that the law ever applied to patients (it was blocked before enforcement), or that the law expresses the moral and political priorities of the Texas legislature (clearly supported by the legislative record)? Each of these is a different claim with a distinct truth value, and collapsing them into a single assertion produces a statement that is simultaneously factually grounded and substantively misleading.
