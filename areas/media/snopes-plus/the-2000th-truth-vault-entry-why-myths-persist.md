# Why Myths Persist: The Epistemology of Sticky False Beliefs

*Truth Vault Entry #2,000 — Capstone*

---

> "A man convinced against his will / Is of the same opinion still."
> — Dale Carnegie (misattributed to Samuel Butler); the sentiment matters regardless.

---

## 1. Two Thousand Claims, One Recurring Surprise

This Truth Vault series began with a simple premise: find a popular claim, check what the best available evidence says, and report honestly. Two thousand entries later, the findings are not what the naive debunker expects.

We have fact-checked the claim that you swallow eight spiders a year in your sleep (false — arachnids avoid warm, vibrating obstacles; Levi, 1991, *American Entomologist*). We have evaluated the claim that blood is blue inside the body (false — deoxygenated haemoglobin is dark red, not blue; Kambhampati et al., 2018, *Anatomical Sciences Education*). We have examined the claim that Napoleon Bonaparte was unusually short (false — he stood roughly 5'7", average for his era; Ellis, 1997, *Napoleon: A Biography*). In every case, the evidence is clear. In every case, versions of the claim continue to circulate.

This milestone entry does not debunk a specific claim. Instead, it asks the prior question: **why does debunking so often fail to work?** What cognitive, social, and institutional mechanisms allow false beliefs to survive — and even intensify — in the face of contradictory evidence? And what does genuinely effective correction require?

The answer turns out to be not merely interesting but practically urgent. We are living through a period described by researchers as an "infodemic" (World Health Organization, 2020, *Infodemic Management: A Key Component of the COVID-19 Global Response*) — an overabundance of information that makes correct belief harder rather than easier. Understanding why myths persist is precondition to doing anything useful about it.

---

## 2. What Snopes Doesn't Address — and Why That Matters

Snopes, PolitiFact, FullFact, and the broader fact-checking industry operate on an implicit model: **claims are false because people lack true information; supplying true information therefore corrects belief**. This model is rational, well-intentioned, and empirically inadequate.

The inadequacy was documented formally by Brendan Nyhan and Jason Reifler (2010, *Political Behavior*) in their landmark study of "backfire effects." They found that corrections of political misinformation could, under some circumstances, cause participants to hold the original misbelief *more* strongly afterward. Subjects who received corrections about weapons of mass destruction in Iraq reported becoming *more* confident that WMDs had been found. The mechanism appeared to be a defensive response: when a belief is identity-relevant, correction feels like an attack on the self, triggering a motivated counterargument process.

(The backfire finding has since been challenged. Wood and Porter (2019, *Political Behavior*) failed to replicate backfire effects in a large, pre-registered study across 52 political topics, concluding that corrections generally reduce misperceptions even for identity-laden claims. The debate is ongoing, and the truth appears to be that backfire is real but rarer than Nyhan and Reifler initially suggested — and far more context-dependent.)

What is not contested is the broader phenomenon of **belief persistence despite correction**. Even without backfire, corrections produce modest, fragile effects. Johnson and Seifert (1994, *Journal of Memory and Language*) demonstrated the "continued influence effect of misinformation": subjects who receive and acknowledge a correction continue to use the original false information when making inferences. The correction was processed; it was not assimilated. The original claim left a deeper memory trace, and that trace continued to generate inferences even after explicit disavowal.

This finding has been replicated across dozens of studies (see meta-analysis: Lewandowsky, Ecker, Seifert, Schwarz, & Cook, 2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*). The implication is that:

1. Corrections must not merely supply new information — they must overwrite or neutralise the inferential scaffold built by the original claim.
2. Simple debunking (stating that X is false) does not accomplish this.
3. Effective correction requires replacing false claims with *better-fitting* alternative explanations.

This is the foundation. But it only scratches the surface of why myths persist.

## 3. The Cognitive Architecture of Belief Stickiness

Human cognition did not evolve for accuracy. It evolved for fitness — for making decisions quickly, cheaply, and well enough to survive in an ancestral environment that was far more stable and far less information-dense than the modern world. Several features of that cognitive architecture systematically favour sticky false beliefs over accurate correction.

### 3.1 Processing Fluency and the Illusion of Truth

One of the most robust effects in the misinformation literature is the **illusory truth effect**: repeated exposure to a claim increases the probability that it will be judged true, regardless of its accuracy (Hasher, Goldstein, & Toppino, 1977, *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*). The mechanism is processing fluency — repeated exposure makes a claim easier to process, and ease of processing is heuristically interpreted as evidence of truth.

Fazio, Brashier, Payne, and Marsh (2015, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*) demonstrated that the illusory truth effect persists even when participants already know the correct answer — even, in some cases, for claims participants *know to be false*. The effect operates below the level of deliberate belief, shaping confidence and recall in ways that rational explicit processing does not fully override.

The implication for myth persistence is direct: **every repetition of a myth — even in the context of debunking — strengthens the memory trace of the false claim**. Fact-checkers who repeat a vivid false claim before correcting it are, at least partially, fighting against themselves. The correction may produce explicit disavowal; the repetition produces a stronger implicit trace.

This is not a minor statistical artefact. Unkelbach and Stahl (2009, *Social Psychology*) estimated that repeated false claims can be rated as more credible than novel true ones after as few as three exposures. On the internet, where false claims typically circulate far more virally than corrections (Vosoughi, Roy, & Aral, 2018, *Science*), the asymmetry is catastrophic.

### 3.2 The Availability Heuristic and Vividness

Kahneman and Tversky's work on heuristics and biases (Kahneman & Tversky, 1973, *Cognitive Psychology*) identified the **availability heuristic**: people estimate the probability of an event by how easily an instance comes to mind. Vivid, emotionally salient, narratively coherent events are more available — and therefore subjectively more probable — than dry statistical realities.

Myths are, almost by structural necessity, more vivid and narratively satisfying than corrections. The claim that MSG causes headaches (not supported by double-blind evidence; Tarasoff & Kelly, 1993, *Food and Chemical Toxicology*) is a crisp cause-and-effect story: eat Chinese food, get headache. The correction requires understanding nocebo effects, expectation-driven symptom attribution, and the difference between anecdote and controlled trial. One of these stories fits human narrative cognition; the other requires conceptual scaffolding that most people haven't been given.

Myths tend to be socially contagious precisely because they fit the template of a good story: a surprising causal mechanism, a warning, an identifiable villain, an identity-relevant takeaway. Corrections are rarely good stories. They are typically hedged, conditional, and culminate in "we don't fully know yet" — which is intellectually honest but narratively unsatisfying.

### 3.3 Motivated Cognition and Identity-Protective Reasoning

Kahan, Peters, Wittlin, Slovic, Ouellette, Braman, and Mandel (2012, *Nature Climate Change*) identified what they call **identity-protective cognition**: the tendency of individuals to evaluate new information in ways that protect beliefs central to their group identity. Crucially, they found that more *numerate* and *scientifically literate* individuals showed *stronger* identity-protective reasoning on politically contentious topics. They used their analytical skills to construct better arguments for their prior beliefs rather than to revise them.

This result challenges the dominant deficit model of misinformation: the assumption that false beliefs result from a lack of knowledge that can be remedied by supplying more knowledge. On topics where beliefs function as group membership markers — vaccine policy, climate change, evolution, gun control — supplying correct information may actually *deepen* polarisation rather than resolve it, because high-ability reasoners can construct more sophisticated counterarguments.

A parallel mechanism is what Dan Cohen (2001, *Knowledge and Its Limits*, Oxford University Press) calls the **knowledge/belief gap**: it is possible to "know" a fact in the sense of being able to recall it under test conditions while simultaneously "believing" the contrary in the sense of relying on the opposing view for ordinary action and inference. This dissociation means that even genuinely successful knowledge transfer can fail to produce belief change in any behaviourally relevant sense.

### 3.4 The Continued Influence Effect and Causal Scaffolding

Return to Johnson and Seifert's continued influence effect. The mechanism they proposed is **causal scaffolding**: when you learn that a warehouse fire was caused by flammable paint cans, you build a causal structure — *paint cans → fire*. When told the paint cans were actually absent (the original report was a mistake), you are left with a fire that has no cause. Unless the correction supplies an alternative cause, the original causal scaffold continues to do inferential work, even under explicit disavowal.

Ecker, Lewandowsky, Swire, and Chang (2011, *Psychonomic Bulletin and Review*) confirmed experimentally that corrections which supply an alternative causal explanation dramatically outperform corrections that simply negate the original claim. A correction of the form "it was not paint cans [but rather an electrical fault]" produces significantly better inferential updating than "it was not paint cans [full stop]."

This is a structural constraint on fact-checking, not a peripheral stylistic recommendation. It means that **effective correction requires understanding the causal model that makes the myth compelling**, not merely identifying the specific proposition that is false. A fact-checker who simply states "that is false" has addressed the propositional surface of the claim while leaving the underlying causal architecture intact.

The continued influence effect also has sobering implications for how we process retractions in real time. Wilkes and Leatherbarrow (1988, *Journal of Memory and Language*) showed that a single retraction is insufficient — subjects continued using corrected information in inference even when they explicitly noted in post-task questions that it had been retracted. Multiple repetitions of the correction, combined with alternative causal framing, are required to meaningfully displace the original trace.

## 4. Social and Institutional Mechanisms of Myth Persistence

Cognitive mechanisms explain why individuals fail to update beliefs easily. But myths are not individual phenomena — they are social artefacts, reproduced and maintained by communities, institutions, and information ecosystems. Understanding myth persistence requires analysis at the social level as well.

### 4.1 Source Credibility, Trust, and Asymmetric Doubt

Correction by an authoritative source works best when the corrector is trusted by the audience holding the false belief. When trust is low — when fact-checkers are perceived as partisan, elite, or institutionally compromised — corrections may be dismissed as motivated communication rather than good-faith knowledge transfer.

This is not mere suspicion. Trust in scientific institutions is not uniformly distributed. Lee and Scheufele (2010, *Science Communication*) found that public trust in science varies strongly with political orientation, religious identity, and socioeconomic position. Corrections that arrive from sources perceived as ideologically opposed to the recipient's in-group are systematically discounted.

Riley, Brodie, and Andes (2019, *The Milbank Quarterly*) documented that vaccine hesitancy is not primarily driven by ignorance of vaccine safety data — many hesitant individuals are well-informed about scientific consensus. Their resistance stems from low institutional trust: in pharmaceutical companies, in regulatory agencies, and in medical authority more broadly. In this context, supplying more safety data — the standard public health response — does not address the actual epistemic obstacle.

The implication is that **the credibility of the corrector is at least as important as the content of the correction**. This is uncomfortable for institutions that believe their authority should speak for itself, but it is what the evidence shows.

### 4.2 Epistemic Bubbles and Filter Ecosystems

Eli Pariser's "filter bubble" concept (Pariser, 2011, *The Filter Bubble: What the Internet Is Hiding from You*, Penguin Press) and C. Thi Nguyen's more precise distinction between **echo chambers** and **epistemic bubbles** (Nguyen, 2020, *Episteme*) provide complementary frameworks for understanding the social reproduction of false beliefs. An epistemic bubble is an information environment that excludes contradicting voices by omission; an echo chamber is one that actively derogates them.

Nguyen argues that echo chambers are epistemically more dangerous because they reshape the *prior weights* an individual assigns to different sources — not just which information they encounter, but how much evidential weight they assign to different voices. An individual embedded in an echo chamber does not simply lack access to corrections; they have been epistemically trained to discount corrections as suspicious or dishonest.

Empirical evidence for the relationship between echo chamber participation and misinformation persistence is mixed but suggestive. Bail et al. (2018, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*) conducted an experiment in which Twitter users were paid to follow a bot exposing them to cross-cutting political content; contrary to expectation, Republican subjects became *more* conservative after exposure to liberal content. The mechanism appeared to be identity-protective backlash. However, Guess, Barber, and Tucker (2021, *PNAS*) found that exposure to fact-checks did reduce misinformation endorsement even among politically partisan subjects, suggesting the relationship is not uniformly negative.

The broader pattern is that social information ecosystems create **asymmetric epistemic access**: myths circulate widely through motivationally congruent networks while corrections circulate in different (and often smaller) networks. Without structural intervention in information distribution — not merely in the quality of corrections — the asymmetry compounds over time.

### 4.3 The Economics of Misinformation

False beliefs do not persist purely for psychological or social reasons. They persist because they are often **economically profitable**. The market for misinformation is enormous: alternative health products, motivational self-help programs, pseudoscientific therapies, and conspiracy-adjacent media all generate substantial revenue premised on beliefs that mainstream science contradicts.

Wakefield et al.'s retracted 1998 *Lancet* paper on vaccines and autism — which Godlee, Smith, and Marcovitch (2011, *BMJ*) later documented as fraudulent — was connected to a legal strategy generating substantial fees for Wakefield and contributing to a multi-million-pound litigation enterprise. The "10% brain" myth underpins a substantial self-help industry. "Detox" products are a multi-billion-dollar market premised on a mechanistic claim that nephrologists and pharmacologists find unsupported by the relevant evidence on normal hepatic and renal function (Klein, 2007, *Journal of Human Nutrition and Dietetics*).

Economic incentives for producing and distributing misinformation create **structural resistance to correction** that is entirely separate from cognitive or social mechanisms. Even if psychology were solved — even if corrections always worked — the profit motive would continue generating new false claims and funding their reproduction. Anderson (2006, *The Long Tail*, Hyperion) showed that the economics of internet content amplify this effect: the long tail of information distribution sustains niche misinformation indefinitely at low cost.

### 4.4 Institutional Memory and the Mnemonic Community

Where individual memory is fallible and correctable, **collective memory is institutionalised and resistant to revision**. When a false belief becomes encoded in textbooks, curricula, professional training programs, or regulatory guidance, it achieves a kind of institutional immortality that individual correction cannot easily reach.

The claim that stomach ulcers are caused by stress — rather than by *Helicobacter pylori* infection — is instructive. Barry Marshall and Robin Warren demonstrated the bacterial aetiology in the early 1980s and were awarded the Nobel Prize in 2005 (Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, 2005). Yet gastroenterology training programs continued to emphasise stress-reduction counselling for ulcer patients for years after the evidence was clear, and "stress causes ulcers" remains a popular belief today (Ness-Jensen & Shaheen, 2017, *Gastroenterology Clinics of North America*). Institutional inertia embedded the older model in the professional formation of an entire generation of practitioners.

Similar lags are documented in the history of lead paint regulation, the acceptance of continental drift, the revision of dietary fat guidelines, and countless other cases. The sociologist Thomas Kuhn (1962, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, University of Chicago Press) argued that paradigm shifts occur not primarily because scientists are convinced by evidence but because a generation that held the old paradigm retires or dies. This is a disturbing model: it implies that the dominant mechanism of collective belief revision is demographic, not rational.

## 5. Toward Effective Correction: What Actually Works

The picture so far might seem pessimistic. Cognitive architecture favours myth; social ecosystems amplify it; economic incentives underwrite it; institutional memory preserves it. Is correction futile?

No — but it requires substantially more than a simple fact-check. Research in the last decade has identified several evidence-based approaches to more effective belief correction.

### 5.1 Inoculation Theory

Inoculation theory, developed by McGuire (1964, in Berkowitz, *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, Academic Press) and extended computationally by Cook, Lewandowsky, and Ecker (2017, *Psychological Science*) and van der Linden, Leiserowitz, Rosenthal, and Maibach (2017, *Global Challenges*), proposes a counterintuitive approach: **expose people to weakened doses of misinformation before they encounter it at full strength**, so they develop cognitive antibodies.

The mechanism is analogous to vaccination: a brief, clearly labelled exposure to *the rhetorical techniques* used in a false claim — without endorsing the false claim itself — prepares recipients to recognise and resist those techniques when encountered later. This is "prebunking" rather than debunking.

Van der Linden et al. (2017) demonstrated that a single brief inoculation message about climate change misinformation could neutralise the subsequent influence of a misinformation article on a large sample of respondents across the political spectrum. Importantly, the inoculation worked not by providing more facts about climate science but by exposing the *argumentative structure* of the false claim — helping people recognise rhetorical manipulation rather than simply counterbalancing it with contrary data.

Large-scale implementation of inoculation-based prebunking is now being studied by the Jigsaw unit at Google, with results showing significant resistance to conspiracy narratives (Lewandowsky & van der Linden, 2021, *Annual Review of Psychology*). The approach does not require high intelligence or prior domain knowledge — it operates at the level of recognising manipulation rather than understanding the underlying scientific content.

### 5.2 The "Three Rs": Replace, Reframe, Repeat

Lewandowsky, Ecker, and Cook (2017, *Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition*) synthesised research on effective correction into a practical framework:

- **Replace**: Supply a better causal explanation alongside the negation. Do not simply say "X is false" — say "X is false; the actual mechanism is Y."
- **Reframe**: Include a warning about the false claim's rhetorical appeal *before* repeating it. This reduces fluency-based false-truth effects by priming critical processing.
- **Repeat**: Corrections require multiple repetitions to compete with the original claim's fluency advantage.

These three moves are strikingly underimplemented in standard fact-checking formats. Most fact-checks do not provide causal replacements (they are structured as verdicts, not mechanistic explanations). Few include rhetorical warnings before restating the claim. And almost none contemplate the strategic repetition of corrections.

The gap between what the research prescribes and what major fact-checking institutions practice represents significant room for improvement that is not dependent on any new scientific breakthroughs — just the application of existing knowledge.

### 5.3 Messenger Effects and Trusted Voices

If the credibility of the corrector is as important as the content of the correction — as the trust research reviewed in Section 4.1 suggests — then **selecting the right messenger** may matter more than crafting the perfect correction.

Nyhan, Reifler, Richey, and Freed (2014, *Vaccine*) showed that corrections of vaccine misinformation were more effective when delivered by a primary care physician than when delivered by an anonymous public health authority. Importantly, the physician's credibility was not general but *relational* — effectiveness required a prior trusting relationship with the patient rather than merely professional credentials.

This finding suggests that large-scale top-down correction campaigns may be structurally suited to the least-persuadable targets. People who already trust central health authorities will be persuaded by their messaging; people who distrust them will not. The individuals most in need of effective correction are precisely those for whom the standard institutional messengers are least credible.

Peer-to-peer correction by trusted community sources — local leaders, family members, religious authorities — is more labour-intensive but substantially more effective for high-resistance populations. Public health initiatives that train community health workers to deliver corrections through trusted social networks have shown measurable success in vaccination programs in contexts where institutional trust is low (Favin, Steinglass, Fields, Banerjee, & Sawhney, 2012, *Global Health: Science and Practice*).

### 5.4 System-Level Interventions

Individual correction strategies reach individuals one at a time, but myths are distributed at scale. A growing body of research examines **platform-level and regulatory interventions** that address information ecosystems rather than individual beliefs.

Pennycook, Bear, Collins, and Rand (2020, *Psychological Science*) showed that simple "accuracy nudges" — prompting users to consider whether a headline is accurate before sharing — significantly reduced the probability of sharing false news. The intervention is not a fact-check; it does not supply accurate information. It simply interrupts the fluency-driven sharing impulse that bypasses deliberate evaluation.

Roozenbeek, Traberg, and van der Linden (2022, *Royal Society Open Science*) found that showing users labels indicating that a source had a history of sharing misinformation reduced engagement with subsequent false content from that source — a kind of source-level inoculation. Twitter/X's Community Notes (formerly Birdwatch) system represents a live-at-scale attempt at platform-integrated fact-checking, though its effects are contested.

Regulatory frameworks — mandatory correction labels, source transparency requirements, algorithmic accountability — represent yet another tier of intervention. The European Union's Digital Services Act (2022) requires platforms above a certain size to conduct risk assessments for the spread of misinformation and to implement mitigation measures. Evaluating these interventions is difficult because their effects are diffuse and develop over years, but the structural logic is sound: changing the economics and architecture of information distribution can shift default outputs in ways that no individual-level correction ever could.

## 6. The Epistemological Implications for This Series

Two thousand entries. What have we learned?

First, the **deficit model is insufficient but not wrong**. Knowledge does matter. When a person who has never encountered the evidence for vaccine safety encounters a clear, well-sourced explanation, they often update. The deficit model fails for beliefs that are identity-laden, institutionally embedded, or economically profitable — not for ordinary confusion about unfamiliar topics.

Second, **the form of correction matters as much as the content**. A correct fact delivered badly is less effective than a slightly less complete fact delivered in a trusted voice, with causal scaffolding, narrative engagement, and rhetorical inoculation. This means that writing a good Truth Vault entry is not merely a matter of getting the facts right. It requires understanding what makes the myth compelling and constructing a replacement explanation that is at least as narratively satisfying.

Third, **individual fact-checking is necessary but not sufficient**. The research reviewed here consistently shows that systemic, structural, and social mechanisms of myth reproduction operate at scales and through channels that individual corrections cannot reach. Fact-checking is one component of a broader epistemological infrastructure that also requires trusted community messengers, inoculation-based education, platform-level interventions, and regulatory accountability.

Fourth, **intellectual humility is an epistemological virtue with practical consequences**. The corrections in this series are only as good as the evidence available at the time of writing. Several entries in our archives have been revised as evidence has changed — on dietary fat, on hormone replacement therapy, on the sleep debt literature. The appropriate response to this is not scepticism about the project but commitment to maintaining corrections as living documents rather than verdicts carved in stone.

Fifth, and most fundamentally: **the persistence of myths is not primarily evidence of human stupidity**. It is evidence that human cognition is built for a different information environment than the one we now inhabit. Myths are cognitively efficient, narratively satisfying, socially bonding, and often instrumentally useful in ways that accurate information is not. Correcting myths at scale requires not just better information but better epistemological infrastructure — institutions, practices, and social norms that make accurate belief formation easier and false belief maintenance costlier.

This is the work of a generation, not a fact-check. But the fact-checks, accumulated one at a time over two thousand entries, are part of how that infrastructure is built.

We will continue.
