## §1 Claim and Verdict

The claim that turkey makes people specifically drowsy—and that the recognized "food coma" post-Thanksgiving is attributable to turkey's tryptophan content—is **False**. Snopes author Barbara Mikkelson correctly identifies the core error: turkey contains approximately the same tryptophan per serving as chicken, ground beef, and pork (roughly 350 mg per 4-ounce serving), and less than Swiss cheese on a gram-by-gram basis. If tryptophan were the operative mechanism for post-meal drowsiness, all of these foods would induce sleep in equal measure—yet none of them carries the same cultural association with somnolence.

The claim's persistence is explicable through at least three mechanisms: the salience of Thanksgiving as a culturally marked event in which many Americans simultaneously experience post-meal drowsiness, a real but misapplied fact about tryptophan as a precursor to sleep-regulating neurochemicals, and the reinforcement of the turkey-sleep association through media—most notably the 1997 Seinfeld episode in which characters deliberately induce sleep through wine-and-turkey administration, which the Snopes article specifically references as a "reinforcement to yet another generation of viewers." The cultural myth is therefore maintained by a combination of misattribution, partial truth, and media amplification.

The full scientific story is more interesting than either the myth or its simple refutation suggests. Tryptophan does play a role in sleep regulation—but through pathways that are specifically *inhibited* when tryptophan is consumed alongside other protein sources, which is precisely how turkey is consumed at Thanksgiving.

## §2 Historical and Factual Record

The turkey-sleep association is difficult to date with precision. The Snopes article does not provide a specific origination point, and the tracing of nutritional myths to their precise popular-press emergence is generally difficult given the diffuse nature of their propagation. The tryptophan component of the myth required awareness of tryptophan as an amino acid and its relationship to sleep regulation—knowledge that entered mainstream health discourse primarily through the commercial marketing of L-tryptophan as a dietary sleep supplement in the 1970s and 1980s.

The L-tryptophan supplement craze reached regulatory crisis in 1989-1990, when approximately 1,500 cases of eosinophilia-myalgia syndrome (EMS) were traced to contaminated batches of L-tryptophan from a single Japanese manufacturer, Showa Denko (Mesce, 1990, *Associated Press*, March 22; *The New York Times*, August 9, 1990). The FDA recalled all tryptophan supplements in 1990. Importantly, retrospective analysis suggested the EMS cases were linked to a specific contaminated batch (a product of an altered fermentation process) rather than to tryptophan itself; tryptophan as such appears safe at dietary levels. However, the regulatory action removed L-tryptophan from the supplement market for over a decade and may have paradoxically reinforced the idea that tryptophan was a potent sleep-relevant compound by associating it with dramatic regulatory events.

The specific nutritional facts are well-established. Carroll and Vreeman (2009, *Don't Swallow Your Gum!*, St. Martin's Press, pp. 119-120) provide a clear summary: chicken and ground beef contain approximately the same tryptophan as turkey (~350 mg per 4-ounce serving). Mark Leyner and Bill Goldberg (2005, *Why Do Men Have Nipples?*, Three Rivers Press, pp. 44-45) offer a similar debunking, placing turkey's tryptophan content in comparative context with pork and cheese. The Snopes article notes that the sleep-inducing doses used in clinical tryptophan supplement research were 500-2,000 mg daily—systematically higher than a single turkey serving provides, and taken in isolation from other protein sources rather than embedded in a multi-protein holiday meal.

The post-meal drowsiness that Thanksgiving reliably produces is better explained by a multi-factor account: alcohol consumption (common at holiday meals, directly sedating via GABA-A receptor agonism), caloric overload (meals of 2,000-4,000 kcal for a single sitting in some cases), high carbohydrate load (mashed potatoes, stuffing, pie), and the physiological redistribution of blood flow to the gastrointestinal tract during active digestion—a state sometimes called postprandial somnolence or informally "food coma." The Snopes article cites an unnamed source noting that solid-food meals produce faster fatigue onset than liquid diets, and that increased metabolic rate for digestion and increased GI blood flow contribute substantially to post-meal lethargy independent of any specific nutrient.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty and Scientific Context

The epistemic novelty of this entry lies in correctly explaining *why* tryptophan would not induce sleep when consumed as part of a protein-rich meal—a mechanistic nuance absent from the Snopes article and from most popular debunking treatments.

The pathway from dietary tryptophan to serotonin (and subsequently melatonin) requires tryptophan to cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) via the large neutral amino acid (LNAA) transporter. This transporter is shared by tryptophan and several other large neutral amino acids—tyrosine, phenylalanine, leucine, isoleucine, valine, and methionine. Competition for this transporter is the key regulatory mechanism: when plasma concentrations of competing amino acids are high relative to tryptophan, less tryptophan crosses the BBB, and serotonin synthesis is attenuated (Fernstrom & Wurtman, 1972, *Science*, Vol. 178, No. 4063). This is the fundamental finding that established the dietary regulation of brain serotonin as a field of study.

The pharmacological implication is critical: L-tryptophan supplements were effective as sleep aids precisely because they were taken *in isolation*, on an empty stomach or with carbohydrates but not with protein. In this context, the tryptophan/LNAA ratio in plasma is artificially elevated, maximizing BBB crossing and brain serotonin synthesis. When tryptophan is consumed as part of a protein meal—turkey dinner surrounded by multiple other protein sources, each contributing their own complement of competing LNAAs—the ratio is dramatically unfavorable and tryptophan's access to the brain is actually *reduced* relative to fasting baseline (Markus et al., 2000, *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, Vol. 71, No. 6).

Put concisely: the conditions under which dietary tryptophan would meaningfully increase brain tryptophan and enhance serotonin-melatonin synthesis are precisely the conditions absent at a Thanksgiving meal. The myth has the mechanism exactly backwards: turkey consumed as part of a mixed protein meal is the least effective delivery vehicle for sleep-relevant tryptophan.

The carbohydrate paradox is also worth registering. High-carbohydrate, low-protein meals actually *do* increase brain tryptophan via insulin-mediated clearance of competing LNAAs from plasma (Wurtman et al., 1981, *Journal of Psychiatric Research*, Vol. 17, No. 2). Insulin stimulates muscle uptake of branched-chain amino acids (leucine, isoleucine, valine) but does not affect tryptophan (which is largely bound to albumin in plasma), so a high-carbohydrate meal reduces the competing LNAA population without reducing tryptophan, effectively increasing the tryptophan/LNAA ratio. This mechanism suggests an irony: the carbohydrate-heavy components of a Thanksgiving meal (stuffing, sweet potatoes, pie) may contribute more to post-meal tryptophan availability and serotonin synthesis than the turkey itself.

However, even this mechanism must be placed in context. The magnitude of tryptophan ratio change from a typical carbohydrate-rich meal is modest, occurring over hours rather than minutes, and its contribution to post-meal drowsiness is secondary to the direct sedating effects of alcohol and the physiological burden of caloric processing. Markus et al. (1998, *Journal of Psychiatric Research*, Vol. 32, No. 1) found that high-carbohydrate meal interventions produced modest improvements in psychological stress response via tryptophan pathway effects, but this is a far cry from inducing immediate post-meal sleep.

EARC rating: **E** (Empirically Confirmed)—the specific false claim is well-established in nutritional science, and the correct mechanistic explanation is corroborated by multiple peer-reviewed studies. Epistemic gap: **Low**.

## §4 Neuroscience of Postprandial Somnolence

The phenomenon popularly called "food coma"—the drowsy, lethargic state following a large meal—has a genuine physiological basis that is more complex than popular accounts suggest. Several concurrent mechanisms contribute.

First, postprandial blood redistribution: following a large meal, parasympathetic nervous system activation promotes gastric motility and enzymatic secretion, increasing mesenteric blood flow. The increased metabolic demand of digestion competes with other physiological functions. The brain, which constitutes approximately 2% of body mass but receives approximately 15% of cardiac output at rest, may experience reduced relative blood flow during peak postprandial GI demands. Woods et al. (2000, *British Medical Journal*, Vol. 321, No. 7266) discuss the cardiovascular mediators of postprandial hypotension, noting that substantial blood pressure drops occur in some individuals following large meals, correlating with increased drowsiness.

Second, gut hormone signaling: large meals provoke the release of cholecystokinin (CCK), glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), and peptide YY, among other hormones. CCK was identified as a potential sleep-promoting factor by Papadimitriou et al. (1988, *Peptides*, Vol. 9, No. 2), who found that CCK administration increased non-REM sleep in rats. Whether the postprandial CCK spike from a large meal contributes meaningfully to human drowsiness remains insufficiently characterized.

Third, adenosine accumulation: the metabolic processing of a large caloric load increases cellular ATP consumption and, consequently, adenosine production. Adenosine is a primary sleep-promoting signal operating through adenosine A1 and A2A receptors in the brainstem and basal forebrain (Bjorness & Greene, 2009, *Current Neuropharmacology*, Vol. 7, No. 3). This mechanism is the same one that caffeine blocks to promote wakefulness—caffeine acts as an adenosine receptor antagonist. The adenosine accumulation during intensive metabolic processing provides a mechanistic thread from caloric load to sleep pressure that does not require tryptophan at all.

Fourth, circadian alignment: for many people, the timing of large holiday meals at midday or early afternoon coincides with a secondary peak in circadian sleep pressure (the "post-lunch dip" in alertness) that is endogenous to human circadian rhythms and visible even in fasting individuals (Lavie, 1986, *Psychological Bulletin*, Vol. 99, No. 3). Thanksgiving dinner, often consumed in late afternoon, may coincide with or slightly precede this window.

Together, these mechanisms produce postprandial somnolence through multiple converging physiological channels that collectively swamp any marginal tryptophan-related effect, particularly when alcohol is also present.

## §5 Cultural and Media Context

The turkey-sleep association is a case study in how media amplification crystallizes and perpetuates nutritional mythology. The Seinfeld episode "The Merv Griffin Show" (Season 9, Episode 6, originally aired November 6, 1997) explicitly incorporated the turkey-tryptophan logic as a plot device: Jerry and George deliberately exploit it as a pharmaceutical strategy. The episode presented the turkey-as-sedative not as a question to be investigated but as established fact—part of the shared cultural knowledge that the characters (and viewers) could take for granted.

Fictional treatment of folk science as established fact is a powerful vector for myth perpetuation. When a comedy beloved by tens of millions of viewers builds a plot around the turkey-sleep association, the association is reinforced not through persuasion or argument but through implicit assumption. Nobody in the episode fact-checks the tryptophan claim; it is simply the known world that the comedy inhabits. Viewers who internalize the story internalize its premises along with it.

The turkey-sleep myth also benefits from what Lewandowsky et al. (2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, Vol. 13, No. 3) call the "continued influence effect"—the phenomenon whereby corrective information, even when accepted, fails to fully displace the original belief's influence on reasoning. Even readers who learn that turkey contains no more tryptophan than chicken may find themselves "remembering" post-Thanksgiving drowsiness as specifically turkey-related, because the original association is embedded in episodic memory linked to specific sensory and emotional experiences.

Thanksgiving itself is a powerful contextual anchor. The holiday is associated with specific cultural scripts: large family gatherings, abundant food, football viewing, and afternoon napping. The napping norm is culturally established independently of any specific food; in America, it is simply "what you do" after Thanksgiving dinner. This normative napping behavior then generates the experience of post-turkey drowsiness in millions of people simultaneously, which is subsequently attributed to the most culturally salient feature of the meal (the turkey) rather than to the more diffuse contributing factors (alcohol, caloric load, circadian timing, social relaxation).

There is also selection bias in memory: the many times a person has eaten turkey (sandwiches, casual meals) without becoming drowsy are not encoded as "didn't feel sleepy after turkey" because there was no salient expectation to disappoint. The Thanksgiving instances, with their unique cultural context and expectation of drowsiness, are remembered precisely because they confirm the association. This confirmatory memory structure is self-maintaining even without any documentary or scientific support.

## §6 Research Gaps and Verdict Summary

The primary research gaps relevant to this entry divide into mechanistic and applied questions.

On the mechanistic side, the specific contribution of postprandial CCK release to human drowsiness is not yet adequately characterized in normal human subjects consuming normal meal-size quantities. Animal studies show clear CCK-sleep effects; human studies have used pharmacological CCK administration rather than meal-induced CCK release, making translation to dietary conclusions uncertain. Similarly, the specific contribution of adenosine accumulation to the magnitude of postprandial drowsiness in humans is plausibly modeled but not directly measured in the post-meal context.

On the applied side, there is no dietary intervention study that has directly compared isocaloric meals with identical macronutrient ratios but different protein sources (turkey vs. chicken vs. beef vs. pork) and measured post-meal sleepiness and sleep onset latency. Such a study would directly test the turkey-specific claim and is apparently absent from the published literature. The evidence basis for the null claim—turkey is not distinctively sleep-inducing—is thus primarily inferential from the tryptophan equivalence data and the mechanistic account of LNAA competition, rather than from direct comparative dietary trials.

The broader public health relevance of nutritional myths is itself a genuine research topic. Schwartz and Woloshin (2013, *JAMA Internal Medicine*, Vol. 173, No. 3) documented systematic deficiencies in nutritional reporting in mass media, finding that quantitative data presentation and contextual comparison (e.g., "turkey has the same tryptophan as chicken") were rare in popular coverage of nutritional topics. The turkey-sleep myth is a canonical example of a nutritional association claim that is technically grounded in real biochemistry but misapplied to a context where the mechanism does not operate. Corrective communication requires not just providing the true fact (equal tryptophan across proteins) but explaining the mechanism (LNAA competition at the BBB) that explains why tryptophan from turkey doesn't produce its theoretical effect—a level of mechanistic explanation that is rarely provided in mainstream journalism or social media debunking.

**Verdict summary:** The claim that eating turkey makes people specifically drowsy because of its elevated tryptophan content is **False**. Turkey contains comparable tryptophan to other common protein sources; post-Thanksgiving drowsiness is attributable to caloric overload, high carbohydrate intake, alcohol consumption, circadian timing, and GI blood redistribution. Tryptophan's sleep-relevant effects require specific dietary conditions (isolated consumption, low competing protein) that are absent during a typical turkey meal. EARC rating: **E** (Empirically Confirmed). Epistemic gap: **Low**.
