# The Onion was banned from Twitter


## 1. The Claim

On April 21, 2022, many users of Twitter — and subsequently Reddit, Facebook, and various media commentary outlets — encountered what appeared to be alarming news: The Onion, the longstanding American satirical publication, had been permanently banned from Twitter. The Onion's own Twitter account was the source of this breaking news, posting a series of tweets in rapid succession lamenting the ban. These posts were presented in The Onion's characteristic mock-earnest headline style: declarative, slightly over-explained, with the kind of bureaucratic gravity the outlet has historically deployed to make ordinary things sound calamitous.

The claim spread across social media with meaningful velocity. Reddit threads such as those in r/GamerGhazi and r/OutOfTheLoop generated hundreds of comments from users processing the apparent news. The posts were taken at face value by a noteworthy proportion of readers, partially because the contextual moment — April 2022 — was an active period of genuine controversy around Twitter's content moderation decisions, and partially because the satirical delivery was subtle enough that readers unfamiliar with The Onion's ironic mode missed its orientation. Screenshots were shared without the context of the original account, and in that decontextualised form the claim circulated as straight news: "The Onion has been banned on Twitter."

The claim became sufficiently widespread that Snopes published a formal fact-check on the same day, April 21, 2022, authored by journalist Bethania Palma, rating the claim "Labeled Satire" and explaining that The Onion's account remained active throughout. The Snopes entry was the primary instrument of formal correction, providing a searchable reference against which the claim could be checked for users who encountered it after the initial viral moment.

The question of whether The Onion was actually banned from Twitter in April 2022 is, therefore, the surface of a more interesting and more layered question about what The Onion was doing — and why, in a specific political moment, enough people missed the joke.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The Onion was not banned from Twitter.** The April 21, 2022 tweets from The Onion's official account claiming a permanent ban were satirical content posted from an active, unbanned account. The Onion's Twitter presence — operating as @TheOnion, at the time of writing still active under that handle or its successor — was not suspended, shadow-banned, or otherwise restricted by Twitter's platform moderation systems on that date or in the immediate surrounding period. The posts appeared, spread, and continued to be visible from the same account on the same platform. The joke was that the account was simultaneously claiming to be banned and demonstrably not banned.

**The specific satirical target.** The April 2022 Onion posts were not random comedy. They were a timed satirical intervention responding to a specific news environment that made the joke legible to attentive readers. Two related events provided the context:

First, on April 19, 2022, Twitter temporarily suspended the account of The Babylon Bee, a Christian conservative satirical website, over a tweet — which the platform had flagged as violating its rules against hateful conduct — calling then-Assistant Secretary for Health Dr. Rachel Levine the outlet's "Man of the Year" in a transphobic framing. The Babylon Bee's suspension became a cause célèbre in conservative media, with numerous voices invoking the language of cancel culture, Big Tech censorship, and the silencing of conservative satire.

Second, on April 19, 2022, Washington Post journalist Taylor Lorenz published an article identifying Chaya Raichik as the person behind the "Libs of TikTok" Twitter account. The Libs of TikTok account had been building a following by reposting content from LGBTQ+ people and institutions to its conservative audience with commentary that critics argued functioned as targeted harassment. Around the same period, the account itself was temporarily suspended from Twitter for violating the platform's harassment policies, and the account's operators loudly proclaimed that suspension as an instance of censorship.

These two events produced a specific and recognisable discourse pattern in the political media environment of late April 2022: real or claimed Twitter suppression being used as a political identity performance. Accounts and commentators were declaring their imminent banning or protesting their temporary suspensions in ways that generated audience sympathy and media attention regardless of whether any substantive censorship had occurred. The Onion's satirical gambit was a precise parody of this behaviour — specifically of accounts that perform victimhood around platform bans while continuing to use the platform, and of the discourse that makes platform bans into partisan symbols.

**The Snopes verdict.** Bethania Palma's April 21, 2022 Snopes article (Palma, B., 2022, "The Onion Has Been Banned on Twitter, Tweets The Onion," *Snopes*, April 21, 2022) provides the definitive contemporaneous fact-check. Palma noted that The Onion's account was active, that the tweets directly mocked the pattern of partisan Twitter accounts claiming bans that never materialise, and that the publication of the Lorenz/Libs of TikTok story provided the immediate trigger. The rating "Labeled Satire" is Snopes' category for content that is intentionally satirical but has been taken as fact by some audiences.

**The history of The Onion's Twitter presence.** The Onion has maintained an active Twitter presence since the platform's early mainstream growth period. The account has not been without controversy: in 2013, The Onion's Twitter was hacked by the Syrian Electronic Army (a pro-Assad hacktivist group that targeted multiple Western media organisations' social accounts), which resulted in several false news posts being published before the hack was detected and the account secured (Maclean's, 2013; CNET, 2013). Separately, in 2013, The Onion deleted and apologised for a tweet about then-nine-year-old Oscar nominee Quvenzhané Wallis that used a misogynistic slur, which CEO Steve Hannah publicly called "outrageously offensive" (Hannah, S., 2013, apology statement via The Onion).

None of these events — the 2013 hack, the 2013 offensive tweet deletion — constitute a ban. The April 2022 posts constitute intentional satire. There is no moment in the documented history of The Onion's Twitter/X presence at which the account was banned by the platform.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The Onion's irony is not universal.** Research on media literacy and satire comprehension consistently finds that satirical intent is not universally recognised, and that recognition rates vary significantly by familiarity with the source, political alignment, and contextual framing. A study by LaMarre, Landreville, and Beam (LaMarre, H. L., Landreville, K. D., & Beam, M. A., 2009, "The Irony of Satire: Political Ideology and the Motivation to See What You Want to See in *The Colbert Report*," *The International Journal of Press/Politics*, 14(2), 212–231) found that audiences' ideological commitments shaped whether they read particular satirical content as sincere or ironic, with politically opposed audiences reading the same material in opposite orientations. A similar dynamic affects comprehension of Onion content: readers who encounter it without visual or contextual cues indicating its satirical register may process it as straight reporting.

**Screenshot decontextualisation.** The claim spread substantially through screenshots of The Onion's tweets that were shared independently of the Twitter interface, removing platform context (including the verified account status and format that sometimes cues experienced users to the source). A screenshot of an Onion tweet saying "The Onion has been permanently banned from Twitter" looks, in isolation, like a media report. Context that would cue the satirical reading — the name of the account, the format of The Onion's characteristic style, the presence of other obviously satirical posts in the thread — was often stripped in the screenshot-sharing process.

**Epistemic novelty: the Poe's Law topology of political satire in 2022.** The April 2022 Onion posts succeeded simultaneously as satire and as confusion-generating content because of a distinctive feature of the media environment they inhabited. Poe's Law, the informal internet principle formulated by Nathan Poe in a 2005 Usenet post (Poe, N., 2005, post to Christian Forums, cited in McDonald's documentation of the law's propagation), holds that without a clear marker of intent, parody of extremist positions is indistinguishable from genuine extremism. A corollary that the 2022 moment illustrates is that in a highly polarised media environment, *satirical performance of victim behaviour becomes indistinguishable from genuine victim behaviour*. The Onion's fake ban was indistinguishable in form from actual accounts' genuine (if sometimes exaggerated) reports of platform suppression because the source material for the parody was itself performed rather than purely factual. Many conservative accounts in April 2022 were performing outrage at bans in stylised, theatrical ways. Parodying theatrical performance of victimhood requires an audience that recognises the performance as theatrical, which that audience did not consistently do among non-Onion-familiar readers. This is a specifically 2022 political media phenomenon, and it is epistemically novel: the satirical object (performed platform-ban grievance) was so close to its own parody that the parody could not be reliably distinguished from the thing itself. The Onion did not merely make a joke; it revealed something about the nature of a media moment in which authentic and performed grievance had become formally inseparable.

**The plausibility of The Onion actually being banned.** For readers aware of the Babylon Bee suspension or other conservative media claims about platform bias, the idea that a satirical outlet critical of conservative politics might face a Twitter ban was not obviously absurd. In a media environment in which real suspensions were being debated and Twitter's moderation inconsistencies were under genuine scrutiny, "The Onion banned on Twitter" was not self-evidently false in the way that, say, "dogs banned on Twitter" would be. The possibility was live enough to be credible, which increased the confusion.

**Volume and velocity.** The Onion posted multiple tweets in rapid succession as part of the bit, each one further elaborating the "ban" (including, as noted in the Snopes article, posting that its intern had died from the pain of the ban). This volume and urgency of posting mimicked the way real crisis communications and breaking news threads propagate on Twitter, adding to the verisimilitude for readers not familiar enough with The Onion's style to recognise the parody of that form.


## 4. Verdict

**False: intentional satire rated "Labeled Satire" by Snopes.** The Onion was not banned from Twitter on April 21, 2022. The Onion's own tweets claiming the ban were intentional satire targeting the pattern of partisan accounts performatively lamenting platform suppression — most proximately, discourse around The Babylon Bee's temporary suspension and the Libs of TikTok controversy of the same week. The Onion's account remained live and active throughout. Snopes (Palma, 2022) rated the claim "Labeled Satire" on the same day the posts appeared.

There is no moment in the verified history of The Onion's Twitter presence at which the account was banned by the platform. The April 2022 incident is entirely a case of intentional satire that was taken literally by a proportion of the audience, and of that literalised version spreading through screenshot decontextualisation and the peculiar conditions of the April 2022 political media environment.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Onion's fake Twitter ban belongs to a long tradition of the publication's most effective satirical manoeuvres: producing content that reveals something true about a media moment by performing its most characteristic absurdity with enough fidelity that the performance is briefly indistinguishable from the thing it mocks. The Onion has done this across multiple decades: its famous post-September-11 issue, its satirical congressional testimony performance in 2011, its 2012 "endorsement" of Kim Jong-un for Time Person of the Year, which Time declined to remove from the voting system. In each case, the joke works precisely because it is accurate enough to be confused with non-satire.

The April 2022 case is distinctive in that the satirical target was not a stable social phenomenon (political pomposity, celebrity culture, corporate spin) but a *current media behaviour* in the middle of occurring. The Onion was satirising something that was happening right now, at the same speed at which it was happening. This real-time satirical mimicry is much harder for audiences to locate — there is not enough temporal or cultural distance to create the recognition gap that allows satire to be identified as satire.

Research on satire comprehension in the social media era (Young, D. G., 2019, *Irony and Outrage: The Polarized Landscape of Rage, Fear, and Laughter in the United States*, Oxford University Press) documents how the accelerated circulation of content strips contextual frames and produces misreading at scale. Young's analysis would predict exactly the kind of reception The Onion's April 2022 posts received: the most politically engaged audiences — those who would most likely share the posts — were also the most likely to be primed to read them through the lens of the real April 2022 moderation controversies, which activated a framework incompatible with satire recognition.

The broader media literacy dimension involves the distinction between satire-as-correction and satire-as-evidence. When The Onion produces content, it is almost always functioning as correction — using humour to expose the structure of something. When that content circulates as evidence (i.e., as proof that a real thing happened), it stops functioning as satire and starts functioning as misinformation, regardless of the producer's intent. The April 2022 posts became evidence in the hands of readers who took them literally. This transformation — from satire to misinformation through reception — is one of the fundamental dynamics of how satirical journalism interacts with contemporary information ecosystems, and it has no clean solution.

The Babylon Bee comparison is instructive. The Babylon Bee is itself a satirical outlet — described as such — but has been consistently noted by media researchers as producing content where the satirical frame is sometimes deployed strategically to provide cover for ideologically motivated misinformation (Funke, D., 2021, "The Babylon Bee is a right-wing satire site, but its content has become harder to distinguish from misinformation," *Poynter*, October 2021). The proximity of The Onion's fake ban to The Babylon Bee's real ban created a media environment in which the category "satirical outlet claiming platform ban" contained both legitimate satire and ideologically motivated performative grievance simultaneously. The Onion's bit was precisely aimed at this ambiguity — and the effectiveness of the satire was inseparable from the confusion it generated.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes handled this case with notable speed and clarity. Bethania Palma's April 21, 2022 entry (Palma, B., 2022, *Snopes*) appeared on the same day as the original posts, which reflects both Snopes' operational speed on viral social media events and the significance of the story in the Twitter news cycle. The "Labeled Satire" rating is the correct category: it distinguishes between content that is false because of error or deception and content that is false because it is intentionally fictional — i.e., satire — but has been misread as factual by some audiences.

The "Labeled Satire" category on Snopes raises its own challenges for media literacy. A rating system designed for factual claims does not map cleanly onto satirical content, because the "claim" being fact-checked in a satire case is not what the producing outlet claims — they claims nothing factual — but what audiences have understood. The fact-check is, functionally, a check on the accuracy of a misreading rather than on the accuracy of a production. This is an unusual epistemic position and points to a gap in conventional fact-check infrastructure: the tools optimised for checking factual assertions are less well suited to adjudicating cases where the primary information failure is a comprehension failure rather than a production failure.

For media literacy educators, the Onion fake-ban case provides an unusually clean illustration of several key concepts simultaneously: the importance of source context in determining ironic register; the dynamics of screenshot decontextualisation; the Poe's Law problem in politically polarised information environments; and the distinction between satire functioning as satire versus satire circulating as misinformation. The fact that the case was addressed by Snopes on the same day it occurred also provides a teachable resource: students can compare the original posts, the coverage, and the Snopes entry as a synchronous media event in a way that is often impossible with older cases.

The case also documents something about The Onion's institutional approach to its own platform presence. The publication has used its social media accounts not merely as distribution channels for its web content but as independent creative venues — the Twitter-as-satirical-stage model the April 2022 posts exemplify. This practice has precedents in The Onion's print and web history (the mock newspaper format, the mock-earnest television show on IFC, the satirical congressional appearance) and represents a distinct strand of institutional satirical identity that differs from how most media organisations conceive of their social media use. Understanding this institutional orientation is a necessary prerequisite for reading The Onion's social media output accurately.
