# The Science of Fright: Why We Love to Be Scared


## 1. The Claim

**Core phenomenon**: Each Halloween season, millions of people voluntarily pay to be frightened — standing in lines at haunted house attractions, watching slasher films and supernatural horror through their fingers, seeking out jump scares and haunted hayrides with evident pleasure. This behavior is, on its face, paradoxical. Fear evolved as an aversive signal — a deterrent response to genuine threat that motivates escape or defense. If fear is fundamentally unpleasant and motivationally directed toward avoidance, why do people seek it out, and why do so many report enjoying the experience?

**The psychiatric perspective**: The article analyzed here was authored by Arash Javanbakht and Linda Saab, both Assistant Professors of Psychiatry at Wayne State University, and was republished by Snopes via The Conversation in October 2019. As practicing psychiatrists who study fear neurobiology and treat fear disorders clinically, Javanbakht and Saab bring both mechanistic and clinical perspectives to the question. Their analysis is grounded in the well-established neuroanatomy of fear processing and in the emerging understanding of how contextual appraisal modulates the subjective experience of arousal.

**The key explanatory question**: The article addresses three interrelated questions: (1) What are the neurobiological mechanisms by which the brain generates and processes fear? (2) Why do some people experience high-arousal fear states as pleasurable rather than aversive? (3) Why do some people not enjoy being scared, and why do fear responses sometimes exceed contextual appropriateness? These questions map onto a fourth, clinical dimension: when does the fear-regulation system go wrong, producing anxiety disorders and post-traumatic stress?

**Cultural framing**: The piece is published around Halloween as an accessible entry point into fear neuroscience, but the questions it addresses are fundamental to understanding not just seasonal entertainment but one of the most evolutionarily ancient and clinically significant psychological systems in the brain. Fear is, as Javanbakht and Saab note, "as old as life on Earth" — a deeply conserved biological mechanism present in organisms from snails to humans, shaped by hundreds of millions of years of selection pressure and instantiated in neural circuitry that we share in substantial part with other vertebrates.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The amygdala as fear-detection center**: The fear response initiates in the amygdala, a paired almond-shaped structure in the medial temporal lobe consisting of several functionally distinct nuclei. The amygdala's role in emotional salience detection — determining how much a stimulus "stands out" — is among the most replicated findings in affective neuroscience. Lesion studies in both humans and non-human primates, combined with neuroimaging data from healthy subjects, converge on the conclusion that the amygdala is critical for the recognition of threatening stimuli and for triggering downstream fear responses (LeDoux JE, 2000, *Annual Review of Neuroscience*, vol. 23, pp. 155–184). The amygdala responds particularly strongly to facial expressions of fear and anger — social signals of potential danger — and activates in response to any stimulus that has been associated through learning with aversive outcomes.

When a threat stimulus is detected — a predator, a dark alley, a stranger moving aggressively — the amygdala triggers a cascade of changes throughout the body via the hypothalamus and the brainstem. The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis activates, releasing cortisol into the bloodstream. The sympathetic nervous system drives norepinephrine release, which produces the familiar physical manifestations of acute fear: increased heart rate and blood pressure, pupil dilation, bronchodilation, hepatic glucose release, and vasoconstriction in the gastrointestinal tract (redirecting blood to skeletal muscle). The brain becomes hyperalert, with enhanced sensory processing and attentional narrowing. These changes collectively prepare the organism for rapid action — the "fight or flight" response first systematically described by Walter Cannon (1932) in *Wisdom of the Body* (W.W. Norton, New York).

**The role of the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex**: Fear responses do not operate purely through the amygdala in isolation. The hippocampus — a structure immediately adjacent to the amygdala and densely connected with it — encodes the contextual associations of fear: where threats were previously encountered, what stimuli co-occurred with aversive outcomes, what environmental features predict danger. Hippocampal-amygdala interaction is essential for contextual fear conditioning, the process by which a previously neutral environment acquires fear-triggering properties through association with an aversive event (Maren S and Fanselow MS, 1996, *Hippocampus*, vol. 6, pp. 347–352).

The prefrontal cortex serves a critical regulatory function in the fear circuit. Specifically, the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) is involved in fear extinction — the process by which previously learned fear responses are suppressed when a stimulus repeatedly occurs without the aversive outcome (Milad MR and Quirk GJ, 2002, *Nature*, vol. 420, pp. 70–74). The vmPFC provides inhibitory input to the amygdala, dampening fear responses when contextual appraisal indicates that a threatening stimulus is not currently dangerous. This cortical modulation — the "thinking brain" reassuring the "emotional brain" — is the mechanistic foundation of the voluntary fear experience. When you enter a haunted house knowing you are in no real danger, your vmPFC-mediated contextual appraisal sends inhibitory signals to the amygdala, allowing the high-arousal sympathetic activation to be experienced as excitement rather than terror.

**Learning fear: personal experience, observation, and language**: Fear acquisition is not pre-programmed for specific content but is learned through experience and social transmission. Javanbakht and Saab identify three principal mechanisms. First, direct conditioning: a dog attack produces a conditioned fear response to dogs and related stimuli — a process that has been studied since Watson and Rayner's foundational (if ethically contested) "Little Albert" study in 1920. Second, observational learning: watching another person be attacked by a dog, or watching a scary film in which dogs attack, produces fear conditioning without direct experience of the aversive event — a mechanism studied extensively by Mineka and Cook in the context of primate fear learning (1988, *Journal of Abnormal Psychology*, vol. 97, pp. 585–591). Third, instructional learning: symbolic information alone — a sign reading "Dangerous dog" — can produce fear conditioning through language-mediated representation of threat, an evolutionarily novel mechanism unique to language-competent species (Olsson A and Phelps EA, 2007, *Nature Neuroscience*, vol. 10, pp. 268–275). This third mechanism is particularly important: humans can acquire powerful fears without any direct encounter with the feared stimulus, through verbal and written description alone.

**Why some people enjoy being scared**: The neurochemical overlap between fear and positive arousal provides the mechanistic foundation for voluntary scare-seeking. The primary neurotransmitters and hormones activated in the fear response — norepinephrine, dopamine, adrenaline (epinephrine) — are the same compounds involved in many pleasurable states including exercise-induced euphoria, sexual arousal, and excitement at positive unexpected events. The sympathetic nervous system activation pattern of "excitement" is physiologically nearly identical to that of "fear": elevated heart rate, heightened alertness, accelerated breathing. Schachter and Singer's (1962) two-factor theory of emotion, published in *Psychological Review* (vol. 69, pp. 379–399), proposed that emotional experience involves both physiological arousal and cognitive labeling — the same arousal state can be experienced as fear or as excitement depending on how the situation is cognitively appraised.

This "re-labeling" process is precisely what Javanbakht and Saab describe as the mechanism of enjoyable fear. In the haunted house context, the cortical appraisal of safety — "this is not real, I am not in danger" — allows the high-arousal sympathetic state generated by the amygdalar fear response to be re-coded as excitement. The temporal dynamics are also important: the re-labeling appears to work best when the initial fear response is relatively brief and rapidly followed by the contextual safety signal, which may explain why jump scares and brief intense frights are often more enjoyable than prolonged moderate dread.

Social context contributes independently. Fear, like other emotional states, is socially contagious through a process understood in terms of emotional mimicry and neural resonance — observing another person's emotional expression produces correlated physiological and subjective responses in the observer (Hatfield E, Cacioppo JT, and Rapson RL, 1993, *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, vol. 2, pp. 96–99). When a companion at a haunted house transitions from screaming to laughing in immediate response to a fright, that transition communicates a re-labeling from threat to amusement, and observers socially update their own emotional labeling in response. Groups that experience voluntary fear together show greater enjoyment and social bonding through this mechanism — a finding consistent with the empirical work on shared emotional experience and social cohesion.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty: the phenomenological intuitiveness of fear-as-aversion and the non-obvious nature of contextual re-labeling**

The neuroscience of voluntary fear enjoyment is genuinely non-obvious — it requires understanding that the same physiological state can receive opposite subjective valences depending on cognitive context, which is a sophisticated and counterintuitive claim. Most laypeople's folk psychology treats fear and pleasure as fundamentally opposed, on opposite ends of an affective valence dimension. The scientific insight that high arousal can be re-labeled across valences depending on cognitive appraisal conflicts with this intuition.

This is why a large fraction of people report being genuinely puzzled by their own scare-seeking behavior: they know they enjoy haunted houses but cannot fully articulate why, precisely because the folk-psychological model that fear is simply bad does not accommodate the experience. The Javanbakht and Saab explanation — that the enjoyment depends on the contextual re-labeling enabled by prefrontal safety appraisal — provides a mechanistically coherent account of an experientially puzzling phenomenon. The power of the explanation lies in its ability to account not just for why some people enjoy being scared but for why some don't: if the fear response is too intense, if the experience is "too real," if the prefrontal safety signal is overwhelmed by amygdalar activation, the re-labeling fails and the experience becomes simply terrifying rather than fun.

The related insight about individual differences — that people differ systematically in their tendency to experience fear as pleasurable based on differences in amygdalar reactivity, vmPFC inhibitory capacity, anxiety sensitivity, and sensation-seeking temperament — also has a folk-psychological resonance: people know that some of their friends enjoy horror movies and others find them intolerable, but they often attribute this to character ("brave" vs. "timid") rather than to neurobiological individual variation. The neuroscientific framing transforms a moral characterization into a mechanistic one, which is both more accurate and more epistemically useful.


## 4. The Broader Pattern

**The cultural universality of voluntary fear**: The seeking of fear-inducing experiences is documented across cultures and historical periods, suggesting it responds to deep features of human psychology rather than being culturally specific. Horror genres exist in literature from ancient times — the ghost stories of ancient Greece and Rome, Japanese *kaidan*, the Gothic tradition in English literature — and fear-inducing spectacle has been a feature of public entertainment from gladiatorial combat to theatrical tragedy. The institutionalization of a holiday (Halloween in the North American/European tradition) specifically dedicated to fear aesthetics represents a cultural acknowledgment of the positive social and psychological value of safe fear encounters.

This universality is consistent with several broader psychological theories. Terror management theory (Greenberg J, Pyszczynski T, and Solomon S, 1986, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, vol. 50, pp. 435–449) proposes that humans have a specific psychological need to confront and manage existential anxiety about mortality, and that cultural rituals including horror entertainment serve this function through safe engagement with death and threat themes. Anxiety sensitivity theory (Reiss S, 1991, *Behaviour Research and Therapy*, vol. 29, pp. 141–152) offers a complementary perspective: the capacity to tolerate and even enjoy physiological fear arousal is a learnable skill, and voluntary scare-seeking may serve a training function that reduces maladaptive anxiety sensitivity through repeated controlled exposure.

**The clinical mirror**: The enjoyment of voluntary fear sits at one end of a continuum whose other end is characterized by anxiety disorders — conditions in which the fear-regulation system fails to apply appropriate contextual modulation. Post-traumatic stress disorder represents a particularly severe failure mode: the vmPFC safety signal is insufficient to suppress amygdalar fear responses to trauma-associated stimuli, producing intrusive fear memories, hypervigilance, and avoidance that persists long after the original threatening context has passed (Rauch SL, Shin LM, and Phelps EA, 2006, *Biological Psychiatry*, vol. 60, pp. 376–382). The approximately 8% lifetime prevalence of PTSD cited by Javanbakht and Saab is consistent with epidemiological estimates in developed countries (Kessler RC et al., 1995, *Archives of General Psychiatry*, vol. 52, pp. 1048–1060). The approximately 25% lifetime prevalence of any anxiety disorder cited in their piece is consistent with large-scale epidemiological surveys including the National Comorbidity Survey (ibid.).

The parallel between the voluntary fear enthusiast and the PTSD patient is mechanistically precise: both experience elevated amygdalar fear responses to specific stimuli. The critical difference is in the efficacy of the prefrontal regulatory system: the haunted house visitor retains the ability to apply safety context and re-label their experience; the PTSD patient's prefrontal regulatory capacity has been compromised by trauma in ways that make contextual safety signals insufficient to suppress the conditioned fear response. This mechanistic symmetry — same input system, different regulatory output — explains both the pleasure of voluntary fear and the suffering of anxiety disorders within the same theoretical framework.


## 5. Evidentiary Assessment

**Quality of core neuroscience evidence**: High. The neuroanatomy and neurochemistry of fear described by Javanbakht and Saab is well-established and has been replicated across numerous laboratories, species, and methodologies. The role of the amygdala in fear initiation, the hippocampus in contextual encoding, and the prefrontal cortex in fear regulation are among the most robust findings in behavioral neuroscience. The description of the physiological fear response (sympathetic activation, HPA axis engagement, bodily changes) accurately reflects the established literature.

**Quality of evidence for contextual re-labeling as the mechanism of enjoyable fear**: Moderate to high. The two-factor theory of emotion and subsequent work on arousal misattribution and re-labeling have substantial empirical support (for a review: Barrett LF, 2006, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, vol. 1, pp. 28–58). Direct neuroimaging studies of horror film viewing and haunted house experiences are a more limited literature, but the available evidence is consistent with the proposed mechanisms. A study by Scrivner et al. (2021), in *Evolutionary Human Sciences* (vol. 3, e42), specifically examined haunted house visitors and found that those in the affective "sweet spot" between too little and too much fear arousal reported the most enjoyment, consistent with the model.

**Quality of evidence for social contagion of fear re-labeling**: Moderate. The emotional contagion literature is robust, but specific studies examining social re-labeling from fear to enjoyment in group scare contexts are limited. The observation is consistent with broader emotional contagion findings but represents an interpretive extension rather than a directly evidenced claim.

**Known limitations**: Individual differences in the preference for voluntary fear experiences are real and likely multidetermined (genetics, prior trauma, anxiety sensitivity, sensation-seeking traits). The article appropriately acknowledges variation but does not present a complete model of individual difference predictors.


## 6. Verdict and Implications

The science of voluntary fear enjoyment, as described by Javanbakht and Saab, reflects well-established neuroscience and provides a mechanistically coherent account of a phenomenon that puzzles many people about their own behavior. The core claims — amygdalar fear initiation, prefrontal contextual regulation, neurochemical overlap between fear and positive arousal, contextual re-labeling as the mechanism of enjoyable fear — are supported by decades of research across animal models and human populations.

The broader implications are clinically and culturally significant. The same circuitry that enables haunted house enjoyment, when disrupted by trauma or dysregulation, produces the suffering of anxiety disorders. Understanding the fear-regulation system mechanistically provides the theoretical basis for effective treatments: cognitive-behavioral therapy for anxiety exploits the prefrontal re-labeling mechanism directly, training patients to apply safety appraisal to previously feared stimuli; exposure therapies similarly rely on vmPFC-mediated extinction of conditioned fear responses. The neuroscience of voluntary fear is not merely a curiosity of Halloween psychology but a window into fundamental mechanisms of emotional health and pathology.

The specific claim that roughly one in four people experiences an anxiety disorder over their lifetimes — and that these disorders are treatable effectively through psychotherapy and medication — is one of the most important public health messages embedded in this article. The stigma attached to anxiety and fear disorders often reflects an implicit folk-psychological model in which fear is a character deficiency rather than a neurobiological condition. The mechanistic framing offered by Javanbakht and Saab provides an alternative model: fear disorders are conditions in which evolutionarily ancient and otherwise adaptive neural circuitry has been disrupted in specific ways that produce suffering. This model is more accurate, more clinically useful, and more consistent with the empirical evidence than any alternative.
