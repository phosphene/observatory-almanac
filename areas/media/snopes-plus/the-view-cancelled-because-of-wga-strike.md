# The Truth Vault: 'The View' Cancelled Because of the WGA Strike?

## 1. The Claim

In August 2023, as the Writers Guild of America (WGA) strike entered its hundredth day and the entertainment industry faced one of its most significant labor disruptions in decades, a YouTube channel named Just In (@JustInCeleb) published a video bearing the headline "Whoopi Goldberg PANICS As 'The View' Gets CANCELLED After NEW Backlash." The video, which accumulated over 400,000 views, asserted that ABC's long-running daytime talk show "The View" had been cancelled as a direct consequence of the WGA strike. The video's description elaborated that "the fallout from this strike affected the show's quality, as the witty banter and thought-provoking discussions we've come to expect started to dwindle," and claimed that hosts were "forced to steer clear of any topics remotely connected to the Writers Guild, leaving them with a limited pool of material to work with."

The claim spread rapidly across social media platforms, including Twitter, TikTok, and Facebook, where users shared the video and accompanying text as though it constituted legitimate entertainment news. The narrative was simple and emotionally resonant: a beloved television program, long considered a cultural institution, had fallen victim to labor tensions sweeping through Hollywood. For viewers who had noticed "The View" was not airing new episodes, the explanation seemed plausible and even obvious.

But the claim was entirely fabricated. "The View" had not been cancelled. It was never in danger of being cancelled. The show was, in fact, on a scheduled seasonal break, having concluded its latest season on August 4, 2023 — a week before the YouTube video appeared. The WGA strike had nothing to do with the show's hiatus. Entertainment news publication The Wrap had already reported on the routine nature of this seasonal pause, explaining precisely when the show would return. Moreover, "The View" operated under a Network Code contract — a specific type of agreement governing certain television programs — that allowed it to continue production during the WGA and SAG-AFTRA strikes, a fact that host Whoopi Goldberg had publicly addressed weeks earlier.

## 2. What's Actually True

What makes this episode worth examining in depth is not the falsehood itself — Snopes dispatched that capably enough — but the genuine realities that the false claim parasitically attached itself to. Several elements of the story were, in fact, true, and it was precisely this grounding in reality that gave the fabrication its credibility.

The WGA strike was real and consequential. Beginning on May 2, 2023, and lasting 148 days, the strike represented one of the longest work stoppages in Hollywood history. The writers were fighting for fair compensation in the streaming era, for protections against artificial intelligence, and for residuals that reflected the fundamentally changed economics of television production. By August, the strike had reached its hundredth day, a milestone of shame that both the WGA and the Alliance of Motion Picture and Television Producers (AMPTP) acknowledged as unprecedented in its duration and intensity. The strike dominated entertainment news, affected production schedules across the industry, and generated enormous public interest and discussion.

SAG-AFTRA had also joined the picket lines on July 14, 2023, marking the first time since 1960 that both the writers' and actors' unions had struck simultaneously. This double strike brought Hollywood to a near-complete standstill, with productions halted, premieres disrupted, and the entire industry grappling with existential questions about its future. The economic impact was estimated in the billions of dollars.

"The View" had, in fact, addressed the strikes on air. Whoopi Goldberg, who has served as moderator since 2007, explained to viewers on July 17, 2023, why the show continued broadcasting despite the SAG-AFTRA strike. She noted that programs like "The View," soap operas, game shows, and news programs operate under what is called the Network Code — a different kind of contract that permits continued production during certain labor actions. This was an accurate and important clarification that Goldberg offered in a spirit of transparency and solidarity with striking workers.

The entertainment industry was genuinely experiencing disruption. The WGA and SAG-AFTRA strikes affected everything from late-night talk shows (which went dark immediately) to streaming series (whose productions were paused indefinitely) to film releases (whose premieres lacked cast participation). Any consumer of entertainment news in the summer of 2023 would have been aware of the widespread impact. The claim about "The View" simply inserted itself into this landscape of genuine disruption, hoping that audiences primed to expect cancellations and shake-ups would accept the addition as credible.

The YouTube channel Just In had built an audience by producing content that occupied a deliberately ambiguous space between entertainment news and rumor. A disclaimer in the video description acknowledged that "content might be gossip, rumors, exaggerated or indirectly besides the truth," and the video itself displayed an on-screen warning that its content "might be gossip, rumors, or exaggerated." These disclaimers, however, were easily overlooked by viewers scrolling through their social media feeds, who encountered the video's dramatic headline without the caveats. The channel's existence and growth reflected a genuine market demand for celebrity gossip and entertainment speculation — a demand that legitimate outlets serve, but that less scrupulous producers also exploit.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes's fact-check of this claim, written by Izz Scott LaMagdeleine and published on August 11, 2023, is a competent and mostly thorough debunking that deserves credit for its clarity and evidentiary foundation. The article correctly identifies the claim as false, provides the key contextual detail that "The View" was on a scheduled break rather than cancelled, cites The Wrap's reporting, and notes the disclaimer on the Just In video as a hallmark of unreliable content. Snopes also helpfully connects this claim to a pattern of falsehoods from the same source, referencing a previous Snopes fact-check about a fabricated story concerning Whoopi Goldberg "losing everything" in a lawsuit from Jason Aldean.

Where Snopes excels is in its straightforward demolition of the factual claim. The article establishes, in clear and concise language, that "The View" was not cancelled, presents evidence for the real reason the show was not airing new episodes, and provides source links that readers can verify independently. This is the core function of fact-checking journalism, and it is performed professionally.

However, the Snopes treatment, while accurate, operates within a relatively narrow frame that leaves important dimensions of the story unexplored. The article does not meaningfully engage with the labor context that gave the false claim its resonance. The WGA strike is mentioned as the stated cause of the alleged cancellation, but the article does not examine why audiences might have found that explanation plausible, nor does it explore the genuine tension between the show's need to continue producing content and the broader labor struggle in which the entertainment industry was embroiled. The Network Code contract that allowed "The View" to keep airing is referenced through Goldberg's on-air explanation, but Snopes does not analyze the implications of this arrangement — the way it positioned "The View" and similar programs as exceptions to the strike, operating in a gray area between struck and non-struck work.

The article also misses an opportunity to examine the media ecosystem that produced and amplified this falsehood. The Just In channel is identified as the source, and its use of disclaimers is noted, but Snopes does not explore the YouTube economy that rewards sensational content, the algorithmic dynamics that propelled the video to over 400,000 views, or the role of social media platforms in distributing the claim beyond YouTube. The mention of a pattern of falsehoods from the same source is useful but superficial.

More significantly, Snopes does not address the structural vulnerability that makes "The View" a target for this kind of misinformation. The show has long been a lightning rod for political controversy, with its panel of hosts representing a spectrum of viewpoints and its format encouraging the kind of heated debate that generates both loyal viewership and intense criticism. A show that is frequently the subject of cancel-culture discourse, that has weathered multiple host controversies, and that operates in the volatile landscape of daytime television, is precisely the kind of institution that rumors of cancellation will reliably attach to. Snopes treats the story as an isolated falsehood rather than as an episode in an ongoing pattern of media distrust and misrepresentation.

Finally, the Snopes article does not consider the broader implications of the false claim for understanding how labor information — and misinformation — circulates during high-profile strikes. The WGA and SAG-AFTRA strikes generated enormous public interest and a corresponding demand for information about which shows were affected and how. This information vacuum was filled not only by legitimate reporting but also by speculation, rumor, and deliberate fabrication. Understanding how and why false claims about the strikes proliferated requires an analysis that goes beyond individual fact-checks, and Snopes does not offer that analysis.

## 4. Why This Matters

The false claim about "The View"'s cancellation matters for several reasons that extend well beyond the specific inaccuracy at its center.

First, the episode illustrates a fundamental dynamic of misinformation in the entertainment space: the parasitic relationship between genuine disruption and fabricated narrative. The WGA and SAG-AFTRA strikes represented a real and significant upheaval in the entertainment industry. The strikes had genuine consequences — halted productions, delayed releases, economic hardship for thousands of workers. Into this landscape of authentic disruption entered a fabricated claim about one specific show's cancellation. The false claim did not need to invent a crisis; it simply needed to position itself as a specific instance of a crisis that everyone already knew was happening. This pattern — attaching false specifics to genuine general disruption — is a recurring feature of misinformation across domains, from politics to health to entertainment.

Second, the case demonstrates the limits of fact-checking as a response to misinformation. Snopes performed its function correctly: it investigated the claim, found it false, published a debunking with supporting evidence, and made that debunking available to the public. Yet the original video had already accumulated over 400,000 views by the time the fact-check was published, and the social media shares were already in circulation. The debunking, however thorough, would reach only a fraction of the audience that had already been exposed to the false claim. This is not a criticism of Snopes, which cannot reasonably be expected to prevent the spread of falsehoods, but an acknowledgment of the structural asymmetry between the production of misinformation and the production of fact-checks.

Third, the episode raises important questions about platform responsibility. The Just In channel operated with disclaimers that acknowledged its content might be "gossip, rumors, exaggerated or indirectly besides the truth," yet YouTube's recommendation algorithm propelled the video to hundreds of thousands of views. The platform's economic incentives — which reward engagement, and engagement rewards sensationalism — are in direct tension with its stated commitments to information quality. This is not a problem unique to YouTube, but it is a problem that the "The View" cancellation story illustrates with particular clarity.

Fourth, the case reveals something about public attitudes toward established media institutions. "The View" is a show that people love to hate. It is frequently the subject of criticism from across the political spectrum — from conservatives who see it as a liberal echo chamber and from progressives who see it as insufficiently radical. This ambivalence creates a receptive audience for negative claims about the show. The false cancellation story capitalized on this preexisting skepticism, offering an explanation for the show's absence that fit a preexisting narrative of decline and deserved comeuppance.

Fifth, and perhaps most importantly, this misinformation episode matters because it distorts public understanding of labor actions and their consequences. The WGA and SAG-AFTRA strikes were serious efforts by workers to secure better conditions in an industry undergoing rapid transformation. The false claim about "The View" being cancelled because of the strike subtly reinforced a narrative that labor actions are destructive — that they kill jobs, end shows, and harm the very industry they seek to improve. This narrative is not only false but actively harmful to public support for workers' rights. Every time a strike is blamed for a cancellation that did not actually occur, it chips away at the solidarity that labor actions depend on.

Finally, the episode serves as a case study in the evolution of misinformation production. The Just In channel is not a sophisticated disinformation operation directed by a hostile state actor; it is a small-scale content producer operating in the gray zone between entertainment journalism and rumor-mongering. The fact that such an operation can produce a video that reaches hundreds of thousands of viewers and generates significant social media distribution illustrates that the infrastructure of misinformation is not limited to grand conspiracies. It is also — perhaps primarily — a mundane commercial enterprise, driven by the same algorithmic incentives that shape all content production on platform-mediated networks.

## 5. Research Notes

The Snopes article by Izz Scott LaMagdeleine, published August 11, 2023, serves as the primary reference for this Truth Vault entry, along with the sources it cites. The Wrap's reporting, by Andi Ortiz and published August 7, 2023, is the key evidentiary source for the fact that "The View" was on a scheduled break after its season ended on August 4. The Wrap article is a straightforward piece of entertainment reporting that answers the question its headline poses: "Here's Why 'The View' Isn't On For the Rest of August."

Deadline's coverage of the WGA strike's hundredth day, by Peter White and Rosy Cordero, published August 9, 2023, provides important context about the strike's duration and the state of negotiations. The Los Angeles Times coverage of SAG-AFTRA's position, by Wendy Lee, published August 10, 2023, adds the dimension of the simultaneous actors' strike.

Whoopi Goldberg's on-air explanation of the Network Code contract, available via the official "The View" YouTube channel in a video posted July 17, 2023, is a crucial primary source. In this video, Goldberg explains that the show continues production during the SAG-AFTRA strike because it operates under "a different kind of contract, which is called the network code." She explicitly expresses support for striking workers while clarifying why her show is not shut down.

The connection to previous Snopes fact-checking of the Just In channel is established through Jordan Liles's article from August 7, 2023, which debunks a false claim about Whoopi Goldberg losing everything in a Jason Aldean lawsuit. This establishes a pattern of behavior from the same YouTube channel.

Missing from the Snopes article and from the sources it cites is any analysis of the Just In channel's broader content strategy, its subscriber count and growth trajectory, the demographic composition of its audience, or the role of YouTube's algorithmic recommendations in its reach. Also absent is any examination of how the Facebook, Twitter, and TikTok shares of the claim were distributed, whether they originated from the same accounts, and how the platform's content moderation systems (or lack thereof) handled the false claim.

Further research could usefully examine the Network Code contract in greater detail, including its history, its scope, and the labor politics surrounding its use during strikes. The Network Code was originally negotiated between the networks and the unions representing performers and other workers, and its provisions for continued production during strikes have been the subject of debate within the labor movement. Understanding this context would illuminate why Goldberg felt compelled to explain the arrangement to viewers and why the false cancellation narrative may have resonated with audiences unfamiliar with the arcane details of television labor contracts.

## 6. Verdict

The claim that "The View" was cancelled because of the WGA strike is false. The show was not cancelled. It was on a planned seasonal break after concluding its regular season on August 4, 2023. The WGA strike and the simultaneous SAG-AFTRA strike were real and consequential labor actions that affected much of the entertainment industry, but "The View" continued production during the strikes under a Network Code contract, a fact that host Whoopi Goldberg publicly explained to viewers.

Snopes's fact-check correctly identifies the claim as false and provides adequate supporting evidence. However, the Snopes treatment is narrow in scope, focusing on the factual inaccuracy without exploring the deeper dynamics — the labor context, the media ecosystem, the algorithmic amplification, the public's ambivalent relationship with "The View," and the pattern of entertainment misinformation — that made the false claim credible and consequential.

Beyond its factual falseness, the claim matters because it parasitically attached itself to genuine labor disruption, distorted public understanding of the strikes, revealed the structural vulnerabilities of platform-mediated information, and illustrated the limits of fact-checking as a standalone response to misinformation. The episode is a small but instructive case study in how falsehoods about entertainment can serve as vectors for broader misunderstandings about labor, media, and institutional trust.

The most important correction this Truth Vault entry offers is not simply that the claim was false, but that the truth it obscured — about the realities of the WGA strike, the economics of daytime television, the contractual arrangements that govern broadcast production, and the algorithmic dynamics of platform distribution — is far more interesting and important than the fiction that briefly captured public attention. Understanding why the false claim spread, and what genuine anxieties it exploited, is ultimately more valuable than knowing that it was wrong.
