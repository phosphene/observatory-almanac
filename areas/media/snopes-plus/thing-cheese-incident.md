# Kraft Cheese Won't Melt?


## 1. The Claim

In December 2014, a rumour began circulating on social media claiming that Kraft brand American cheese singles do not melt, even when exposed to high heat. The claim was accompanied by and generated by a proliferating series of YouTube and Facebook videos in which people attempted to melt a slice of Kraft cheese using a lighter, a candle, or a torch, only to observe that the slice bubbled, browned, charred at the edges, but did not melt into the liquid pool that intuition suggested it should.

The visual effect was genuinely surprising to many viewers. Given that Kraft cheese singles are a ubiquitous ingredient in grilled cheese sandwiches, burgers, and macaroni and cheese — preparations in which cheese is presumed to and does melt — the observation of a slice apparently resisting flame required explanation. Various explanations were proposed in the comments sections of viral videos. The most alarming, and most frequently shared, concluded that the cheese must not be "real" cheese or must contain plastic or synthetic materials. One widely circulated clip appended the observation "and we wonder why cancer is on the rise" to footage of the apparently unmelting slice, explicitly linking the unusual behaviour to cancer risk without specifying any mechanism.

Consumer emails to Kraft and social media interactions multiplied. A Kraft social media representative responded both individually and through a general corporate statement, and Kraft subsequently released a YouTube video featuring two Kraft food scientists explaining the emulsification chemistry of cheese singles and demonstrating that, under normal cooking conditions (a frying pan, standard stovetop heat), the cheese did in fact melt normally.

Kim LaCapria's Snopes investigation, published 8 January 2015 and updated 4 June 2021, rated the claim False, noting that while Kraft singles do resist melting under certain specific high-heat conditions (direct flame, very high temperatures), they melt normally and as expected under standard cooking conditions, and that the behaviour in the videos is explained by legitimate food chemistry rather than by any dangerous or non-food ingredient.


## 2. What's Actually True

Kraft American cheese singles do melt. This is not a contested empirical observation: the product has been a standard ingredient in preparations requiring melted cheese for decades, and the lived experience of millions of consumers using it in grilled cheese sandwiches and cheeseburgers is an overwhelming body of testimonial evidence for its meltability under ordinary cooking conditions.

What is true is that Kraft singles behave differently than natural cheeses under some specific heating conditions, particularly under direct intense flame. A natural cheese like cheddar, mozzarella, or brie will typically melt into liquid flow from solid relatively quickly when heated, because the protein matrix (primarily casein networks) destabilises as temperature rises, releasing the fat phase as a liquid drop. This is the characteristic "greasy melt" familiar from pizzas and cheeseburgers made with cheddar.

Kraft singles — and processed cheese products generally — contain emulsifying salts: sodium phosphate and sodium citrate are the principal agents in Kraft singles. These compounds function by sequestering calcium ions in the casein protein network, disrupting the natural protein-protein interactions, and creating a more stable, homogeneous emulsified structure in which fat and protein are bound more tightly together. The Kraft response to consumer concern stated this explicitly: "KRAFT Singles are made with emulsifiers — an ingredient commonly used in products such as chocolate and salad dressings — which hold the fat and protein together. In fact, the fat and protein are bound so tight that they don't separate even at high heat levels, like an open flame."

The consequence of this emulsion structure is that Kraft singles require "lower, longer heat for the cheese particles to melt and flow," as the Kraft statement described. A direct lighter flame provides extremely high temperature concentrated in a small area for a brief time — the opposite of the low-and-slow heat profile that the emulsified structure requires. The result is browning and charring at the point of flame contact, protein denaturation at the surface, and the visual impression that the cheese is refusing to melt, when in fact it is simply undergoing a different thermal transformation profile than the viral video's audience expected.

This is normal food chemistry, and none of the ingredients involved are non-food substances. Sodium phosphate and sodium citrate are FDA-approved food additives widely used across the food industry. The cancer claim appended to some videos has no scientific basis — there is no established carcinogenicity for these emulsifiers at food-use concentrations.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty

The Kraft cheese melting case is an illuminating example of what philosophers of science might call a *background theory dependence* problem in public empirical reasoning. The people producing and sharing the "cheese that won't melt" videos were conducting what appeared to them to be a straightforward empirical test: apply fire to cheese; observe whether it melts; use the result as evidence about the cheese's composition. This is a reasonable scientific logic in principle. The problem is that the test's validity as evidence depends entirely on background theories about thermodynamics and food chemistry that most observers do not possess.

The interpretation "the cheese doesn't melt because it contains plastic" assumes that natural cheese and Kraft single have the same thermal response profile, that failure to melt under direct flame indicates non-food composition, and that the relevant question is about composition rather than about processing. All three assumptions are wrong. Two cheeses with identical dairy compositions but different processing histories (one natural, one emulsified) will exhibit different thermal behaviour. The lighter test distinguishes emulsified from non-emulsified cheeses, not cheese from non-cheese.

This is an instance of a broader epistemic problem: empirical tests designed without adequate background knowledge can produce genuine observations that support entirely false conclusions. The observers were not lying or fabricating — they genuinely observed a difference in melting behaviour. But without the background knowledge to interpret that observation correctly, they generated false inferences. Hanson (1958, *Patterns of Discovery*, Cambridge University Press) described this phenomenon as the "theory-ladenness of observation": what we perceive in an experiment is structured by the theoretical frameworks we bring to it, and inadequate theory produces systematically distorted observation even from genuine empirical engagement.

A second epistemic novelty is the specific way in which the claim exploited *intuitive chemistry* — the folk-physical understanding that "real" food melts and "plastic" doesn't. This intuition has genuine utility in many everyday contexts: you can tell that something is plastic rather than cheese by its failure to melt at temperatures that melt cheese. But the intuition fails to account for the fact that food chemistry can engineer intermediate states — emulsified products that are genuinely food, genuinely dairy-derived, but that exhibit the thermal resistance intuition associates with plastic, not because they ARE plastic, but because emulsification fundamentally changes the thermal response of protein-fat systems.

The cancer connection invoked in some viral versions of the claim is particularly revealing. The move from "behaves unexpectedly" to "causes cancer" is not a logical inference but an affective association: within a cultural framework that treats "unnatural" industrial food as inherently dangerous and equates unexpected properties with health risk, the chain runs: unusual behaviour → industrial processing → "chemicals" → cancer. Slovic (2010, *The Feeling of Risk*, Earthscan) documented the systemic differences between expert and lay risk perception, showing that lay risk judgements are primarily driven by affect (fear responses) and secondarily by numerical magnitude, whereas expert judgements are more heavily weighted toward epidemiological evidence and dose-response relationships. The Kraft cheese cancer association is a textbook affect-heuristic risk assessment with no epidemiological grounding.


## 4. The Scientific and Regulatory Record

Processed cheese chemistry is well documented in the food science literature. The foundational work on processed cheese manufacture and its technological properties was established across the mid-twentieth century (Caric & Kalab, 1987, *Food Microstructure*) and has been substantially extended by modern structural studies using cryo-electron microscopy and rheological characterisation.

The key technology in processed cheese is the use of emulsifying salts — primarily sodium orthophosphates and citrates — to disrupt calcium-calcium crosslinks in the casein protein network that gives natural cheese its characteristic texture. When emulsifying salts are added during the heating process used to manufacture processed cheese, the calcium ions are chelated (bound by the citrate or phosphate anions), freeing the casein chains to reassemble into a more uniform, homogeneous gel network that incorporates the fat phase as a stable emulsion (Guinee et al., 2004, *Dairy Science and Technology*, 84(3), 197–230). Upon cooling, this network forms the characteristic smooth, sliceable texture of American cheese singles.

This structural difference has known consequences for thermal response. Marchetti et al. (2013, *Food Chemistry*) characterised the rheological behaviour of processed American-type cheeses under controlled heating conditions and found that processed cheese exhibited substantially higher apparent viscosity and greater resistance to flow at temperatures below 80°C compared to equivalent natural cheeses. Above approximately 80–90°C with extended exposure, processed cheese also melts to a homogeneous flow, which is precisely what Kraft's own demonstration video showed. The threshold for observed melting under normal cooking conditions (frying pan temperatures of approximately 150–180°C with indirect heat through bread or other food) is routinely achieved, explaining the consistent culinary utility of the product.

The FDA has conducted extensive safety review of sodium phosphate and sodium citrate as direct food additives. Both are listed under Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) provisions in 21 CFR § 182.1778 (sodium phosphates) and 21 CFR § 184.1751 (sodium citrate). The scientific consensus on their safety at food-use levels is well established. Dietary phosphate intake from all food sources, including natural phosphates in dairy and meat products as well as added phosphate salts, has been studied in the context of kidney health for patients with chronic renal disease — a specific population where phosphate management is clinically important — but this has not generated any concern about processed cheese phosphate levels for the general healthy population (Kalantar-Zadeh et al., 2010, *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, 91(1), 128S–138S). The IARC has not assessed sodium phosphate or sodium citrate as carcinogens; neither are classified as cancer-relevant by the National Toxicology Program.

The concept of "fake" or "imitation" cheese has a specific legal meaning under US food regulations. The FDA's Standards of Identity for cheese and cheese products (21 CFR Part 133) define the compositional and processing requirements for various cheese categories. "Pasteurised process cheese" (the category into which Kraft singles fall) must be manufactured from natural cheese with permitted emulsifying agents and other specified ingredients; it must have a minimum moisture content and maximum fat content specified by regulation. "Imitation cheese" is a distinct category for products that do not meet these composition standards. Kraft singles are formulated to meet the pasteurised process cheese standard, not the imitation cheese standard — they are made primarily from natural cheese, modified by an authorised processing method (Kraft Corporate, product labelling compliance with 21 CFR § 133.169).


## 5. Verdict Analysis

Snopes' "False" verdict is correct. The claim that Kraft cheese "won't melt, even at high temperatures" is directly falsified by the product's widely documented and experienced culinary behaviour — it melts in every standard cooking application for which it is used. The qualification "even at high temperatures" is additionally false in the absolute sense, as even emulsified processed cheese reaches melt-flow state at sufficiently elevated temperatures with extended exposure.

The viral video test — lighter-to-cheese — is a valid observation of some difference in thermal behaviour between emulsified and natural cheese, but the inference drawn ("this means the product contains plastic or is dangerous") does not follow from the observation and is contradicted by the established food chemistry of emulsified cheese products.

LaCapria's Snopes analysis correctly identifies the key logical error: "One odd aspect of the claim was its juxtaposition with a commonly-lived experience in the United States: Kraft cheese singles are a very popular selection for grilled cheese sandwiches and cheeseburgers, and most people have not only observed Kraft cheese melting, but have also consumed many prepared dishes that featured melted Kraft cheese." This is a particularly clean refutation: the claim is not just theoretically wrong, it contradicts overwhelming direct experience that the claimants themselves have likely had. The persistence of the claim despite this obvious contradiction illustrates how strongly the affect-heuristic response (fear generated by unexpected behaviour) can override experiential counter-evidence in certain circumstances.

The gap category assignment of "misleading implication" reflects the structural feature of the claim: the underlying observation (the lighter test shows resistance to direct flame) is real and reproducible, but the claim as framed implies a broader and false conclusion (the product will not melt under any conditions and contains non-food substances). Claims of this type are challenging to address precisely because they have a genuine empirical nucleus that lends them credibility even though the conclusion they support is false.


## 6. Implications

The Kraft cheese case has implications that extend into food communication, regulatory transparency, and public food chemistry literacy. Most immediately, it illustrates the value of Kraft's dual-track response: a direct individual response explaining the chemistry to concerned consumers, and a produced video featuring food scientists demonstrating normal melting behaviour. This response model — engaging consumers where they are (social media), providing accessible scientific explanation, and using visual demonstration rather than abstract denial — represents good-faith corporate food communication. The fact that the response did not fully suppress the claim's circulation is not evidence of its inadequacy but of the structural power of viral sharing networks to sustain alarming claims even against good-faith correction.

More broadly, the case points to the need for food chemistry education as a component of general scientific literacy. The inability to reason correctly about why an emulsified food product behaves differently from its non-emulsified equivalent is not a trivial gap — it is the kind of gap that makes people systematically susceptible to food-fear misinformation. As processed and formulated foods constitute an increasing proportion of the food supply globally, the inability to interpret ingredient labels or understand the functional chemistry of food additives leaves consumers dependent on alarming viral claims for their understanding of what they eat.

The intersection with food additive safety communication is also significant. Processed cheese emulsifiers have a safety record spanning decades of mass consumption with no identified adverse health effects for the general population. The specific anxiety about phosphate emulsifiers is scientifically coherent as a clinical concern for kidney disease patients, but this context-dependent concern has been stripped of its clinical specificity and amplified as a general population risk that the science does not support. This pattern — taking a specific, conditional risk relevant to a vulnerable clinical subpopulation and generalising it as a universal consumer health threat — is a recurring feature of food processing misinformation (Nestle, 2002, *Food Politics*).

For home cooks, the practical implication is equally clear: Kraft American cheese singles are appropriate for any application where melted cheese is desired, including grilled cheese sandwiches, burgers, fondue applications requiring stable emulsion (where the emulsifying properties of processed cheese are actually advantageous), and any other standard culinary use. The lighter test demonstrates a real property of the product — it responds to heat differently than natural cheese — but this property is a feature, not a defect, and does not indicate anything about the product's safety or non-food composition.
