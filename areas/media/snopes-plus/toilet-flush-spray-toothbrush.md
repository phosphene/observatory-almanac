# Flushing the toilet sprays bacteria onto your toothbrush


## 1. The Claim

The claim is stated with varying degrees of alarm: flushing a toilet launches a spray of microscopic bacterial particles into the surrounding air; these particles travel to the toothbrush sitting open on the bathroom counter; and as a consequence, every time you brush your teeth, you are placing a fecal-bacteria-colonised implement in your mouth. The extreme version — promoted by anxiety-generating content in popular media and occasionally in school science demonstrations — frames this as a serious, ongoing, and actionable health threat that justifies either storing toothbrushes in closed containers, moving them outside the bathroom, closing the toilet lid before flushing, or purchasing toothbrush sanitising devices.

The claim traces partly to legitimate microbiological research and partly to the amplification dynamics of health scare journalism, which reliably generates engagement from the visceral disgust response triggered by fecal contamination imagery. It recurs periodically in popular media — Mythbusters addressed it in 2004, various broadcast news features have revisited it, and it circulates in social media posts citing the underlying research, usually without the qualifications the original researchers included.

The accurate kernel: there is genuine scientific documentation that toilet flushing produces an aerosol event (the "toilet plume"), that this plume carries microbial particles, that these particles can be detected in the surrounding bathroom environment, and that toothbrushes stored in bathrooms contain fecal indicator bacteria. This is established, replicated science.

The problematic inference: that this contamination event represents a meaningful hygiene risk to the toothbrush user, that closing the toilet lid eliminates the problem, or that toothbrush contamination from this source is meaningfully different from the general microbial environment of bathroom surfaces and ambient air.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The toilet plume is real.** The aerosol generation event associated with toilet flushing has been documented and characterised in peer-reviewed literature. Gerba, Wallis, and Melnick (1975, *Applied Microbiology*) conducted foundational research demonstrating that flushing a toilet generates an aerosol plume that can project microbial particles at least 1.8 metres (6 feet) from the fixture, with particles remaining airborne for extended periods. This research, published in a credible and specialised microbiology journal, established the empirical foundation for the claim and has been replicated in subsequent work.

Johnson et al. (2013, *Journal of Applied Microbiology*) produced a frequently cited study examining toilet plume aerosol generation in more detail, measuring particle size distribution, trajectory, and microbial load. They found that a single flush generates thousands of aerosol particles, with the smallest particles (under 5 micrometres) remaining airborne for six minutes or more. Microbial analysis confirmed that fecal bacteria and viruses could be recovered from these aerosol particles.

Reynolds et al. (2011, *Infection Control and Hospital Epidemiology*) addressed the health care context specifically, demonstrating that aerosolisation from toilet flushing can contribute to environmental contamination with *Clostridium difficile* in hospital settings — a finding with genuine clinical significance in a high-risk environment.

**Toothbrushes do contain fecal bacteria.** Abramson and Parmet (2012, *Journal of Dental Research*) and several earlier studies have documented that toothbrushes stored in bathrooms contain fecal coliform bacteria — specifically, bacteria of the type used as indicators of fecal contamination (principally coliform bacteria and enterococci). The American Dental Association's own guidance acknowledges that stored toothbrushes can harbour bacteria, though it frames this in terms of general microbiological good hygiene rather than toilet plume contamination.

Verran et al. (2010, *Biofouling*) examined bacterial colonisation of toothbrushes comprehensively and found that toothbrushes accumulate a complex microbiome including organisms from the oral cavity, from handler hands, from the bathroom environment, and from ambient air. The presence of fecal indicator bacteria on toothbrushes is well-documented; the attribution of that contamination predominantly to toilet plumes rather than other sources is much less secure.

**Where the claim overstates.** The research demonstrates aerosol generation and microbial deposition but does not support the claim that toilet-flush aerosolisation represents a clinically significant individual health risk for toothbrush users. Several lines of evidence qualify the alarm:

*Ambient bathroom microbiology.* Bathroom air contains fecal indicator bacteria as a baseline condition entirely independent of flush events. Studies of indoor air quality in domestic bathrooms, including work by Hambraeus (1988, *Journal of Hospital Infection*) and subsequent researchers, have documented continuous baseline fecal bacterial presence on bathroom surfaces and in bathroom air attributable to normal occupancy, incomplete hand hygiene, and the general dispersal dynamics of bathroom use. Toilet plume events add to this baseline, but they do not create a microbially unique hazard from a background of cleanliness.

*Lid-closure does not eliminate the plume.* A frequently cited intervention recommendation — close the toilet lid before flushing — is partially but not fully effective. Closing the lid reduces but does not eliminate aerosol generation. Lid-flush mechanics in household toilets mean that considerable turbulence occurs at the lid-seat interface during and after flushing. Significant particle generation occurs before lid deployment is practically effective. Johnson et al. (2013) noted that lid closure reduced aerosol generation but did not eliminate it, and that aerosol particles generated during pre-lid-closure flushing still dispersed into the room.

*The toothbrush is a high microbial load environment regardless.* Every toothbrush used by a typical adult is an object inserted repeatedly into an oral cavity containing hundreds of species of bacteria in concentrations of 10^8 to 10^10 organisms per millilitre of saliva (Dewhirst et al., 2010, *Journal of Bacteriology*). The oral microbiome includes organisms that are by conventional standards considerably more likely to cause disease in immunocompromised individuals than the fecal coliforms indexed in toothbrush contamination studies. The specific public health concern that toilet-plume fecal bacteria are meaningfully adding to the risk of an implement already coated in oral Flora involves a claim about marginal risk that has not been established.

*No documented clinical cases.* Despite decades of research documenting toilet plume aerosols and fecal bacterial toothbrush colonisation, there is no published case series, case-control study, or epidemiological investigation documenting clinically significant infections attributable to toilet-plume toothbrush contamination in healthy individuals. The absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, but decades of use of toothbrushes in shared bathrooms without documented disease clustering significantly limits the plausibility of a substantial individual clinical risk.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Disgust responses short-circuit risk calibration.** The claim activates the disgust emotion through multiple pathways simultaneously: fecal material, invisible aerosol contamination, oral contact with a contaminated object. Disgust is an evolved emotional response associated with pathogen avoidance (Curtis, de Barra, and Aunger, 2011, *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*), and it reliably produces risk overestimation in experimental conditions. Research by Rozin et al. (1999, *Psychological Science*) established that disgust-eliciting scenarios are perceived as more dangerous than non-disgust scenarios of equal or greater actual risk, and that disgust is remarkably refractory to factual correction — knowing that a contamination source is insignificant does not reliably reduce the disgust response.

The toilet-toothbrush claim is close to an optimal disgust trigger: it involves fecal matter, it involves invisible microbial agents, and it implicates an object placed in the mouth — a triple conjunction of disgust-significant features. The claim spreads and persists not primarily because it is well-supported by evidence but because it reliably activates a strong affective response that overrides quantitative risk assessment.

**The epistemological novelty: invisible ongoing exposure creates asymmetric credibility.** The claim benefits from a specific epistemic asymmetry that makes it difficult to correct through ordinary reassurance. When a risk is visible and discrete, people can observe its absence and update their beliefs accordingly. When a risk is framed as invisible and ongoing — microbial particles drifting through the air at all times — there is no observable evidence that could disconfirm it. The absence of illness could always be explained away as luck or immune resilience rather than absence of risk. This unfalsifiability-in-practice gives the claim unusual staying power compared to claims that can be tested through direct observation.

Furthermore, the underlying science is *genuinely true* at the level of mechanism — the aerosol is real, the bacteria are real, the deposition on surfaces is real — and the claim exploits this mechanistic accuracy to license a risk magnitude inference that the evidence does not support. This is a pattern common to many health scares: the mechanism is real, the effect is documented, and the alarm derives from pattern-matching to the mechanism without quantitative anchoring.

**Media incentive structures.** Health scare stories involving bathrooms, contamination, and everyday objects perform predictably well in broadcast and digital media environments. The Mythbusters 2004 episode on toothbrush contamination reached a large audience and framed the result in terms of contamination documentation rather than risk qualification. Subsequent media coverage tends to cite the contamination finding without the quantitative context, and the result propagates through the media ecosystem as a validated hygiene concern.

The Reeves and Nass (1996) media equation framework explains part of this: audiences do not effectively distinguish between a finding that "bacteria can be detected" and a finding that "bacteria present a substantive health threat." The confirmation of presence is treated as confirmation of threat, and the media presentation format — which emphasises the dramatic detection over the quantitative risk assessment — supports this elision.


## 4. Verdict

**EARC Rating: A — Accurate, But Sensationalised**

The claim is **mostly true at the mechanistic level and misleading at the risk level.** Toilet flushing does produce aerosol events. Microbial particles from those events do deposit on bathroom surfaces including toothbrushes. Toothbrushes stored in bathrooms do contain fecal indicator bacteria. These are empirically established facts.

The framing that makes the claim a health alarm — that this constitutes a meaningful, ongoing, actionable cleanliness risk specifically from toilet-flush contamination — is not supported by the evidence. The contamination is real; the clinical significance is negligible for healthy individuals in typical domestic contexts; and the specific attribution of toothbrush contamination to toilet flush events rather than other ambient bathroom microbiology sources is not adequately established.

The claim would be rated **Mostly True** by typical fact-checking standards when framed as "toilet flushing sends bacteria into the air," with the important qualification that this does not represent a demonstrable health risk under normal conditions.

**Snopes status:** The specific claim has not been formally addressed in a dedicated Snopes entry, though adjacent bathroom hygiene claims (whether toilet seats are dirtier than cutting boards, whether phones are dirtier than toilets) have received treatment. The toothbrush claim represents a gap — it is sufficiently specific, widely circulated, and empirically adjudicatable to merit formal treatment.

**Practical guidance:** The ADA recommendation to rinse toothbrushes thoroughly after use, store them upright in an open position (not in closed containers that encourage moisture and bacterial growth), and replace them every three to four months represents appropriate evidence-based guidance that does not rely on toilet plume fear. Closing the toilet lid before flushing is a reasonable hygienic practice but should not be understood as eliminating aerosol risk or as the primary determinant of toothbrush safety.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**The toilet plume in health care settings.** While the individual domestic toilet plume claim is overstated, the same underlying mechanism has genuine clinical relevance in health care environments. Best et al. (2012, *Journal of Hospital Infection*) investigated whether *Clostridium difficile* contamination in hospital bathroom environments was attributable to aerosolisation from toilet flushing, finding evidence that flushing events generated C. difficile-containing aerosols that contributed to ward surface contamination. In contexts where patients include immunocompromised individuals, individuals on antibiotic therapy, or individuals with C. difficile infection, toilet plume dynamics are a legitimate infection control consideration.

The discrepancy between the negligible risk in a healthy domestic context and the genuine consideration in health care settings illustrates a broader principle: microbial contamination risk is population-dependent and context-dependent. The same microbial load represents different risks depending on host immune status, baseline pathogen exposure, and the specific organisms involved. Broadcasting health care infection control concerns to a general healthy population without adjustment for population risk produces systematic risk overestimation.

**Aerobiology and the general contamination baseline.** The toilet plume claim sits within the broader field of aerobiology — the study of airborne biological particles. Research in this field consistently documents that indoor environments contain continuous, complex microbial aerosols from dozens of sources: occupant skin shedding, respiratory exhalation, clothing, pet dander, HVAC systems, and resuspension from floor surfaces. The specific contribution of flush events to this broad baseline has not been established as quantitatively dominant. Research by Hospodsky et al. (2012, *PLOS ONE*) profiling indoor air microbiomes found high diversity and abundance of microorganisms in domestic indoor environments attributable primarily to occupant and pet-associated sources rather than any single fixture-associated event.

**Consumer product markets.** The toilet plume claim has generated a notable consumer product category: toothbrush sanitizers, toothbrush covers, medicine cabinet enclosed toothbrush storage, and UV toothbrush sanitizing devices. These products market directly against toilet plume contamination anxiety. The UV sanitizing devices in particular are marketed with claims about killing 99.9% of bacteria — claims that may be accurate in laboratory conditions but have not been consistently validated in clinical studies of infection prevention outcomes.

The FDA has not approved UV toothbrush sanitizers as medical devices for infection prevention purposes, classifying them as cosmetic products. The FTC has taken limited enforcement action against specific misleading claims but the category continues to generate substantial consumer spending driven partly by toilet plume anxiety.

**The comparison with hand hygiene.** The focus on toilet plume toothbrush contamination consumes cognitive and behavioral resources that, if directed toward evidence-based hygiene behaviors, would produce substantially larger health returns. Hand hygiene research — including foundational work by Semmelweis in the 1840s, Curtis et al. (2009, *American Journal of Public Health*), and the WHO multimodal hand hygiene campaign evidence base — consistently demonstrates that handwashing is among the highest-return individual hygiene behaviors available. The toilet plume risk, if it exists at all in clinical terms for healthy individuals, is orders of magnitude smaller than the documented risk reduction available from consistent handwashing, yet the toilet plume claim receives substantially more popular media attention than hand hygiene compliance does.

This is a general feature of health misinformation ecosystems: viscerally interesting but low-consequence claims crowd out less viscerally compelling but higher-consequence accurate guidance.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Fact-checking coverage of bathroom contamination claims has addressed related but not identical claims. Snopes has addressed whether toilet seats are dirtier than kitchen cutting boards (finding the claim technically supportable but misleadingly contextualised) and whether phones are dirtier than toilets (a common claim with similar accuracy-without-relevance features). The toilet-flush-toothbrush specific claim has not received dedicated Snopes treatment.

Where individual fact-checkers and science communication outlets have addressed the claim — including Scienceline, HealthLine, and various university extension resources — the consistent assessment is: the contamination is real; the health alarm is disproportionate; close the lid if you prefer but don't panic. This consensus among science communicators has not penetrated popular media coverage sufficiently to suppress recirculation.

Mythbusters' 2004 treatment remains the most widely cited popular examination. Adam Savage and Jamie Hyneman documented bacterial contamination of toothbrushes in bathroom settings and briefly noted that control toothbrushes stored outside the bathroom also showed bacterial contamination from ambient sources — a finding that qualifies the toilet-plume-specific interpretation, though this qualification was not the takeaway encoded in audience memory or subsequent citations.

If subjected to formal Snopes treatment, the most accurate verdict structure would be **Mostly True** with the qualifier that the health significance is negligible for healthy individuals and that the contamination source is not uniquely attributable to toilet plume events. The claim's continued media circulation without this qualification represents a gap in fact-checking coverage of a widely shared and demonstrably anxiety-generating health misconception.
