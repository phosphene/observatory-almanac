# "Toilet Paper Was Invented in America"


## 1. The Claim

The assertion that toilet paper was invented in America represents a particularly clear example of how invention narratives can be shaped by Western-centric historical perspectives that conflate commercialization with innovation. In its most common formulation, the claim credits Joseph Gayetty with inventing toilet paper in 1857 when he began selling "Gayetty's Medicated Paper" in New York, or alternatively credits the Scott Paper Company with the invention when they began mass-producing toilet tissue in 1879. More sophisticated versions of the claim acknowledge that paper had been used for hygiene in other cultures but maintain that "modern" toilet paper—perforated, rolled, or commercially produced—is an American invention.

This belief appears across multiple contexts with varying degrees of specificity and cultural chauvinism. In casual conversations about American innovation, toilet paper often appears alongside inventions like the light bulb, the airplane, or the telephone as examples of American ingenuity improving daily life worldwide. In more formal historical accounts, the claim typically focuses on the industrial and commercial innovations that made toilet paper widely available, treating these developments as tantamount to invention. Educational materials frequently present Gayetty or the Scott brothers as toilet paper's inventors without acknowledging prior art from other cultures, creating a narrative of American innovation solving a universal human problem.

The claim gains plausibility from several factors that characterize many invention myths. First, it focuses on the version of the technology most familiar to contemporary users—commercially produced, mass-marketed toilet paper does indeed have American origins. Second, it reflects the historical reality that American companies were primarily responsible for transforming toilet paper from a luxury item into a ubiquitous household necessity. Third, it operates within broader narratives about American technological leadership during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, when American companies did pioneer many consumer products and manufacturing processes.

However, the claim also reflects deeper epistemological problems about how invention is defined and how historical credit is assigned. By defining "invention" as commercial production rather than initial innovation, the narrative erases over a millennium of prior development in other cultures. By focusing on the technological forms most familiar to Western users, it treats cultural and technological context as more important than chronological priority. Most fundamentally, by failing to distinguish between invention and commercialization, it perpetuates Western-centric historical narratives that systematically undervalue non-Western innovation.

The stakes extend beyond historical accuracy to broader questions about how technological development is understood and credited. If toilet paper "invention" can be attributed to its American commercializers despite Chinese origins dating back over 1,200 years, similar logic can be applied to countless other technologies where non-Western innovation was later commercialized or refined by Western companies. The toilet paper case thus serves as a paradigm for examining how invention narratives are constructed and what assumptions about cultural priority and technological legitimacy they embed.


## 2. What's Actually True

The historical record on toilet paper development reveals a clear chronological sequence that contradicts American invention claims while illustrating how commercial innovation can be mistaken for original invention. The evidence divides into three distinct phases: ancient Chinese paper hygiene, global pre-modern hygiene practices, and modern commercial toilet paper development.

The Chinese origins are historically well-documented and substantially predate any American developments. The earliest recorded reference to paper being used for toilet purposes appears in the writings of Yan Zhitui (531-591 CE), a Chinese scholar who mentioned the practice of using paper for personal hygiene in his family instructions. By the Tang Dynasty (618-907 CE), paper production had advanced sufficiently that paper hygiene was becoming more widespread among educated classes. The most compelling evidence comes from the Yuan Dynasty (1271-1368), where imperial court records document the production of specialized toilet paper for the royal family. These documents describe paper manufactured specifically for hygiene purposes, cut to standard sizes (approximately 2 feet by 3 feet), and even perfumed for enhanced comfort.

The scale of Chinese paper hygiene by the medieval period is remarkable and completely absent from American invention narratives. Arab traveler accounts from the 9th and 10th centuries describe widespread use of paper for personal hygiene in Chinese cities, treating it as a notable but established practice rather than a recent innovation. By the 14th century, Chinese imperial workshops were producing over 720,000 sheets annually of specialized toilet paper for the imperial court alone, suggesting sophisticated manufacturing processes and quality control that predated American commercial toilet paper by over 500 years.

The global context of pre-modern hygiene practices provides important perspective on why paper-based solutions emerged in China specifically. Romans used communal sponges attached to sticks (tersorium), shared among users in public latrines. Greeks used pottery fragments (ostraka), often inscribed with the names of political enemies as a form of symbolic revenge. Medieval Europeans used a variety of materials including wool, hay, leaves, or corn cobs depending on social class and regional availability. Colonial Americans similarly relied on corn cobs, leaves, or pages from almanacs and catalogs. The Chinese adoption of paper for hygiene reflected their earlier development of papermaking technology (traditionally attributed to Cai Lun around 105 CE) and their cultural emphasis on cleanliness and personal comfort.

The American developments that generate invention claims occurred within this broader historical context and represented commercial innovation rather than technological breakthrough. Joseph Gayetty's 1857 "Medicated Paper" was indeed the first commercially marketed toilet paper in the United States, but it was explicitly modeled on existing practices and failed commercially due to high costs and cultural resistance. Gayetty's innovation was primarily marketing-related—packaging paper specifically for toilet use and adding aloe for supposed medicinal benefits—rather than representing fundamental technological advance.

The Scott Paper Company's contributions, beginning in 1879, were more significant but remained focused on manufacturing and distribution rather than invention. The Scott brothers developed processes for mass-producing toilet tissue, created the perforated roll format that became standard, and established distribution networks that made toilet paper widely available. These were genuine innovations in industrial manufacturing and marketing, but they built upon centuries of established paper hygiene practices rather than creating an new technology.

Seth Wheeler's 1891 patent for perforated toilet paper rolls represents perhaps the strongest American claim to toilet paper innovation, as the roll format with perforations for easy tearing became the global standard. However, Wheeler's patent covered a specific improvement to existing toilet paper rather than the invention of toilet paper itself—a distinction that is crucial for accurate attribution of technological development.


The toilet paper case illustrates how invention narratives can embody implicit assumptions about technological legitimacy that systematically bias historical understanding toward Western commercial contexts while erasing non-Western innovation. The American invention claim is false in any reasonable interpretation of "invention" as original development, but it persists because it operates within cultural frameworks that treat commercialization as more significant than innovation and Western technological forms as more legitimate than non-Western precedents.

The temporal dimension is particularly stark and difficult to rationalize within standard invention criteria. Chinese court records document specialized toilet paper production over 500 years before any American commercial activity, using sophisticated manufacturing processes that produced standardized, quality-controlled products at industrial scale. The Chinese paper was specifically designed and manufactured for hygiene purposes, distributed through organized supply chains, and used by consumers who explicitly chose paper over available alternatives. By any reasonable definition of what constitutes a "product" or "technology," Chinese toilet paper qualifies fully.

The American contributions, while historically significant, represent refinements and commercialization of an existing technology rather than original invention. Joseph Gayetty's medicinal additives, the Scott brothers' mass production techniques, and Seth Wheeler's perforated rolls were genuine innovations that improved toilet paper's accessibility, affordability, and convenience. These contributions deserve recognition and credit—but as developments within an existing technological tradition, not as the creation of an new product category.

The persistence of American invention claims despite clear historical evidence of Chinese precedence reflects deeper epistemological issues about how technological development is understood and valued. The focus on recent commercial developments over ancient practical innovations suggests implicit assumptions that "real" technology must be industrially produced, commercially marketed, and integrated into modern economic systems. These assumptions systematically privilege developments that occur within familiar institutional contexts while devaluing innovations that emerge from different cultural and economic systems.

The patent-centric understanding of invention that characterizes much American technological history creates additional bias toward Western developments that were created within legal frameworks emphasizing documentation and formal protection. Chinese technological innovations, developed within different institutional contexts that did not emphasize patent protection or formal invention attribution, become invisible to analytical frameworks that prioritize documented legal claims over historical evidence of practical implementation.

The practical implications extend well beyond toilet paper to broader questions about how technological achievement is recognized and credited across cultures. If Chinese innovations can be erased from invention narratives despite clear historical precedence and sophisticated implementation, similar erasure likely affects countless other technologies where non-Western developments preceded Western commercialization. This pattern not only distorts historical understanding but also affects contemporary perceptions about which cultures have contributed to technological progress and which deserve credit for ongoing innovation.

The corrective approach requires explicit recognition that invention and commercialization represent different types of technological contribution that deserve different types of credit. Original invention—the first practical implementation of a technological solution—should be distinguished from commercial innovation—improvements in manufacturing, marketing, and distribution that make existing technologies more widely available or more effective. Both contributions are valuable and deserve recognition, but conflating them creates historical narratives that systematically misattribute technological development.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The research trajectory on toilet paper history illustrates broader patterns in how invention narratives are constructed and how Western-centric biases affect technological attribution. The evolution of scholarly understanding demonstrates clear progression from American-focused invention claims toward more comprehensive global historical perspectives.

Early twentieth-century historical accounts of toilet paper typically reflected the perspectives and available sources of their American authors. These accounts naturally focused on American commercial developments that were well-documented in business records, patent filings, and contemporary media coverage. Chinese historical sources were largely inaccessible to American historians due to language barriers, limited cultural exchange, and inadequate translation of historical documents. The result was historical scholarship that accurately described American commercial innovation while remaining ignorant of much longer Chinese precedents.

Mid-twentieth-century developments in comparative cultural history and Asian studies began to reveal the broader global context of hygiene practices, but this scholarship remained primarily within academic circles without substantially affecting popular invention narratives. Anthropological studies of hygiene practices across cultures documented the diversity of pre-modern solutions, while sinological scholarship revealed the extensive Chinese historical record of paper hygiene. However, these findings remained largely compartmentalized within specialized academic disciplines rather than integrating into mainstream technological history.

The late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries have seen more comprehensive approaches to technological history that deliberately seek to identify and correct Western-centric invention narratives. Scholars like Joseph Needham in his monumental "Science and Civilization in China" series documented countless Chinese technological innovations that preceded Western developments, providing both specific evidence about paper hygiene and broader frameworks for understanding how invention attribution has been systematically biased toward Western contexts.

Digital humanities and improved access to historical documents have further accelerated the correction of invention myths. Online databases of Chinese historical texts, improved machine translation, and collaborative scholarship between Chinese and Western historians have made previously inaccessible evidence readily available to general audiences. The result is increasingly sophisticated popular understanding of technological development that acknowledges multiple cultural contributions and avoids simplistic invention attribution.

However, the epistemological patterns revealed by toilet paper history extend far beyond this single case and illuminate systematic biases in how technological development is understood and credited. The privileging of commercial over practical innovation, the emphasis on patent documentation over historical practice, and the equation of Western technological refinement with original invention appear across countless invention narratives. These biases create distorted impressions of technological history that systematically undervalue non-Western contributions while overemphasizing Western commercial developments.

The definitional question of what constitutes "invention" proves particularly important for understanding how these biases operate. If invention requires commercial production, patent documentation, or mass market availability, then recent Western developments will systematically receive credit over earlier non-Western innovations that lacked these characteristics. If invention means first practical application or earliest documented use, then non-Western precedents become visible and Western contributions are properly understood as refinements or commercialization rather than original creation.


## 4. Verdict

**Epistemic Status**: The claim confuses commercialization with invention, erasing over 1,200 years of documented Chinese innovation while crediting American manufacturers with developing an existing technology.

**The American Invention Claim**: Toilet paper was invented in America during the 19th century.
**Evidence Grade**: F — Demonstrably false by any reasonable historical standard. Joseph Gayetty's 1857 commercial toilet paper and subsequent American developments occurred over 1,200 years after documented Chinese paper hygiene practices. American contributions represent commercialization and refinement of existing technology, not original invention.

**The Chinese Historical Record**: Paper was used for hygiene purposes in China by the 6th century CE, with specialized toilet paper production documented by the 14th century.
**Evidence Grade**: A — Extensively documented in contemporary Chinese sources, including Yan Zhitui's 6th-century writings and Yuan Dynasty imperial court records describing large-scale specialized toilet paper production (720,000+ sheets annually for the imperial court alone). Arab traveler accounts confirm widespread Chinese paper hygiene by the 9th-10th centuries.

**The American Commercial Innovation**: American companies pioneered mass production, perforated rolls, and widespread distribution of toilet paper.
**Evidence Grade**: A — Well-documented through business records and patent filings. Gayetty's 1857 commercial marketing, Scott Paper Company's mass production techniques, and Seth Wheeler's 1891 perforated roll patent represent genuine innovations in manufacturing and distribution that made toilet paper widely accessible and affordable.

**The Global Historical Context**: Various cultures developed different hygiene solutions before modern toilet paper.
**Evidence Grade**: A — Archaeological and historical evidence documents diverse pre-modern hygiene practices: Roman tersorium (communal sponges), Greek ostraka (pottery fragments), European use of wool/hay/leaves, American corn cobs and catalog pages. Chinese adoption of paper reflected their earlier papermaking technology and cultural emphasis on cleanliness.

**The Definitional Question**: Whether commercial production versus practical innovation should determine invention credit.
**Evidence Grade**: C — This involves interpretive judgments about what constitutes "invention" rather than purely factual determinations. However, standard historical practice typically credits original innovation over subsequent commercialization, making Chinese precedence the more defensible attribution under conventional invention criteria.

**Observatory Assessment**: This case exemplifies how Western-centric invention narratives systematically erase non-Western technological achievements by conflating commercial success with original innovation. The toilet paper example is particularly clear because the chronological gap is so substantial (over 1,200 years) and the Chinese evidence is so well-documented (imperial court records, contemporary writings, traveler accounts) that American invention claims cannot be sustained through any reasonable historical analysis.

The deeper pattern involves implicit assumptions about what forms of technological development deserve recognition as "real" innovation. By privileging patent documentation over historical implementation, commercial scale over practical application, and Western institutional contexts over non-Western cultural practices, invention narratives systematically undervalue non-Western contributions while overemphasizing Western refinements and commercialization.

The epistemological lesson concerns the relationship between technological diffusion and invention attribution. Most technologies develop through incremental improvements across multiple cultures and time periods. Toilet paper evolved from Chinese papermaking through medieval Chinese hygiene applications to modern American commercial production, with each stage building on previous developments. Attributing "invention" to the final commercial stage rather than the original practical innovation creates distorted historical narratives that misrepresent technological development as discrete cultural achievements rather than collaborative cross-cultural processes.

The practical implications affect contemporary understanding of global technological contributions and cultural achievement. If Chinese innovations can be erased from invention narratives despite clear historical precedence and sophisticated implementation, similar erasure likely affects countless other technologies where non-Western developments preceded Western commercialization. This pattern not only distorts historical understanding but also shapes contemporary perceptions about which cultures have contributed to technological progress.

**Updated Assessment**: Toilet paper was not invented in America. While American companies pioneered the commercial mass production and distribution systems that made toilet paper widely available, the Chinese were using paper for hygiene purposes by the 6th century CE and had developed sophisticated specialized toilet paper manufacturing by the 14th century—over 500 years before any American commercial activity. American contributions represent important innovations in manufacturing and marketing an existing technology, not the creation of an new product category.

The most accurate current statement: "Americans didn't invent toilet paper—they industrialized it. While American companies developed the mass production and distribution methods that made toilet paper a global household staple, the Chinese were manufacturing specialized toilet paper for imperial courts over 500 years before the first American commercial toilet paper appeared."

## 5. The Wider Picture

The toilet paper invention myth connects to broader patterns in how technological history is constructed and how cultural biases affect the attribution of innovation credit. These patterns extend across multiple domains and time periods, revealing systematic tendencies in how Western-centric historical narratives handle non-Western technological achievement.

The printing press provides a parallel case where Western invention claims conflict with earlier non-Western developments. Johannes Gutenberg's movable type printing press (circa 1440) is typically credited as the "invention" of printing, despite Chinese development of movable type by Bi Sheng around 1040—400 years earlier. Korean bronze movable type from the 13th century preceded Gutenberg by over 200 years and produced books that still survive today. Yet Gutenberg receives invention credit because his printing press had greater historical impact on European development and led to mass production techniques that transformed global communication. The pattern mirrors toilet paper: earlier non-Western innovation is acknowledged as "precedent" while Western commercialization receives "invention" credit.

The gunpowder case follows similar logic but reveals additional complexities about how cultural context affects invention attribution. Chinese development of gunpowder is well-documented and universally acknowledged, occurring sometime during the Tang Dynasty (9th century). However, European innovations in firearms, artillery, and military applications during the 14th-16th centuries are often treated as separate "inventions" despite building directly on Chinese foundations. The distinction reflects assumptions about what types of technological development deserve independent credit—basic chemistry versus applied engineering, original discovery versus military application.

The compass, papermaking, and porcelain represent additional examples where Chinese innovations preceded Western developments by centuries or millennia, yet Western refinements and commercialization receive disproportionate attention in technological histories. The pattern is sufficiently consistent to suggest systematic rather than accidental bias in how invention narratives are constructed and maintained.

The broader epistemological issue concerns how technological diffusion and cultural transmission affect invention attribution. Most technologies develop through incremental improvements across multiple cultures and time periods rather than through single moments of breakthrough innovation. Toilet paper evolved from early Chinese papermaking through medieval Chinese hygiene applications to modern American commercial production, with each stage building on previous developments. Attributing "invention" to any single stage misrepresents this collaborative, cross-cultural process while privileging whichever cultural context happens to align with the historian's perspective or available sources.

The patent system's role in shaping invention narratives deserves particular attention as an institutional factor that creates systematic bias toward Western technological claims. Patent protection emerged as a formal legal framework primarily in Western industrial contexts during the 17th-19th centuries, creating documented records of technological innovation that are easily accessible to contemporary historians. Earlier innovations developed outside patent systems—including most Chinese technological developments—lack comparable documentary evidence, making them less visible to research methodologies that prioritize formal legal records over historical implementation.

The economic dimension of invention attribution also affects how technological development is understood and credited. Technologies that generate substantial economic value or transform commercial markets receive more attention than innovations that remain culturally localized or economically limited. American toilet paper commercialization created industries, generated profits, and affected millions of consumers, while Chinese court toilet paper remained primarily within elite cultural contexts without broader economic impact. This difference in economic significance can overshadow chronological priority when invention credit is assigned.

The language and translation barriers that have historically limited access to non-Western technological records are gradually diminishing due to digital humanities initiatives and collaborative international scholarship. Projects like the Chinese Text Project and the Digital Silk Road are making vast archives of historical Chinese texts searchable and accessible to international researchers. Similar initiatives for Arabic, Sanskrit, and other historical languages are revealing technological innovations that preceded Western developments across multiple domains. These resources enable more comprehensive technological histories that avoid Western-centric bias through more complete evidence rather than just more inclusive intentions.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not dedicated a specific fact-check to the toilet paper invention claim, likely because it exists in the category of historical assertions that seem too specialized to merit systematic debunking attention. Unlike contemporary misinformation or conspiracy theories that have immediate political or health consequences, historical invention claims typically circulate in educational contexts or casual conversations without triggering fact-checking protocols. This absence of formal scrutiny allows invention myths to persist largely unchallenged in popular discourse.

Where toilet paper history has been addressed in historical and cultural media, the treatment has often been incomplete or misleading. Many sources acknowledge that paper was used for hygiene in ancient China but frame this as a precursor to "real" toilet paper rather than as toilet paper itself. This framing reflects implicit assumptions about what counts as legitimate technological innovation—assumptions that privilege Western commercial contexts over non-Western practical applications. The result is a historical narrative that treats Chinese paper hygiene as interesting prehistory while reserving actual "invention" credit for American commercial developers.

The absence of systematic fact-checking also reflects broader challenges in evaluating historical claims that involve definitional questions about what constitutes "invention." Unlike factual claims about contemporary events that can be verified against clear evidence, invention claims require judgments about which innovations count as sufficiently novel or significant to merit priority. These judgments inevitably involve cultural assumptions about what forms of technology deserve recognition, creating space for bias and incomplete analysis that formal fact-checking processes are not well-equipped to address.

However, the lack of debunking attention has practical consequences for how technological history is understood and taught. Students learning about American innovation may encounter toilet paper as an example of American ingenuity without learning about its Chinese origins, creating distorted impressions about the geographical distribution of technological development. Popular understanding of invention may overemphasize recent Western commercial developments while undervaluing older non-Western innovations, perpetuating biases about which cultures have contributed to human technological progress.
