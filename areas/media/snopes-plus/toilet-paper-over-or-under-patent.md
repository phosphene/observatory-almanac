# The original toilet paper patent proves "over" is correct


## 1. The Claim

Few domestic debates generate more enthusiasm in proportion to their stakes than the question of whether toilet paper should hang with the free end coming over the top of the roll ("over") or behind and under the roll ("under"). The dispute is sufficiently widespread and sufficiently earnest that it has attracted actual social science research, media attention, and — in its most viral form — a specific empirical trump card: the original patent for the toilet paper roll, filed in 1891 by Seth Wheeler, depicts the paper feeding from the top of the roll. Ergo, the claim goes, "over" is not merely a preference — it is the original intended design. Wheeler himself settled it. The "over" camp has won by historical documentation.

The claim has substantial reach. It was reported in a 2015 feature by the *Huffington Post*, immediately went viral, and was subsequently picked up and repeated across hundreds of other publications and social media posts as definitive resolution to the debate. The feature noted the existence of US Patent No. 465,588, filed by Seth Wheeler of Albany, New York, and assigned to the Albany Perforated Wrapping Paper Company — and included an illustration from the patent showing a roll holder with trailing paper feeding from the top, in the "over" configuration.

The framing in virtually all popular coverage was one of resolution and vindication: a historical document, carrying the authority of a legal patent filing, had resolved the question. The "over" camp had been right all along, and now they had proof. The implicit logic was that Wheeler's patent, as a primary document from the invention's origin moment, represented the designer's intent — and that the designer's intent was normative, was "correct," and should resolve contemporary practice.

The claim is worth examining carefully, because it is simultaneously more true and less meaningful than the popular framing suggests, illustrating a distinctive category of factual claim that is accurate about a narrow statement while deeply misleading about what that accuracy implies.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The patent is real and the illustration shows "over."** US Patent No. 465,588 is a genuine historical document. It was filed on December 22, 1891, by Seth Wheeler, and was granted on December 22, 1891 (the same day — a same-day patent approval, common in that era for applications without competing prior art claims). Seth Wheeler was a New York-based entrepreneur who held a number of patents related to perforated paper products; his Albany Perforated Wrapping Paper Company was involved in the nascent commercial toilet paper industry.

The patent document, which is freely available in the US Patent Office's historical patent database and has been widely reproduced since the 2015 viral coverage, does include technical illustrations accompanying the patent claims. These illustrations show a roll mounted on a horizontal spindle, with the trailing paper descending from the top and front of the roll — what is now called the "over" configuration. The illustration is unambiguous on this point. The patent text discusses the perforated separations between sheets and the mechanism for tearing cleanly, but the illustrated configuration is clearly "over."

So the factual core of the claim is accurate: Wheeler's patent illustrations show the "over" configuration, and Wheeler is a legitimate originating figure in the commercial toilet paper roll's history.

**Where the claim becomes misleading.** The inferential leap from "Wheeler's patent illustration shows 'over'" to "this proves 'over' is correct" involves several categorical errors that are worth unpacking in detail.

*Patents describe, not prescribe consumer use.* A patent is a legal document that describes a specific invention in sufficient detail to establish priority and protect the inventor's rights. Patent illustrations are technical drawings supporting the description of how the invention works — they are not design standards, consumer manuals, or usage guides. The fact that Wheeler chose to draw his example with the paper feeding from the top reflects either his personal preference, the way the drawing tool or convention of the era rendered such objects clearly, or simply the arbitrary choice of which orientation to illustrate when both orientations were functionally possible with the same physical device. Patent illustrations do not establish normative standards for product use.

To illustrate the category error: the original patent illustrations for many consumer products show specific orientations, configurations, or use cases that represent one example rather than the only permissible usage. A patent for a drinking vessel illustrated with the handle on the right does not mean left-handed users employ the object incorrectly. A patent for a keyboard illustrated with a specific hand position does not establish that position as the correct typing form. Patent illustrations document how the inventor conceived of one specific implementation; they are silent on the range of permissible adaptations by end users in specific contexts.

*The invention and the standard are different things.* Wheeler patented a specific mechanism involving perforated paper on a roll — the technical advance was the perforation and the roll dispenser, not a rule about which direction the paper should feed. The patent's claims, which are the legally operative section of the document, describe the perforation mechanism and the overall dispenser structure. The orientation of the paper is not a claimed element; it is incidental to the technical illustration used to support the structural claims. The thing Wheeler was inventing and protecting was the perforated separable roll, not the "over" orientation specifically.

*Both orientations are functionally possible with Wheeler's design.* The physical structure described in Wheeler's patent accommodates both "over" and "under" orientations — the roll sits on a spindle and the paper feeds whether it comes from the top or the bottom. There is nothing in the patent's technical claims that constrains the roll to one orientation; both are compatible with the invention as described. This means that even granting the patent maximum normative authority — treating it as if it established a usage standard rather than merely patenting a mechanical device — the patent would not actually prohibit the "under" orientation, because that orientation is equally achievable with the patented mechanism.

*The "correct" framing imports a normative claim the evidence cannot bear.* The claim is framed not as "Wheeler originally preferred 'over'" but as "the patent proves 'over' is correct." The word "correct" carries normative force: it suggests that "under" users are wrong, are deviating from a standard, are making an error. But a patent illustration provides no normative authority over user preferences and adaptation. There is no tribunal that adjudicates toilet paper orientation against a patent standard; there is no standards body that has endorsed Wheeler's illustration as the defining usage specification. The normative force of "correct" is entirely a popular addition to the claim — it is not present in the patent and cannot be derived from it by any reasonable interpretive method.


## 3. The Epistemology of "Settling" a Preference Debate with Design History

The toilet paper patent claim is a clean example of a broader rhetorical phenomenon: using historical or authoritative documentation to "settle" a question that is fundamentally a question about personal and contextual preferences, not about facts. Understanding why this move is appealing and why it is logically flawed is the primary epistemic interest of this case.

**The appeal of settlement.** Preference debates are frustrating precisely because they are not resolvable by evidence in the normal sense. If two people disagree about whether "over" or "under" is better, there is no experiment they can run, no shared standard they can appeal to, that will produce a verdict both must accept. The debate's irresolvability is part of its social function: it is precisely because neither side can win definitively that the debate generates continuous social engagement, becoming a personality marker, a relationship test, a small arena for the performance of conviction about things that do not ultimately matter much.

When a piece of documentary evidence arrives that appears to settle such a debate — the patent says "over"! — it is emotionally satisfying in a way that goes beyond the intellectual content of the document. The patent seems to offer an exit from the exhausting stalemate: we can now stop arguing because history has spoken. This emotional resolution is compelling enough that the category error in the argument — patents don't establish consumer usage standards — is not investigated, because the conclusion is already emotionally desired.

**The authority of historical documents.** A related factor is the cultural authority of formal historical documents, especially patents, which carry the prestige of the legal system and the technology establishment. A patent issued by the United States Patent Office in 1891 sounds authoritative in a way that a contemporary preference poll would not. The document has survived over a century; it is yellowed, official-looking, part of the national record. This aura of authority is not epistemically relevant to the question of what orientation users should choose, but it functions rhetorically to make the patent's testimony feel definitive.

**Epistemic novelty: the invented evidence category.** The toilet paper patent case belongs in a category that might be called "true-but-irrelevant" evidence — evidence that is factually accurate about a narrow proposition while being genuinely incapable of supporting the broader conclusion for which it is cited. This is distinct from fake evidence (which is simply false) and from misread evidence (which mischaracterises what the document actually says). True-but-irrelevant evidence correctly reads the document but draws an invalid inference from it by treating evidence of design-as-illustrated as evidence of norm-for-use.

This category of epistemic error is particularly difficult to correct precisely because fact-checkers who verify the underlying factual claim ("does the patent show 'over'?") will find the answer is yes and may conclude the claim is verified. The error is not in the factual subproposition but in the normative inference drawn from it. Media literacy and fact-checking education would benefit from explicit training in distinguishing "the document says X" from "X therefore settles question Y" — a distinction that requires not just source verification but logical analysis of the inferential chain.

The toilet paper case is valuable as a teaching example precisely because the underlying fact is true, making it impossible to debunk by simple falsification. The corrective must engage at the level of inference rather than fact, which is a harder and less familiar mode for popular fact-checking.


## 4. Verdict

**True-but-misleading — the patent illustration is real but settles nothing normative.** Seth Wheeler's 1891 toilet paper roll patent is a genuine historical document that does depict the "over" orientation in its technical illustrations. This factual claim is accurate and verifiable. However, the leap from this accurate narrow claim to the conclusion that "the patent proves 'over' is correct" involves a category error: patents document specific technical implementations, not normative standards for consumer use. The "over" orientation in Wheeler's illustration reflects how he chose to draw an example, not a prescribed standard that all subsequent users are obligated to follow.

Furthermore, both orientations are physically possible with Wheeler's patented design, so the patent could not prohibit "under" even if patents were interpreted normatively. The normative framing — "correct" versus "incorrect" — is entirely a popular addition to the claim and is not derivable from the patent's content by any valid interpretive method. The patent provides no leverage to resolve a preference debate, because patent illustrations are not usage standards.

This result should not be taken as resolving the debate in the other direction either: nothing in this analysis proves "under" to be correct. The honest conclusion is that the debate is a genuine preference question that cannot be resolved by appeal to historical documents, and that the patent claim, however appealing, does not change this.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The toilet paper patent debate, minor as the underlying subject matter is, illuminates several important features of how factual claims function in contemporary information environments.

**Virality grammar.** The 2015 *Huffington Post* article demonstrated the virality grammar of the "myth-debunked-by-historical-document" format: identify a longstanding debate, introduce a primary historical source, frame it as settlement. This grammar is reliably engaging regardless of whether the document actually settles the question. The format generates the sensation of resolution, of the record being set straight, of the past speaking to the present — sensations that are intrinsically shareable. The reception of the toilet paper patent story illustrates that content which produces this sensation will spread regardless of whether the underlying logical inference is valid.

**Social science on the actual debate.** Interestingly, there is more empirical social science data on toilet paper orientation preferences than on most domestic product debates. Smartphone entrepreneur and author Ron Ghuman conducted a 2011 informal survey that garnered press attention; psychologists have noted that personality factors including assertiveness correlate with orientation preference, with "over" users slightly more likely to score high on assertiveness scales in some small samples. These psychological studies do not establish a normative standard either — they are descriptive associations, not recommendations — but they represent a more epistemically appropriate type of evidence for a question that is ultimately about preferences and behavioural patterns.

**Context genuinely matters.** One genuine resolution available within this debate is that context matters legitimately and differently for different circumstances, which is a more accurate framing than any "correct" answer. "Over" orientation is typically easier to tear one-handed and generally considered more accessible from a physical standpoint, which is why hospital and care settings commonly prefer it. "Under" orientation is recommended in settings where small children or animals might unroll the entire roll, since "under" makes continuous unrolling slightly more difficult. "Over" tends to be preferred in environments with formal aesthetic standards, while "under" may better conceal the free end in certain dispenser designs. These genuinely contextual considerations, which are addressed in the practical writing on the topic, are vastly more useful than appealing to a patent illustration — and they demonstrate that the question is genuinely context-dependent rather than having a universal correct answer.

**Seth Wheeler's actual legacy.** Wheeler was a legitimate innovator in the sanitary paper products industry. His contributions to the development of perforated wrapping paper and the toilet paper roll as a consumer product are real and documented in multiple patent filings, business records, and industry history. The viral framing of the patent story has made Wheeler briefly famous, more than a century after his patent filing, for an aspect of his work that was incidental to his actual inventive contribution. The perforation mechanism — enabling clean, individual-sheet separation — was the genuine technical advance that Wheeler was protecting; the "over" illustration is the least interesting part of the document and the part that attracted all the attention. This is a characteristic irony of how viral content engages with historical material.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The toilet paper patent claim has been assessed by several popular fact-checking and media literacy sources. The general handling has been accurate about the factual subproposition — yes, the patent shows "over" — while varying in how clearly they articulate the inferential problem with treating this as proof of normative correctness.

The *Huffington Post* 2015 article that triggered the viral spread presented the finding in terms that strongly implied settlement ("a 125-year-old patent shows that the correct way to hang toilet paper is over, not under"), and subsequent coverage largely reproduced this framing. Some later analyses, including in science and media literacy outlets, have noted the category error but have not displaced the viral version in popular memory.

For fact-checkers, the case offers a methodological model for handling "true-but-misleading" claims. The verification chain should distinguish between: (1) the narrow factual claim ("does the patent illustration show 'over'?" — yes); (2) the relevance claim ("does a patent illustration establish normative usage standards?" — no, patents don't work that way); and (3) the normative conclusion ("is 'over' therefore correct?" — this cannot be derived from the available evidence). A fact-check that stops at step (1) and reports "TRUE" has technically verified the factual subproposition while completely missing the logical error in the broader claim. This is an instance where a simplistic true/false verdict is inadequate and where a verdict of "true-but-misleading" or "contextually misleading" is more epistemically honest.

Media literacy educators can use this case to illustrate the importance of interrogating the inferential chain in claims that rest on documentary evidence, not merely verifying that the document exists and says what the claim reports. The toilet paper patent claim is a clean, low-stakes example where the factual and logical moves are both highly visible and easy to separate — making it pedagogically useful for teaching the distinction between verification and analysis.

The patent itself remains interesting as a historical artefact. Wheeler's 1891 filing is a genuine piece of the commercial toilet paper industry's early history, and its illustration is a legitimate data point about how Wheeler conceived of his own product. The problem is not with the patent or its illustration; the problem is with what the claim asks the patent to certify. A patent that shows one design choice does not enforce that choice as universal standard, and treating it as if it does confuses the authority of technical documentation with the authority of normative prescription — a confusion that applies far beyond toilet paper orientation and is worth understanding clearly whenever historical documents are invoked to settle contemporary practical debates.
