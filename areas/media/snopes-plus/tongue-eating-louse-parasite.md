# A Parasitic Isopod Replaces a Fish's Tongue and Lives in the Fish's Mouth

**Verdict: True — and epistemologically rich in ways the viral version entirely misses.**

The image has been making rounds on social media for years: a photograph of an open fish mouth revealing, where the tongue should be, a white, segmented, slightly alien-looking crustacean. The creature stares back at the camera with compound eyes. It looks invented. It is not. The parasite is real, the photograph is authentic, and the biology is even stranger than the caption suggests.

---

## 1. The Claim

A photograph shared widely on social media — first by photographer and marine biologist Jimmy Bernot in 2020, and later by the Smithsonian Institution during *#ParasiteWeek2021* — showed a parasitic crustacean inside a fish's mouth, appearing to have replaced its tongue entirely. The claim accompanying the image: the parasite attaches to the tongue, causes it to degrade, and then lives permanently in the fish's mouth as a functional pseudo-tongue, feeding on the host's blood.

As a basic description of what these organisms — called cymothoid isopods — do, this is accurate. The photography is authentic. But the full story of what is actually happening, why it happens, how it works, and what it means scientifically involves layers that no social media caption has ever captured.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

### The organism

The parasite in Bernot's photograph is a cymothoid isopod of the family Cymothoidae. Isopods are crustaceans — the same broad group that includes crabs, shrimps, and lobsters — and most are free-living marine invertebrates. Familiar terrestrial examples include woodlice (also called pillbugs or roly-polies). Of the at least 95 known families of isopods, only a handful are parasitic, and Cymothoidae is among the most morphologically extreme.

The Cymothoidae family contains approximately 350 described species with a nearly global distribution across both oceanic and freshwater habitats. They range in length from a few millimetres to around 7–8 centimetres in the largest species. The fish in Bernot's photograph is *Mugil cephalus*, the flathead mullet — a widespread coastal and estuarine species. The isopod photographed was approximately one inch (about 2.5 cm) long.

Cymothoid isopods superficially resemble their free-living relatives but carry several derived features suited to permanent parasitism: a flattened, elongated body that tapers toward the posterior, and sharply recurved hooks at the end of each pair of legs — seven pairs in total. These hooks are not incidental; they are the parasite's primary means of permanent attachment, and the presence of their impressions on the dorsal side of the fish in Bernot's photograph provided key forensic evidence that the fish was using the isopod to crush food items, as it would have used its original tongue.

### The invasion sequence

The life history of tongue-replacing cymothoids begins in the water column. Juvenile isopods (called *mancas*) swim freely and seek out host fish, entering through the gills. The juveniles of most tongue-biting species arrive as males and initially attach to the gill arches. As they mature, they migrate into the buccal cavity — the oral chamber — and attach to the base of the tongue. Over time, the tongue atrophies and either partially or wholly degrades. The adult female then settles at the base of the tongue stub, where she remains for the rest of her life.

This is the process described in Smithsonian Ocean communications as "crustaceans sever the host fish's tongue." However, as Bernot explained to Snopes, this description is only partially accurate. The cymothoid does not "snip" through the tongue like a pair of scissors. The tongue degradation results from a combination of: (a) blood loss, as the parasite feeds on the blood supply of the tongue, and (b) physical atrophy caused by the mechanical pressure and damage from the parasite's hooked attachment legs at the tongue's base. The tongue wastes away rather than being cut. The distinction matters not just for precision but for understanding the mechanism: it is a slow process of damage-induced atrophy, not a surgical act.

### Protandrous hermaphroditism

Cymothoids exhibit protandrous hermaphroditism — they begin life as males and transition to females. This is relevant to understanding the social structure of parasitic infection. On a given host fish, there is typically one large adult female attached to the tongue base, accompanied by several smaller males attached nearby on the gills or elsewhere in the oral cavity. If the female dies or is removed, the dominant male undergoes sex reversal and becomes female. This is a common strategy in marine invertebrates where access to the opposite sex is unreliable.

### The uniqueness claim

Bernot's photograph was described by the Smithsonian Institution as "the only known instance in the animal kingdom where a parasite has been observed as fully replacing its host's organ." This is a strong empirical claim and should be received with appropriate caution. The scientific literature on cymothoids is extensive but not uniformly complete — many species are described from limited specimens, and the observation space for in-situ parasite behaviour in the ocean is inherently constrained. What can be said accurately is that:

1. The functional tongue replacement by a cymothoid is extremely well-documented.
2. No other textbook example of a parasite wholly taking over a host organ as a functional substitute is widely recognised.
3. The uniqueness claim may be a function of observation bias as much as genuine biological singularity.

The forensic evidence in Bernot's photograph — incisor impressions from the fish's teeth on the parasite's dorsal surface — is genuinely significant. It demonstrates that the fish was mechanically interacting with the cymothoid in the same way it would have interacted with its original tongue, suggesting some degree of functional integration, however exploitative.

### Effects on the host

The effects of cymothoid parasitism on host fish are well-studied and consistently negative. They include:

- **Blood loss and anaemia** from direct feeding
- **Reduced body condition**: parasitised fish are typically smaller, lighter, and have worse body mass indices than unparasitised conspecifics
- **Reproductive suppression**: reduced gonad development and lower reproductive output
- **Feeding impairment**: the pseudo-tongue relationship, while partially functional, is not equivalent to a natural tongue, and feeding efficiency is probably compromised
- **Physical damage**: tissue scarring, gill damage, deformity in heavily parasitised juveniles
- **Immune depression**: chronic parasitism induces measurable immune stress

In aquaculture settings, cymothoid parasites can infect extraordinarily high proportions of fish — Bernot noted that in some farming operations, over 90% of fish on a single farm can be infected. This has significant economic consequences for sea bass (*Dicentrarchus labrax*), sea bream (*Sparus aurata*), and salmon (*Salmo salar*) farming in the Mediterranean and elsewhere.

### Is the fish safe to eat?

Yes. Cymothoid isopods are harmless to humans — they do not carry zoonotic diseases and their presence does not make the fish's flesh unsafe. A parasitised fish may be smaller, may carry surface scars, and is generally of lower commercial value, but eating one poses no human health risk beyond the entirely forgivable aesthetic displeasure of finding a crustacean where you expected a tongue.

## 3. Why People Believe This (and How They Mis-Believe It)

The cymothoid isopod story circulates primarily because the photograph is genuinely shocking — it activates visceral horror at a biological phenomenon that feels designed to disturb. Several features of its viral life are worth analysing:

**The horror-uncanny response.** The isopod looks out from the fish's mouth with compound eyes; it occupies a biological space that "belongs" to a familiar structure (a tongue); it is the right size and shape to feel like a deliberate impostor. This activates the uncanny valley reflex in a non-humanoid context — the almost-right that reads as deeply wrong.

**The "can this be real?" dynamic.** Images that look photoshopped or AI-generated but are authentic have enormous viral energy. The cymothoid photograph triggers the same mechanism as photographs of mantis shrimps, peacock spiders, and other organisms where biological reality substantially exceeds the imagination. The share impulse is "look what nature actually does."

**Amplification by scientific institutions.** When the Smithsonian Institution posts an image for *#ParasiteWeek2021*, it lends credibility to the phenomenon but also, paradoxically, lends credibility to the simplified caption. Institutional amplification can flatten scientific nuance as effectively as tabloid journalism, because the authority transfer functions regardless of caption quality.

**The partial inaccuracy in the Smithsonian's own description.** Describing cymothoids as organisms that "sever the host fish's tongue" is, as Bernot notes, not quite accurate — the tongue atrophies through damage and blood loss rather than being cut. This inaccuracy is institutionally certified and therefore propagates with added authority. It illustrates a broader epistemological problem: institutional endorsement validates the phenomenon but may ossify an imprecise description as canonical.

**Parasitology as an underrepresented field.** Public understanding of parasitology is thin. Most people have mental models of parasites drawn from dogs with worms, or malaria, or head lice. A crustacean that replaces an organ falls so far outside typical mental categories that the brain has no existing schema to examine it with normal scepticism. Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence, but the brain's evidence-evaluation machinery only kicks in when the claim registers as extraordinary — and if the claim is in a domain where the brain has no map at all, it often registers as "probably correct if someone authoritative said it."

## 4. Verdict

**True — and genuinely scientifically significant in ways the viral version does not fully capture.**

The photograph is authentic. The parasite is real. Cymothoid isopods do enter fish hosts through the gills, do attach to and damage the tongue, and do occupy the resulting space as a functional pseudo-tongue while feeding on the host's blood. The forensic evidence in Bernot's photograph confirms the fish was using the parasite mechanically in the manner of a tongue.

The Smithsonian's claim that this is the "only known instance" of a parasite replacing a host organ is compelling but should be held with appropriate scientific humility — it may reflect observation bias rather than absolute biological uniqueness.

The description of cymothoids as "severing" the tongue is technically imprecise; what actually occurs is atrophy through mechanical damage and blood supply deprivation. This distinction is small in terms of the end result but significant for accurately communicating the mechanism.

The claim is a case where the standard "True" verdict is fully warranted, but where significant epistemic value lies not in verifying the headline claim but in unpacking the mechanisms, the evolutionary significance, and the limits of popular formulations of the phenomenon.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### Convergent evolution and parasitic adaptation

Cymothoid isopods are a significant case study in convergent evolution. Multiple independent lineages within the group have evolved the strongly clawed attachment limbs suited for parasitism, suggesting that the genetic and developmental pathways for this adaptation are accessible to natural selection under consistent parasitic pressure. This kind of convergence is of significant interest to evolutionary and developmental biologists because it reveals which morphological solutions are "easy" for evolution to arrive at — implying conserved genetic architectures that can be repeatedly recruited.

Jimmy Bernot's own research at the Smithsonian focuses on crustacean phylogeny and parasite evolution, and the cymothoid story is embedded in a broader programme of understanding how parasitism emerges and diversifies across crustacean lineages.

### The host specificity problem

Most cymothoid species exhibit host specificity — they parasitise a single host species or a narrow group of related species. This is a common pattern in obligate parasites, where the costs of broadening host range (new immune evasion mechanisms, new attachment morphology, new life cycle synchronisation) outweigh the benefits. The evolutionary literature on host-parasite coevolution — including the Red Queen hypothesis, which proposes that parasites drive continuous immune evolution in hosts — is directly relevant here. Cymothoids and their hosts are likely locked in exactly this kind of arms race.

### Aquaculture and food security implications

The economic impact of cymothoid parasitism in Mediterranean aquaculture is non-trivial. Researchers have investigated a variety of control methods, including cleaner wrasse (*Labroides dimidiatus* and related species) as biological control agents — an approach that introduces its own complications, including potential invasiveness and effects on native fish communities. The problem illustrates the broader challenge of intensive aquaculture: high-density fish monocultures create ideal conditions for parasitic amplification, mirroring analogous problems in terrestrial agriculture.

### What we still don't know

For all the viral attention the cymothoid story has received, the basic biology of many of the 350+ species remains incompletely documented. The phylogenetic relationships among cymothoid species are still being worked out with molecular tools. The mechanisms by which mancas locate and select host fish are not fully understood. The question of whether the pseudo-tongue relationship in heavily parasitised fish involves any form of behavioural or physiological accommodation by the host is unstudied. Biology, even for organisms this visually dramatic, is under-observed.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes' March 2021 article by Madison Dapcevich handles this story notably well for a mainstream fact-check. It sources Jimmy Bernot directly (the photographer is also the scientist — an unusual and valuable combination), correctly explains the isopod's biology including the host-specificity and protandrous hermaphroditism details, and explicitly corrects the Smithsonian's "sever" description with Bernot's more accurate explanation of atrophy. The article also checks in with the California Academy of Sciences and gets a species identification for the fish host.

Where Snopes' coverage is limited is not in accuracy but in depth. The broader evolutionary significance of the cymothoid story — convergent evolution, parasite-host coevolution, aquaculture impacts — goes unaddressed because it lies outside the fact-check mandate, which is to verify the claim rather than fully explicate the phenomenon.

The Truth Vault assessment is that this is one of the cleaner cases in the epistemologically-loaded gap category: the claim is true, it is verified, the source is authoritative, but the full significance of what's true is substantially richer than any viral post (or standard fact-check) can convey. The gap here is not between claim and reality — it is between the flat verification of the claim and the layered scientific world behind it.

The epistemological lesson is about the limits of the viral photograph as a knowledge object. A photograph of a cymothoid isopod in a fish's mouth is simultaneously:

- A piece of evidence that the phenomenon is real
- An aesthetic object that generates horror and shares
- An entry point to evolutionary biology, parasitology, and aquaculture science
- A context-stripped fragment of a much larger biological story

The fact-check confirms the first. The viral share exploits the second. The Truth Vault is interested in the third and fourth.
