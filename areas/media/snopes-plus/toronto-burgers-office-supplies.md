## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In March 2021, a screenshot of a food delivery menu circulated on social media appearing to show that a Toronto restaurant had listed some of its burger options under names that mimicked office supply items — "Wired Earphones with Mic," "Dry Erase Board," and similar — ostensibly allowing customers to submit food purchases as work expense claims. The claim attracted widespread attention and appreciation for its satirical take on the remote-work expense culture that had expanded dramatically during the COVID-19 pandemic, with many viewers sharing the screenshot as an example of creative entrepreneurial humor.

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2021) assigned this claim the rating **Mostly True**, with a nuanced verdict structure:

**What's True:** The Toronto restaurant Good Fortune Burger genuinely used office supply names for some of its burgers in a limited-time campaign, explicitly framed as an opportunity for customers to expense their meals on food delivery platforms including UberEats and DoorDash.

**What's False (qualified):** The campaign was temporary. Jon Purdy, Good Fortune's director of operations, characterized the effort as lighthearted and non-malicious — designed "to put a smile on some people's faces" — and acknowledged the humorous rather than systematic intent behind the menu redesign.

This entry examines the Good Fortune Burger campaign and its mechanics, the remote-work expense culture context that made it legible and resonant, the legal and ethical dimensions of the implied expense fraud subtext, the broader phenomenon of restaurant marketing through quirky gimmicks during the pandemic, and the epistemics of "Mostly True" ratings in cases involving genuine facts with important contextual qualifications.

## §2 The Receats Campaign: Product, Mechanism, and Documentation

Good Fortune Burger is a restaurant in Toronto with two locations. The establishment operates primarily in the premium fast-casual burger segment and had an established menu of competitively priced burgers with names aligned with its branding (the flagship item is the "Fortune Burger"). In approximately February 2021, the restaurant launched what it called the "#Receats" campaign — a portmanteau of "receipts" and "eats" — on its food delivery service listings.

The Receats menu was a deliberately temporary and distinct offering from the restaurant's standard menu, available through the UberEats and DoorDash platforms. Under this campaign, a selection of Good Fortune's burger and chicken sandwich items were given new names designed to appear as plausible office supply or electronics line items on an expense report. The example that circulated virally was the "Wired Earphones with Mic" burger. Good Fortune's Instagram account also promoted a "Dry Erase Board" chicken burger as part of the same campaign.

The pricing of the items was not changed; the burgers cost the same as their standard-menu equivalents. The sole modification was the display name — the item's taxonomy in the delivery app interface — which is what would appear on a UberEats or DoorDash receipt given to the customer. A receipt reading "Wired Earphones with Mic: $15.99" would, in appearance, be indistinguishable from a legitimate technology purchase receipt of similar value, provided the payer did not examine the issuing merchant name carefully.

Jon Purdy, Good Fortune's director of operations, provided a statement to BlogTo, a Toronto media outlet, that Evon (2021) cites directly: "We just wanted an opportunity to put a smile on some people's faces and have them have a little bit of a giggle." Purdy also stated: "There's no malice intended in it, it's all just fun and games." This framing is notable for what it acknowledges and what it leaves aside. The restaurant explicitly positioned the campaign as entertainment rather than as a scheme to enable fraudulent expense reporting, but did not address the question of whether customers actually submitting these items as work expenses would be acting fraudulently, or the restaurant's responsibility for facilitating such behavior if it occurred.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Mostly True Verdict, Contextual Qualifications, and Humor–Harm Ambiguity

The "Mostly True" rating in this case rests on a specific kind of factual qualification that illustrates an underappreciated dimension of multi-level fact claims. The underlying claim — "A Toronto restaurant renamed some of its burgers as office supplies so people could expense them" — is factually accurate with respect to what the restaurant did. The qualification that earns "Mostly" rather than full "True" is the characterization of the campaign: it was temporary (not permanent), and the restaurant's stated intent framed it as humor rather than as a genuine expensing infrastructure. The viral social media framing, by contrast, implied a more permanent and systematic arrangement than the campaign actually represented.

This is a case where the "what" is true (the campaign existed, the naming was real, the intent to allow expensing was explicit) but the "how much" and "how seriously" components are qualified. Fact-check rating systems that operate with five or more gradations (True, Mostly True, Half True, Mostly False, False, plus special categories like Unproven) are better equipped to handle this type of qualified verification than binary true/false frameworks. The Snopes "Mostly True" rating captures the essential accuracy of the claim while flagging the important contextual qualification that the viral framing may have overstated the campaign's scope or permanence.

The epistemic novelty in this case for Truth Vault purposes lies in the humor–harm ambiguity that underlies the campaign's reception. The Receats campaign functions simultaneously on two distinct communicative registers:

**Register 1: Satire/entertainment.** The campaign is a joke about remote-work expense culture, the blurring of work and personal consumption during the pandemic, the absurdity of expense report categories, and the particular opacity of receipt-based expense systems. On this register, it is a lighthearted cultural commentary that does not encourage anyone to actually submit fraudulent expense claims.

**Register 2: Practical facilitation.** The campaign actually, concretely, in the material world, produced receipts that would be difficult to distinguish from legitimate office supply purchases at the receipt-inspection level of many corporate expense systems. On this register, it provided genuine infrastructure for expense fraud, regardless of whether the restaurant "intended" to enable fraud.

The tension between these two registers creates what Shifman (2014), in *Memes in Digital Culture* (MIT Press), calls "irony collapse" — a phenomenon in which a clearly ironic or satirical communication is adopted non-ironically by a subset of its audience who treat it as practical guidance or permission. The fact that some commenters on the viral posts raised concerns about whether employees who submitted these receipts would get in trouble confirms that the communication was received both on the satirical and the practical register simultaneously.

Purdy's characterization — "There's no malice intended in it, it's all just fun and games" — is a statement about the restaurant's intent, not about the consequences for employees who acted on the practical register. This distinction matters epistemically: intent and effect are separable, and a campaign can be non-malicious in intent while having the effect of enabling or encouraging behavior with real-world consequences (employment sanctions, fraud investigations) for the individuals who act on it.

The "Mostly True" rating navigates this by verifying the factual claim about what the restaurant did while noting the temporary and humor-framed character of the campaign, but does not fully address the ethical complexity of the humor-harm ambiguity. This is a legitimate epistemic limitation of the fact-check format: the role of a verdictized fact-check is to assess the factual accuracy of a claim, not to adjudicate its ethical implications.

## §4 Remote Work, Expense Culture, and the Pandemic Context

The Receats campaign would have been legible and resonant only within a specific economic and cultural moment: the large-scale shift to remote work triggered by the COVID-19 pandemic. To understand why the campaign attracted the attention it did, it is necessary to contextualize the expense culture dynamics that it satirized.

Prior to the pandemic, corporate expense accounts and their associated reimbursement systems were structured around a broadly consistent set of categories: travel (flights, hotels, taxis, mileage), meals (typically during travel or client entertainment), and specific equipment purchases. The fundamental logic of the pre-pandemic expense system assumed that work occurred in an office and that work-related expenses were geographically and temporally distinct from personal expenses.

The mass shift to remote work in 2020–2021 substantially disrupted this logic. Millions of workers suddenly incurred genuine work-related expenses in the home environment — desk equipment, webcams, broadband upgrades, ergonomic chairs — that were categorically novel relative to existing expense frameworks. Corporate policy responses were uneven: some companies issued stipends, others updated their expense policies, and many made ad hoc decisions that were ambiguous, internally inconsistent, or communicated poorly.

Bloom, Davis, and Zhestkova (2021), in their analysis of remote work and productivity published in the *NBER Working Paper Series*, found that by mid-2020, approximately 42% of the US labor force was working from home full-time, representing an unprecedented concentration of formerly commuting workers in domestic settings. The associated consumer behavioral changes included increased expenditure on home office equipment, increased food delivery usage (as restaurant dining was restricted), and increased awareness of the costs of home-based work that had previously been employer-subsidized.

The specific intersection of food delivery and expense reimbursement in the Receats campaign reflects a genuine axis of pandemic-era policy ambiguity: could meals ordered via delivery platforms while working from home qualify as work expenses? The answer varies by employer, jurisdiction, and circumstance. Meals during business travel are typically reimbursable; meals during a normal work day (including remote work) are typically not, under standard corporate expense policies. But the lines became blurred during the pandemic period as the home became simultaneously a domestic and professional space.

Brown and Matsa (2020), in a report published by the *National Bureau of Economic Research*, found that restaurant industry revenues in major urban markets collapsed by 40–60% in the early months of the pandemic, with recovery concentrated in delivery and takeout channels. Toronto's restaurant industry — already operating under Ontario's Progressive Conservative government's extended lockdown and restriction regimes — faced particular pressure, and independent restaurants like Good Fortune operated in an environment of severe financial stress.

The Receats campaign can therefore also be read as a form of creative economic solidarity: a restaurant attempting to stimulate delivery sales by giving customers a culturally resonant reason to order, communicated in a tone that matched the exhausted, humor-seeking mood of a population many months into pandemic restrictions. This commercial motivation is not in conflict with the entertainment framing but runs alongside it.

## §5 Restaurant Guerrilla Marketing and the Viral Menu Phenomenon

The Receats campaign belongs to a well-established genre of restaurant marketing that might be called "satirical menu design" — a form of guerrilla marketing in which the menu itself, rather than conventional advertising, becomes the communication vehicle. This genre has precedents in satirical pricing ("pay what you can"), punning menu naming ("The Notorious F.I.G."), and deliberately provocative or absurdist menu descriptions.

Holt (2004), in *How Brands Become Icons* (Harvard Business School Press), argued that brands that successfully insert themselves into cultural conversations through authentic-seeming rather than corporate-seeming communications develop "cultural capital" that conventional advertising cannot purchase. The Receats campaign exemplifies this model: the restaurant spent no advertising budget on the campaign's virality; the organic sharing of the menu screenshot generated media reach across multiple countries at zero direct cost.

The screenshot-as-content model is particularly effective in the social media environment because screenshots present information in a decontextualized but credible-seeming format. A screenshot of a restaurant menu cannot easily be verified as current or as accurately representing the totality of the menu, but it reads as documentary rather than promotional. This is why the initial social media spread generated genuine uncertainty about the campaign's mechanics and permanence — the screenshot format did not carry framing signals that would indicate a limited-time, humor-oriented campaign.

Good Fortune's campaign strategy was sophisticated in its execution: by launching on UberEats and DoorDash specifically, the restaurant ensured that the renamed items would generate genuine receipts with official UberEats/DoorDash branding and formatting, maximizing the practical plausibility of the expense-account gag. A mock-up receipt created independently would not carry the same implied authenticity as an actual delivery app receipt. This attention to the mechanics of receipt-based verification distinguishes Receats from simpler menu-naming gimmicks.

Restaurant marketing innovation accelerated substantially during the pandemic period, with establishments across North America, Europe, and Asia experimenting with novelty approaches to stimulate delivery and takeout sales in an environment where dine-in revenue was severely restricted. Creative menu packaging, novel promotion mechanics (restaurant-branded meal kits, virtual cooking classes, chef-curated delivery experiences), and social media-native campaigns became essential tools for cash-strapped independent restaurateurs who lacked the advertising budgets of chain competitors.

## §6 Verdict Assessment and Broader Implications

The "Mostly True" verdict correctly identifies the essential factual content of the claim as accurate — the campaign was real, the naming was intentional, the expensing intent was explicit — while noting the temporal and framing qualifications that prevent full "True" status under Snopes's rating conventions. This is an appropriate handling of a case where the primary distinction between "True" and "Mostly True" lies not in the facts themselves but in the contextual framing that viral spread imposed on a limited-scope campaign.

The broader significance of the Toronto burger case for Truth Vault analysis is threefold.

First, it illustrates the mechanics of viral screenshot-based claims: screenshots strip away context (temporality, scale, intent framing) in ways that systematically amplify claims beyond their evidence base. A screenshot of the Receats menu captured a moment that implied permanence, comprehensiveness, and systematic intent that the actual campaign did not quite possess. Fact-checking of screenshot-based claims therefore requires interrogating not only what the screenshot shows but what the screenshot omits.

Second, it illustrates the epistemics of humor-framed marketing claims. When a real commercial action is presented with a humor frame, the question of what the claim actually asserts becomes ambiguous: is the claim about what the restaurant did (clearly verifiable), what it intended (a matter of stated corporate communication), or what its customers would actually do with the infrastructure it provided (not within the restaurant's control or fact-checkers' direct observational access)? The Mostly True verdict sidesteps the harder questions by focusing on the first — the factual question of what the restaurant did — which is the appropriate scope for a verdictized fact-check.

Third, the case is a minor but instructive example of how pandemic-era economic distress produced innovation in restaurant marketing communications, and how that innovation intersected with the specific cultural anxieties — productivity theater, work-from-home expense ambiguity, the collapse of work/personal boundaries — that characterized the pandemic economy in 2020–2021. Good Fortune's Receats campaign is, in retrospect, a small cultural artifact of that moment: clever, economically motivated, ethically ambiguous on the margins, and entirely real.

The fact that the campaign required fact-checking at all — that a genuine, documented, publicly acknowledged marketing initiative by an identified restaurant still generated enough uncertainty to warrant verification — reflects the degraded epistemic environment of social media discourse, in which even real things require independent confirmation that they are what they appear to be. This is the normalized condition that digital fact-checking operations inhabit, and the burger-as-office-supply case is a vivid, precisely scaled example of it.
