## The Claim

On April 15, 2024 (and in earlier waves of viral spread from April–May 2023), a photograph circulated widely on Reddit, social media, and mainstream media outlets showing an iceberg with a strikingly phallic shape floating in what was described as Conception Bay, Newfoundland, Canada. The claim was simple and provided through the Reddit headline: "Photographer Ken Pretty of Dildo, Newfoundland captures photo of a phallic looking iceberg." At the time of the fact-check's publication, the Reddit post had received more than 35,000 upvotes.

The image required verification on two independent axes: first, was the photograph genuine and unmanipulated? Second, were the claimed contextual details — photographer Ken Pretty, location in Dildo/Conception Bay, Newfoundland — accurate? The combination of all these elements was sufficiently improbable, and the reputational incentive to fabricate was sufficiently high, that independent confirmation was warranted.

Snopes fact-checker Madison Dapcevich conducted the investigation for a fact-check published April 23, 2024. The verdict was "True" across both axes. The photograph is genuine, the minor edits were disclosed and cosmetic only, and the full contextual framing — photographer identity, location, geographic name context — is confirmed by multiple independent sources including the photographer himself and major international publications.

## What's Actually True

Ken Pretty is a real person and an avid drone hobbyist based in Dildo, Newfoundland. He posted the photographs on Facebook on April 27, 2023. After the images went globally viral in the weeks following, Pretty was interviewed by multiple news organisations including The Guardian (Cecco, L., May 1, 2023, *The Guardian*), Daily Mail Online (Andrews, L., May 1, 2023, *Daily Mail Online*), and Business Insider (Bharade, A., May 2023, *Business Insider*), all of which independently confirmed his identity, location, and the photograph's provenance.

In an email to Snopes, Pretty confirmed the photograph was "real and not photoshopped," though he noted he had made minor adjustments in "clarity and vibrance" — standard post-processing edits that enhance tonal contrast and colour saturation but do not alter the subject's shape, form, or appearance. He also provided Snopes with a link to drone video footage of the iceberg, posted to YouTube on April 28, 2023 — the day after the photograph was taken — which shows the iceberg in motion, from multiple angles, in full video, making manipulation essentially impossible to sustain as a hypothesis. The video was archived by the Wayback Machine on June 10, 2023 (Pretty, K., *Harbour Grace Iceberg Taken April 27, 2023*, YouTube).

Pretty provided additional geographic and contextual detail to Snopes: "The image was captured in a town called Harbour Grace, which is in Conception Bay, Newfoundland. The fact that it was 'Conception' Bay and I live in a town called 'Dildo' drove social media nuts." He added that the iceberg's distinctive shape had been partially visible from land, but the full extent of its form only became apparent from altitude: "But it wasn't until I flew out with my drone and got the aerial shot that you can really see how much" it appeared phallic.

The iceberg was dubbed "Dickieberg" by at least one social media user — a nickname that spread alongside the original photograph. Pretty noted that the iceberg toppled over the day after the photograph was taken, making the image a singular document of a structure that existed for at most a short period before rolling over, a characteristic of icebergs generally as their above-water mass distributions shift with melting and wave action.

The town of Dildo's curious name has an independently documented, if somewhat ambiguous, etymology. As Condé Nast Traveler reported (Condé Nast Traveler, December 10, 2013), the name dates to at least 1711 and was, at that time, a word used for cylindrical objects generally — including nautical pins and test tubes — without the specific sexual connotation it carries today. The regional blog *Product of Newfoundland* identifies the most persistent explanation as derivation from the wooden oar pegs of traditional fishing boats (Product of Newfoundland, accessed 2024). An alternative theory suggests the town may have been named after a nearby island. The toponym therefore predates the modern sexual connotation and carries its own distinct settlement history; the fact-check notes this etymology without claiming to resolve the ambiguity definitively.

## The Science and Epistemics of Iceberg Shape

The photograph documents a real natural phenomenon that is, while unusual, entirely consistent with the physical processes governing iceberg formation and evolution. Understanding why icebergs can form phallic shapes is both intrinsically interesting and epistemically important: it provides the explanatory infrastructure that precludes a viewer from needing to fall back on manipulation claims.

Icebergs are defined by the National Ocean Service (NOAA) as large pieces of freshwater ice that have calved, or broken off, from glaciers or shelf ice (U.S. Department of Commerce, NOAA, *What Is an Iceberg?*, accessed 2024). To meet the classification threshold for "iceberg" — as opposed to smaller categories like "bergy bits" and "growlers" — a floating ice mass must rise more than 16 feet (approximately 5 metres) above sea level, have a thickness between 98 and 164 feet (30–50 metres), and cover an area of at least 5,382 square feet (500 square metres). These thresholds are used by the International Ice Patrol and in marine navigation databases to classify and track sea ice hazards.

Icebergs are additionally classified by shape. NOAA distinguishes two primary morphological categories: tabular icebergs, which have steep near-vertical sides and flat tops resembling plateaux — characteristic of calving events from Antarctic ice shelves — and non-tabular icebergs, which encompass a wide range of shapes including domed, pinnacled, wedge, drydock, and irregular forms. Non-tabular icebergs can develop any morphology consistent with differential melting and mass-wasting from the original calved block. Selective melting around the waterline (where wave action, solar energy, and thermal mixing are concentrated), subaerial melting of the above-water portion by solar radiation and precipitation, and episodic calving of overhanging sections all contribute to iceberg shape evolution over time (Diemand, D., 2001, *Cold Regions Science and Technology*, Elsevier).

The Newfoundland "Dickieberg" is classified as a non-tabular pinnacled iceberg. Pinnacled icebergs feature one or more pointed or columnar projections rising above the main body. The phallic reading of the shape depends on the specific combination of: a tall, comparatively narrow central column; a rounded or slightly wider upper terminus; and a broader base or subsidiary projection at the lower portion. All of these features are consistent with natural differential melting processes. The central column is preserved because it sits within the zone of minimum wave action; the broader base reflects the concentrations of melting at the waterline; the upper protrusion is sustained because above-water melting, while continuous, is offset by the thermal insulation effect of the ice mass itself.

The epistemic novelty in this case is a question about natural morphological diversity: how many icebergs form shapes that approximate human body forms, and how often does such resemblance go unnoticed simply because no drone-equipped photographer is present to document it? The Newfoundland archipelago's southern shore lies in the path of the Labrador Current, which transports icebergs calved from Greenland's west-coast glaciers southward through Iceberg Alley — a stretch of ocean between Labrador and Newfoundland that channels significant iceberg traffic every spring (Marko, J.R. et al., 1982, *Atmosphere-Ocean*, Taylor & Francis). Iceberg Alley is one of the most densely iceberged coastal waters on Earth outside the polar regions. Pretty's ability to photograph this particular iceberg resulted from both his geographic proximity and his possession of drone equipment capable of providing the aerial vantage from which the shape is most clearly visible — from directly above or at a slight angle, where the columnar projection is unambiguous.

## Why People Believe (and Why This Needed Fact-Checking)

The photograph's claim appears self-evidently capable of being true: iceberg shapes are variable, Newfoundland is in Iceberg Alley, drone photography of unusual natural formations is common, and the town of Dildo genuinely exists. So why did it require fact-checking?

**The too-perfect story problem.** The convergence of coincidences in this claim approaches a threshold where it starts to feel *too* good to be true: a photographer named Ken Pretty from a town called Dildo photographs a phallic iceberg in Conception Bay. Each element is amusing; all elements together suggest a degree of narrative neatness inconsistent with typical news randomness. This intuition — that a story sounds too perfectly arranged — is often misapplied (reality is under no obligation to avoid creating amusing coincidences), but it is a legitimate heuristic that responsible media consumers have learnt to apply to content that generates strong emotional engagement.

**The incentive to fabricate.** A photograph with this combination of funny details would be enormously valueable to a fabricator. The demand for this specific type of sexually comedic viral content is high, as evidenced by the content's rapid global spread through major publications. This high fabrication incentive makes independent confirmation more rather than less important, regardless of whether an individual viewer suspects fabrication. The value of Snopes' confirmation is precisely that it closes off residual uncertainty for this category of high-incentive-to-fabricate content.

**Platform cascade and tabloid amplification.** The image's global circulation through The Guardian, Daily Mail Online, and Business Insider indicates a specific type of media dynamics: a story that works across multiple publication formats. The Guardian carries the story as a kind of light news curiosity with geographic interest; the Daily Mail packages it as visual content for maximum engagement; Business Insider frames it through the travel and nature interest angle. Each publication adds apparent verification through its institutional credibility, even though none had independently verified the claim before publication in the initial wave. Snopes' later confirmation filled the verification gap that the initial media cascade had largely bypassed.

**Drone photography and documentary potential.** The use of a drone to capture the image is a significant authenticity signal. Drone operators post video footage as a matter of routine; video cannot be shaped—it documents continuous motion and multiple angles simultaneously. If the photograph had been manipulated to create or exaggerate the phallic shape, the accompanying video would have revealed the manipulation immediately. The existence of the video is therefore a strong (though not absolute) truthfulness signal for any viewer aware of it.

## Verdict

**True.** The photograph was taken by Ken Pretty, a drone hobbyist from Dildo, Newfoundland, on April 27, 2023. The image shows a real iceberg in Conception Bay near Harbour Grace, Newfoundland. The iceberg genuinely had a phallic shape due to natural differential melting processes consistent with the non-tabular pinnacled classification. Pretty made minor standard post-processing adjustments (clarity and vibrance), which he disclosed. The iceberg was documented in drone video from multiple angles, making photographic manipulation implausible. The geographic context — the town of Dildo, Conception Bay — is accurate. The iceberg toppled the day after being photographed. Snopes confirmed the claim as "True" following direct contact with the photographer.

## The Wider Picture

The Dildo iceberg story sits at a productive intersection of physical oceanography, glaciology, place-name linguistics, and the sociology of sexually comedic viral content — fields rarely brought into contact but each illuminated by this particular conjunction.

**Iceberg Alley and Labrador Current transport dynamics.** Newfoundland's annual iceberg traffic is a significant oceanographic phenomenon with both scientific and navigational significance. Approximately 400–800 icebergs per year drift south of 48°N latitude in Iceberg Alley, primarily during the April–July season when meltwater-accelerated calving from Greenland's glaciers peaks and southern-drifting icebergs have not yet melted. Marko, J.R. et al. (1982, *Atmosphere-Ocean*) documented the seasonal patterns of iceberg distribution and tracking in Labrador-Newfoundland waters, providing the baseline statistical picture of iceberg frequency that contextualises any unusual iceberg observations. Climate change is altering this baseline: a warming Arctic is accelerating Greenland glacial calving, increasing the volume of ice entering the transport system, while warmer ocean temperatures are simultaneously accelerating iceberg melt rates, compressing the time window between calving and dissolution (Bigg, G.R. & Wadley, M.R., 2001, *Journal of Quaternary Science*, Wiley).

**Iceberg shape evolution and stability.** Icebergs are dynamically unstable structures. The density difference between ice (approximately 917 kg/m³) and seawater (approximately 1,025 kg/m³) means approximately 90% of an iceberg's mass lies below the waterline. Surface melting and calving continuously alter an iceberg's above-water shape and hence its centre of gravity relative to its centre of buoyancy, leading eventually to sudden capsize events. Weeks, W.F. & Mellor, M. (1978, *Cold Regions Research and Engineering Laboratory*) provided foundational analysis of iceberg stability and capsize dynamics. The "Dickieberg's" toppling the day after photographing is consistent with this dynamic: pinnacled icebergs with high-aspect-ratio projections are inherently unstable, with the projecting column representing a concentration of mass at altitude that progressively destabilises the berg as differential melting continues.

**Newfoundland toponymy and colonial naming.** The town of Dildo's toponym is one of several Newfoundland place names with pre-modern etymologies that have acquired awkward connotations in contemporary English. Newfoundland's settlement history — primarily English and Irish, with earlier Basque and French presence — produced a coastal toponymy that reflects fishing technology, local geography, and the linguistic patterns of 17th and 18th century fishermen and settlers. Names that were straightforwardly descriptive in 1700 may carry very different resonance in 2024. The town has voted multiple times on whether to rename itself, most recently in a 2019 vote in which residents chose — by a ratio of approximately 3:1 — to retain the name. The name is now an asset for tourism and media attention rather than a liability.

**Drone photography as democratised natural documentation.** Pretty's ability to capture the aerial footage that most clearly revealed the iceberg's shape reflects the broader democratisation of aerial photography since consumer drone platforms became widely available circa 2012–2015. Pre-drone, the aerial perspective essential for clearly documenting this iceberg's form would have required a chartered helicopter or fixed-wing aircraft — resources unavailable to most recreational photographers. Consumer drones have substantially expanded the archive of memorable natural documentation by making aerial capture routine for anyone within range of a striking natural feature, and the "Dickieberg" is as clear an example of drone-enabled discovery as any in the viral archive.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes' verification of this claim is notable for its source-contact methodology and its appropriate use of corroborating publication evidence.

**Direct source contact.** The most important verification step was contacting Ken Pretty directly through Facebook. This direct source contact — asking the claimed photographer whether the image was genuine — is the correct first step when the claimant is identifiable and reachable. It is a comparatively rare step in viral-photograph fact-checking: many verifications rely exclusively on reverse-image search, metadata, and expert opinion rather than direct contact with named sources. In this case, Pretty's candid disclosure of the minor post-processing edits he made, and his provision of the video link, increased rather than decreased the credibility of the claim: a fabricator would be unlikely to volunteer information about any editing.

**Corroborating publication search.** The Google keyword search that produced dozens of major international news articles about the photograph — including The Guardian, Daily Mail, and Business Insider — provided corroborating evidence without requiring Snopes to verify those publications' own verification procedures. When multiple independent outlets have investigated and published a claim citing named sources with verifiable identities, the cumulative evidence from these publications substantially lowers the residual false-hood probability even before independent verification is complete.

**NOAA classification as encyclopaedic anchor.** The inclusion of the NOAA definition and classification framework for icebergs anchors the scientific context correctly and provides readers with a clear pathway to understanding why icebergs can form such shapes — the natural mechanism that explains the finding without requiring any special pleading or coincidence beyond What Happens To Ice In Water. This kind of scientific explanation-anchoring is a specific strength of Snopes' style: it links the specific claim to the general scientific framework that makes the claim intelligible.

**The scope of the verification.** One thing not addressed in the fact-check is the iceberg's precise classification and estimated size — whether it met the strict NOAA threshold for "iceberg" status versus a smaller "bergy bit" or "growler." Given the visual impression from the video and photographs, it is plausible but not confirmed that the structure met the 16-foot above-sea-level and 500 square metre coverage thresholds. This detail is not critical to the core claim but would add precision to the physical description.

The Dildo Dickieberg is, in its own way, a perfect case: a genuine image, fully confirmable through standard methods, of a real phenomenon, in a real location, with a story that generates maximum attention precisely because reality arranged itself into a configuration that fiction would hesitate to deploy. There is nothing to debunk here — only to confirm, explain, and enjoy.
