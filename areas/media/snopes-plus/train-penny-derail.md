# Will Placing a Penny on the Tracks Derail a Train?


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: A penny placed on a railroad track will cause the next train to pass over it to derail — that is, to leave the tracks and potentially overturn or crash. The mechanism implied is that the coin creates a sufficient obstacle on the rail surface to disrupt the train wheel's contact with the track, tilting the wheel over the coin and throwing the vehicle off course.

**Cultural prevalence**: The practice of placing coins on railroad tracks to have them flattened by passing trains is genuinely long-standing in American (and broader) culture. Children have placed pennies on tracks for at least a century, collecting the resulting flattened ovals as curiosity souvenirs. The flattened penny is a familiar folk artifact of American childhood, warm from the friction of being run over, stretched into an oval shape that preserves a ghost of Lincoln's profile. Many adults have one in a drawer somewhere. The practice is widespread enough to have generated an entire cottage industry of commemorative pressed-penny machines installed at tourist destinations worldwide, which use rollers to create artistically engraved souvenir ovals using the visitor's own coin.

**The companion warning**: Attached to this well-known practice is a companion warning that many children have received from adults: "Be careful — you could derail the train." This warning dramatically raises the stakes of the coin-placing activity from petty vandalism to catastrophic crime, potentially frightening children into compliance through the image of a massive train wreck caused by a single cent piece. In many retellings, the person who placed the penny would also be liable for the crash, adding legal jeopardy to the physical threat.

**Variants and escalations**: Some versions of the claim specify that larger coins (quarters, silver dollars) have actually caused derailments; other versions restrict the claim to pennies but frame it as a real possibility rather than a locked certainty. A few versions replace the penny with a rock, a bolt, or a piece of wood, blurring the urban legend with a different population of genuinely dangerous track obstructions. The most dramatic versions describe specific incidents of derailments allegedly caused by coins, though these incidents are never documented. 

**The claim's behavioral target**: Whether true or false, the claim is directed at a specific behavior: children being near and on active railroad tracks, which are genuinely and lethally dangerous environments. The derailment warning serves a protective behavioral purpose — discouraging kids from playing on tracks — even if the specific mechanism it invokes (train derailment from a penny) is physically impossible. This dual structure (false mechanism, correct behavioral target) makes the claim a particularly interesting example of a myth that points at real danger through a false analogy.

**Legal dimensions of the claim**: In many jurisdictions, placing objects on railroad tracks with intent to obstruct or damage is a criminal offense, regardless of the object's actual capacity to cause harm. The legal status of penny-placement is therefore distinct from its physical consequences: the act may be illegal as "obstructing a railroad" even if the penny would have zero effect on any train. Snopes notes multiple cases of "pennying the tracks" leading to deaths — not from train derailments, but from people struck by trains while engaged in the activity.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The mass disparity that makes derailment impossible**: The fundamental physics. A standard US penny since 1982 weighs 2.5 grams, consisting of a zinc core electroplated with copper. A typical passenger train locomotive weighs approximately 120–200 metric tons (120,000–200,000 kilograms) depending on the model. A freight locomotive can weigh 180–220 metric tons. Even modestly loaded freight cars add 30–50 metric tons of additional mass per car. A 50-car freight train may have a total weight of 3,000–6,000 metric tons or more. The mass ratio of train to penny is on the order of 10,000,000:1 or larger.

**What happens physically when a train wheel contacts a penny**: A railroad wheel has a flanged profile — a projecting rim on the inner edge that keeps the wheel on the track. The wheel tread is the flat or slightly conical surface that contacts the top of the rail. A penny lying flat on the top of a rail is approximately 1.52 mm thick. When the train wheel rolls over it, the wheel exerts an enormous force — typically tens to hundreds of kilonewtons per wheel depending on load — onto the penny. The penny is not rigid enough to redirect this force; it deforms plastically and is crushed flat against the rail surface within microseconds. The resulting flattened disk is the familiar souvenir.

**The irrelevance of the penny's thickness to wheel-track dynamics**: For an obstacle to derail a train wheel, it would need to exert a lateral force on the wheel strong enough to push the flange over the inner edge of the rail, or to lift the wheel high enough off the rail surface that the flange can no longer contain it. A 1.52 mm thick penny cannot do this. Even if the coin were oriented edge-on (perpendicular to the rail surface, presenting its 19 mm diameter as height), the coin would be crushed laterally by the wheel's enormous load before any significant deflection could occur. The geometry and force magnitudes make derailment from a penny physically impossible.

**What CAN cause derailment**: Objects and conditions that have caused real train derailments are well-documented in railroad safety literature and NTSB (National Transportation Safety Board) investigation reports. These include: large rocks or concrete blocks placed on tracks; rail breaks and track defects (split rails, broken joints); track geometry defects (rail misalignment, improper gauge); improperly secured loads on freight cars shifting weight; extreme thermal expansion of rails in high heat causing "sun kinks"; and relatively large man-placed obstructions. In July 1999, three youth in Indiana were charged in the derailment of a South Shore passenger train after placing a brick on the tracks in Michigan City — demonstrating that a rigid, large-enough object can cause derailment (Maddux, S., 1999, *South Bend Tribune*). A penny is not of that class.

**The documented deaths from pennying**: The real danger of placing pennies on tracks is not to the train but to the person. Snopes documents multiple fatalities from this practice. In 1986, Laura Ann Foote (18) of Chico, California, was killed by a train while attempting to flatten a penny. In 1992, Rolyn J. St. Louis (45) of Columbus, Ohio, died after placing a penny on one set of tracks and then standing on an adjacent set, unaware that the oncoming train was on the track he was standing on. In 1997, Shelly Lynn Wice (14) and Jessica Ann Hart (14) of Oil City, Pennsylvania, died from the same scenario — watching one track while standing on another. In 1996, Bruce Darling (25), a father of two from Northumberland, England, was struck and killed at a train station after attempting to flatten pennies. In 2007, retired police officer David Buffa (55), attempting to demonstrate how a penny would be flattened, was struck while trying to return to a platform. These are documented casualties — not from train derailments, but from the lethal reality of standing near operating railroad tracks.

**The projectile hazard**: An additional genuine physical danger is that the coin, upon being run over, can be ejected at high velocity in an unpredictable direction. The combination of the wheel's rolling motion and the coin's deformation can launch the flattened or partially deformed coin at speeds that could cause serious injury to bystanders. Snopes notes this explicitly: "sometimes those coins shoot out from under the train's wheels at incredible speeds, turning them into potentially deadly little projectiles." This is a real but secondary physical consequence compared to the primary danger of standing near operated tracks.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty — the human failure to intuit force-scale disparities**:

The penny-derails-the-train belief rests on a characteristic failure of human physical intuition: the inability to correctly reason about force and mass across orders of magnitude. People intuitively understand small-scale cause and effect well — a marble placed on a toy track will disrupt a toy train, and this intuition is entirely correct at that scale. The scaling error arises when this intuition is uncritically extended to the real-world case involving force magnitudes that differ by a factor of millions.

Human force intuition evolved in an environment where the heaviest objects we typically interact with are in the range of a few hundred kilograms — large animals, logs, boulders. We have a reasonable visceral sense of what it takes to push, topple, or damage objects in that mass range. A penny genuinely is a meaningful obstacle to a toy train. A penny genuinely is an obstacle that a human foot might trip over. A penny genuinely can crack an egg if dropped on it from a certain height. These experiential frames — penny-as-meaningful-obstacle across different situations — transfer incorrectly to the railroad context because we lack an experiential frame for the forces produced by industrial rolling stock.

The same cognitive failure underlies related myths: that a penny dropped from the Empire State Building will kill a pedestrian (it won't, for aerodynamic reasons) — that a single rifle bullet can destroy an engine block — that a child can cause a car accident by throwing a water bottle. In each case, a small object is attributed destructive efficacy it cannot actually possess because the counterforces (air resistance, engine block hardness, vehicle kinetic energy) are outside everyday experiential intuition.

There is also a kind of moral imagination at work in the penny derailment claim. Children know that trains are massive and powerful and dangerous. The idea that something as small and innocent as a penny could topple such a giant has a mythological resonance — David-and-Goliath, the small thing bringing down the mighty structure. This dramatic narrative logic makes the claim feel emotionally right even when the physics is wrong. Myths that align with morally or dramatically satisfying narrative templates persist more readily than myths that merely arise from information gaps.

**The claim as protective lie**: Adults who warned children about penny-caused derailments may have believed the warning themselves, or may have deployed it knowingly as a fiction designed to keep children away from genuinely dangerous environments. Tracks are lethally dangerous — exactly the deaths cited above demonstrate this without any derailment whatsoever — and a dramatic prohibition built around a catastrophic consequence is more effective at behavioral compliance than a probabilistic warning about "sometimes trains can hit you and the driver might not see you." The absolute falsity of the specific mechanism is less important, in this protective-lie reading, than the behavioral outcome it produces.


## 4. Verdict

**FALSE**: A penny placed on a railroad track will not derail a train. The mass disparity between a 2.5-gram coin and hundreds or thousands of tons of rolling stock makes derailment physically impossible. The coin will be flattened.

**What is true**: Placing objects on railroad tracks is genuinely dangerous — not because of derailment risk, but because people who place objects on tracks are often in dangerously close proximity to operating trains. Active railroad tracks are not safe environments for lingering. The documented casualties from pennying demonstrate real risk to the person, not to the train.

**What is true about actual derailment risks**: Objects substantially larger and more rigid than pennies — bricks, rocks, pieces of rail — can and occasionally do cause derailments. These cases are well-documented by the NTSB and railroad safety investigators. The penny sits far outside the zone of potentially dangerous track objects based on mass and rigidity.

**The legal note**: In many jurisdictions, placing any object on railroad tracks with intent to obstruct a train is a criminal offense regardless of whether derailment occurs. This legal status is independent of the physics. The penny-on-track behavior may expose the practitioner to legal consequences even though the physical consequences for the train are zero.

**Snopes rating**: Snopes rates the claim false and notes that coin-flattening on tracks has never produced a documented derailment incident, while documenting multiple deaths of people who were struck by trains while engaged in the practice.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**Railroad safety culture and the role of myth**: The railroad industry has a long and rich mythology around track safety, partly because railroads have been among the most visible and dramatic sources of accidental death in industrial history. The history of American railroading from the mid-nineteenth century through the early twentieth century is punctuated by massive catastrophic accidents — collisions, derailments, bridge failures — that shaped both safety regulation and popular mythology about railroad danger. In this context, a myth that attributes derailment power to a penny is part of a broader cultural system in which railroads are perceived as quasi-magical in both their power and their vulnerability.

**NTSB records on track-obstruction derailments**: The National Transportation Safety Board maintains detailed records of railroad accidents including derailments from track obstructions. Objects that have been involved in real derailment incidents in NTSB records include large rocks and concrete blocks, steel cable, improperly secured equipment, and on occasion deliberately placed large debris. The mass threshold below which objects cease to pose derailment risk is well below train capacity but well above a penny. No NTSB record exists of a coin — penny, quarter, or other — causing a derailment.

**The physics of commemorative penny-flattening machines**: The widespread legitimate use of coin-flattening machines at tourist destinations worldwide is a revealing counterpoint to the derailment claim. These machines use heavy steel rollers to flatten and emboss pennies into souvenir ovals, and they work precisely because the coin offers essentially no resistance to the applied force. The machine is designed to be reversible — a simple crank mechanism — rather than requiring industrial power; the penny's low resistance confirms that its interaction with any steel roller (including a train wheel) is a one-sided affairs in which the penny absorbs all the deformation.

**Railroad right-of-way as dangerous space**: The actual principal danger of railroad rights-of-way for civilians is not the hypothetical derailment but the documented risk of being struck by trains. Trains are quieter than expected at a distance, can appear suddenly from behind obstructions, travel faster than perceived, and require enormous stopping distances — a freight train traveling at 55 mph may require a mile or more to stop after emergency braking. Trespassing on railroad rights-of-way accounts for the largest category of railroad-related deaths in the United States annually; approximately 900–1,000 people are killed each year by trains in the US, the majority in trespassing incidents (Federal Railroad Administration, 2023, *Rail Safety Statistics*). The penny derailment myth, by redirecting concern from this real risk to a false one, may actually undermine safety by giving the impression that pennies are the source of railroad danger.

**Train flange physics and derailment in real engineering**: In actual railroad engineering, derailment resistance is a precisely studied field. The ratio of lateral wheel force to vertical wheel force — the L/V ratio — is the primary measure of derailment risk, typically studied through the Nadal criterion (Nadal, M.J., 1908, *Bulletin Technique de la Suisse Romande*) which quantifies the wheel-flange force needed to climb a rail. Large lateral forces from track curvature, vehicle dynamics, track defects, or sudden impacts can push the L/V ratio beyond the derailment threshold. A penny on the track produces no meaningful lateral force on the wheel; it has no flange-engagement and its mass is negligible. Real derailment scenarios involve forces measured in kilonewtons, not nanonewtons.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Snopes coverage**: The original Snopes article by Barbara Mikkelson takes an interesting dual approach: debunking the derailment claim while robustly documenting the real casualties from the practice of pennying tracks. This structure implicitly makes an argument that the real danger eclipses the imaginary one, and that fear of the wrong consequence (train derailment) may obscure awareness of the actual lethal consequence (being struck while on tracks). The article names five specific individuals killed in documented incidents — an unusually specific and grim documentation for a "false" ruling.

**Physics educator treatment**: Physics teachers frequently use the penny-on-track question as a force-and-mass thought experiment in mechanics courses, since it requires students to reason about whether the force exerted by the coin on the wheel would be sufficient to displace the wheel laterally. The answer requires basic Newtonian mechanics and an understanding of the train-to-penny mass ratio, making it a useful pedagogical tool for force reasoning at extreme mass disparities.

**Railroad industry's own position**: Class I railroads and organizations like Operation Lifesaver (a railroad safety public education nonprofit) consistently communicate that placing objects on tracks is a Federal crime and a personal safety risk, without amplifying the derailment myth. Their messaging focuses on the physical danger to the person, consistent with the documented casualty record, rather than on any risk to the train.

**The productive false-positive safety logic**: Even knowing the derailment claim is false, fact-checkers generally acknowledge the underlying safety message — stay off active railroad tracks — is correct and important. This creates an unusual situation in which fact-checkers must simultaneously debunk the mechanism and affirm the behavioral conclusion it was designed to produce. The safest outcome for readers is to disbelieve the penny-derailment claim and still believe that railroad tracks are lethal environments where they should not linger or play — for reasons that have nothing to do with derailing trains with coins.

**Confidence level**: High confidence in the false verdict. The physics is unambiguous, the mass ratio is not in dispute, no NTSB record exists of coin-caused derailment, and the real casualties from the practice are documented in court and newspaper records. This is not a case where expert disagreement or contested evidence complicates the picture.
