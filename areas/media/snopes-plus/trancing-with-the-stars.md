# Hypnosis and trance states — can people be hypnotized against their will?


## 1. The Claim

The claim takes several related forms that circulate in popular culture, amateur discussion of psychology, and more recently across social media: that a sufficiently skilled hypnotist can induce a trance state in a willing or unwilling subject; that hypnotic induction can bypass conscious resistance and render a person temporarily controllable; that stage hypnotists "really" hypnotize their volunteers and that the volunteers then execute suggestions they would otherwise refuse; and that trained operatives, therapists, or abusers can use hypnosis to silently and non-consensually control behaviour, implant false memories, or induce amnesia for crimes. In its most dramatic form, the claim holds that individuals can be remotely or covertly hypnotized and made to act against their fundamental interests, values, and will.

This family of claims has a long cultural history that predates experimental psychology by more than a century. The figure of the mesmerist commanding subjects against their will appeared in nineteenth-century fiction well before controlled studies of hypnosis existed, and the archetype has proven sufficiently durable to appear in modern blockbusters, television crime dramas, thriller novels, and a wide genre of conspiracy discourse about mind control. The entertainment industry's hypnotist — dark-eyed, dramatically gesturing, deploying a swinging pocket watch — bears almost no resemblance to the figure described in peer-reviewed clinical or experimental literature, and yet the gap between these two images has never decisively closed in popular understanding.

More concretely, the claim surfaces in specific contexts: concerns about stage hypnotism and whether performers genuinely override volunteers' consent; forensic debates about whether hypnotically recovered "memories" are reliable evidence; therapeutic controversies about whether practitioners can implant false memories of abuse; and wellness-industry claims that particular hypnotic or trance-induction techniques can bypass conscious resistance to install new beliefs and habits. Each of these contexts carries a variation of the core premise: that hypnosis can act on a person without or against their full, ongoing cooperation.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Hypnosis is a real and scientifically studied phenomenon.** The scientific study of hypnosis has a continuous history from the work of Scottish surgeon James Braid, who coined the term "neuro-hypnotism" in 1843 and was among the first to study it physiologically, through to the modern clinical and experimental literature. Hypnosis has been demonstrated to produce genuine and measurable effects on perception, pain experience, and behaviour under controlled laboratory conditions. It is recognised by the American Psychological Association as a legitimate clinical technique with documented efficacy for specific applications including pain management, anxiety, and some medical procedures (Jensen & Patterson, 2014, *Annual Review of Clinical Psychology*; Montgomery et al., 2002, *International Journal of Clinical and Experimental Hypnosis*).

The phenomenology of hypnosis in responsive subjects includes heightened focused attention, reduced peripheral awareness, increased receptiveness to suggestion, and, in some subjects, experiences of involuntariness — the sense that suggested actions are happening "on their own" rather than being deliberately executed. This last feature — the quality of subjective involuntariness — is among the most studied aspects of hypnotic responding and is one source of the popular misunderstanding about compelled behaviour.

**Individual susceptibility varies enormously and is measurable.** Not all people are equally responsive to hypnotic induction. Decades of research using standardised instruments — including the Stanford Hypnotic Susceptibility Scale (Weitzenhoffer & Hilgard, 1959) and its derivatives — have consistently found that hypnotic susceptibility follows an approximately normal distribution in the general population. Approximately 10–15% of adults are highly responsive, experiencing vivid subjective effects, strong dissociation, and reliable behavioural response to formal suggestions. Approximately 20–25% of adults are essentially non-responsive to standard hypnotic induction procedures and show minimal measurable effects regardless of the operator's skill or technique. The majority of people fall in the middle range and show moderate, variable responsiveness depending on context, rapport, and motivation. Hypnotic susceptibility is a relatively stable individual trait, moderately heritable, and correlated with imaginative involvement, absorption, and fantasy proneness (Tellegen & Atkinson, 1974, *Journal of Abnormal Psychology*).

**Consent and cooperation are necessary conditions for hypnotic induction.** The robust finding across experimental hypnosis research is that individuals cannot be hypnotised without their willing participation. The induction process requires the subject to engage — to follow instructions, to direct attention, and to allow suggested experiences to develop. An uncooperative subject who actively resists induction is not hypnotisable by any technique studied under controlled conditions. Hilgard (1977, *Divided Consciousness*, Wiley) acknowledged this explicitly, noting that even the loss of voluntary control that subjects experience in deep hypnosis is itself mediated by an underlying cooperation; the "involuntariness" is a phenomenological quality of an experience the subject is generating, not evidence of extrinsic compulsion.

Attempts to demonstrate covert or non-consensual hypnotic induction under experimentally controlled conditions have not established that it is possible. Research by Orne (1972, *Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences*) found that hypnotic subjects could not be induced to perform actions they found genuinely repugnant even under deep hypnosis, and that apparent compliance with "antisocial" suggestions in laboratory settings was attributable to subjects' trust in the experimental context rather than hypnotic compulsion of behaviour out of character with their values.

**Stage hypnosis involves selection and social dynamics, not compulsion.** Stage hypnotic performances are real in the sense that the performers on stage are generally genuinely responsive to suggestion — because highly suggestible people (roughly the top 15% of susceptibility distribution) are selected through quick screening tests that precede the formal show. Volunteers are not a random sample of the audience; they are self-selected for performance willingness and then further selected by the performer for high responsiveness. The behaviours that stage audiences interpret as impossible to fake — amnesia, hallucinations, apparent age regression — are genuine experiences for highly hypnotisable subjects, not consciously faked compliance. But the interpretation that the subject's autonomy has been overridden is incorrect: the subjects are cooperating with the performance context and experiencing its suggestions as compelling because they are constitutionally inclined to do so. Research by Lynn, Rhue, and Weekes (1990, *Psychological Bulletin*) established that context, demand characteristics, and expectation play significant roles in determining stage hypnotic responding.

**Memory implantation via hypnosis is a real concern, but not in the covert-control direction.** The scientific literature on hypnosis and memory does document a genuine risk: hypnotic procedures increase susceptibility to suggestion and can produce false memories that the subject later experiences as genuine recollections. This is not a mechanism for covert mind control but rather a concern about the reliability of "hypnotically enhanced" memory in forensic and therapeutic settings. Loftus and colleagues established that memory is reconstructive and that suggestion during hypnosis can alter subsequent recall (Loftus & Pickrell, 1995, *Psychiatric Annals*). The American Psychological Association's Division of Psychological Hypnosis and multiple professional bodies have issued formal guidance discouraging the use of hypnosis for memory "recovery" in forensic contexts precisely because of this susceptibility to suggestion-contamination of recollection — a risk that runs in the opposite direction from the popular claim of covert control.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The phenomenology of involuntariness.** The single most powerful mechanism driving belief in against-the-will hypnosis is the genuine phenomenological experience of high-susceptibility subjects who describe their hypnotic responding as involuntary. When a deeply hypnotised subject reports that their arm "floated up on its own" or that they "couldn't remember" their own name despite knowing it, the interpersonal inference is almost irresistible: something external is controlling them. This quality — called the "classic suggestion effect" in the research literature — is a real feature of hypnotic responding in susceptible people, not a performance. The problem is one of interpretation: the subjective involuntariness is generated by the subject's own cognitive architecture, not by the operator's power over them. The experience of "not being in control" does not entail "being controlled by someone else against one's will," but the former is intuitively read as evidence for the latter, particularly by observers rather than participants.

**Dramatic media and the stage archetype.** The entertainment industry's representation of hypnosis has been relentlessly inaccurate in a consistent direction: the all-powerful hypnotist who "breaks" resistant subjects, overrides refusals, and produces apparently impossible compliance. From the nineteenth-century stage mesmerist through Svengali in du Maurier's 1894 novel *Trilby* — which did more to fix the controlling-hypnotist archetype in popular culture than any scientific publication — to contemporary film, the figure of the irresistible hypnotic operator has been narratively compelling precisely because it concentrates autonomous agency in a single powerful figure. Psychological research finds that prior exposure to dramatic depictions of hypnosis shapes subjects' expectations and, to some extent, their responses to hypnotic induction — the "demand characteristic" effect (Kirsch & Lynn, 1995, *Psychological Bulletin*). This creates a feedback loop: fictional representations create expectations that modestly influence actual hypnotic experience, which then retrospectively appears to validate the fictional representations.

**Covert persuasion anxiety and the NLP industry.** The past four decades have seen a substantial industry grow around what is sometimes called "conversational hypnosis," "covert persuasion," and Neuro-Linguistic Programming (NLP), which variously claim to provide techniques for inducing trance states in unsuspecting subjects, bypassing "critical faculty," and installing suggestions that influence behaviour without the subject's awareness. These frameworks are not supported by the peer-reviewed literature — NLP in particular has been evaluated in multiple systematic reviews and found to lack empirical support for its core claims (Heap, 2008, *The Skeptical Intelligencer*) — but they are commercially successful and widely disseminated. The existence of a large and commercially active industry claiming that hypnotic-style techniques can control people non-consensually provides ongoing social evidence for the claim, independent of scientific evaluation.

**Association with known coercive influence phenomena.** Social influence techniques that genuinely reduce autonomous decision-making capacity — sleep deprivation, social isolation, intense emotional manipulation, love bombing, and the group dynamics observed in high-control religious organisations — are sometimes described in popular and journalistic accounts as "hypnotic" or "trance-inducing." This metaphorical usage, while intuitively resonant, creates conceptual conflation: readers reasonably infer that if "hypnosis" metaphorically describes the coercive group dynamics that do genuinely subvert autonomy, then literal hypnosis must be capable of similar effects. The mechanism driving belief is analogical reasoning from a genuinely effective coercive phenomenon to a speculative claim about the coercive power of formal hypnotic induction.


## 4. Verdict

**False as stated in its strong form; the underlying phenomena are real but mischaracterised.** People cannot be hypnotised against their will under conditions that the scientific literature recognises as hypnotic induction. The requirements for effective hypnosis — willing participation, attentional cooperation, and susceptibility — exclude covert or non-consensual induction by definition. Attempts to demonstrate otherwise under controlled conditions have failed.

The genuine phenomena are: (1) that hypnosis is a real psychological state with measurable and sometimes dramatic effects in susceptible individuals; (2) that the subjective quality of involuntariness in hypnotic responding is a real phenomenological feature, not performance; (3) that hypnotic suggestion can contaminate subsequent memory; and (4) that significant individual variation in susceptibility means that some people find hypnotic induction profoundly compelling while others experience nothing.

None of these genuine findings support the popular claim that an operator can override a subject's will, compel behaviour contrary to fundamental values, or induce trance in someone who is not cooperating. Stage performances select for high susceptibility and operate within a strongly demand-characteristic performance context. Clinical hypnosis is a collaborative procedure. "Conversational hypnosis" and covert induction claims are not empirically established.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The belief in against-the-will hypnosis is a striking example of how a genuine psychological phenomenon becomes distorted when filtered through cultural narrative. The phenomenon of hypnotic suggestibility — real, measurable, clinically useful — becomes transformed in popular imagination into its most dramatic possible version: a technology of compelled obedience.

This transformation matters beyond the fact-checking context because it has concrete consequences. Forensic contexts in which hypnotically "recovered" memories have been admitted as evidence have, in well-documented cases, contributed to wrongful convictions: subjects were not covertly controlled, but they were genuinely susceptible to suggestion, and the evidence produced under hypnosis was taken as more reliable than the research warrants (Loftus & Ketcham, 1994, *The Myth of Repressed Memory*, St. Martin's Press). The misunderstanding here runs not in the direction of "hypnotists can control people" but in the direction of "hypnotic testimony is high-fidelity recall" — a different error from the popular will-override claim but arising from the same general misunderstanding of what hypnosis does.

In the therapeutic domain, the widespread belief in hypnosis as a route to bypassing "resistant" clients' critical faculties has encouraged practitioners to use it as a technique for installing beliefs the client has not explicitly consented to — on the premise that conscious resistance is an obstacle to valid treatment rather than a legitimate response. This therapeutic overreach is not supported by the clinical efficacy literature and creates genuine risks of harm (Lynn et al., 1996, *Psychological Science*).

Conversely, legitimate clinical hypnotherapy has faced unnecessary scepticism and cultural resistance in part because of the association with dramatic stage performances and mind-control fiction. Pain management applications of hypnosis — for which there is reasonable controlled evidence, including Montgomery et al.'s meta-analysis (2002) — are sometimes dismissed or distrusted by patients and healthcare providers who associate hypnosis with trickery or compulsion.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This family of claims is not prominently addressed on major fact-checking platforms, partly because it does not attach to a single current news event and partly because it has historically been treated as a psychology or science communication question rather than a media misinformation problem. Snopes has not published a dedicated entry on hypnotic will-override; PolitiFact's scope does not extend to psychological claims of this type in most circumstances.

The most rigorous assessments have appeared in academic psychology journals and in science communication contexts. The American Psychological Association's Division of Psychological Hypnosis publishes educational statements on what hypnosis is and is not. APA has explicitly addressed the forensic memory-recovery issue in formal position papers. The Society for Clinical and Experimental Hypnosis similarly publishes guidance that consistently refutes the coercive control narrative.

For fact-checkers approaching this type of claim, the appropriate methodology involves: (1) sourcing claims about hypnotic capabilities against the peer-reviewed experimental literature, not stage performance accounts or commercial self-help frameworks; (2) distinguishing between the measurable effects of hypnosis in cooperative willing subjects and the claimed ability to override unwilling subjects; (3) identifying the demand-characteristic and selection effects that explain stage performance apparent without requiring compulsion; and (4) noting the forensic memory-contamination risk as a genuine concern that moves in the opposite direction from the compelled-control narrative.

The deeper media literacy principle is that real psychological phenomena are frequently more interesting and more nuanced than the dramatic versions that circulate culturally, and that translation from technical research finding to popular claim almost always involves exaggeration of the mechanism of effect — the leap from "this is a powerful influence on willing subjects" to "this can override the unwilling" is a recurring pattern in psychological misinformation, visible equally in debates about subliminal advertising, lie detectors, and graphological personality assessment.
