## The Claim

In December 2020, an image circulated widely on social media platforms — including Reddit's r/interestingasfuck and r/NatureIsFuckingLit communities, as well as on Twitter and Facebook — showing what appeared to be a transparent or near-transparent frog. The animal's internal organs were visible through its skin. Its heart, liver, and digestive tract could be seen as shadows or distinct structures through what appeared to be a glass-like integument. The image provoked genuine astonishment and the familiar cascade of questions: Is this real? Is this a digital manipulation? Is this a photograph of a real animal?

The claim, as evaluated by Snopes journalist Dan Evon on December 12, 2020, was simply: "A photograph shows a 'transparent' frog." Snopes rated it True.

The frog is a real animal. The photograph is genuine. The transparency is not digital manipulation. The animal belongs to the family Centrolenidae, commonly known as glass frogs — a group of approximately 156 described species distributed throughout Central and South America, with additional undescribed species awaiting formal classification. The specific image in question was identified by its photographer, Jaime Culebras, as a *Hyalinobatrachium valerioi*, photographed at the Mashpi Reserve in Ecuador.

But the Snopes True rating, accurate as it is, is only the beginning of a genuinely interesting scientific story. Glass frogs are unusual not merely in their appearance but in the specific biological mechanisms that produce their transparency, the evolutionary functions those mechanisms may serve, and the active research programme that was — in 2020, precisely when this viral image circulated — producing new findings about how and why transparency evolved in this family.

## What's Actually True

The photograph is genuine. The transparency is real. And the biological explanation for the transparency is considerably richer than the viral framing suggests.

**The glass frog family.** The family Centrolenidae comprises small arboreal frogs found entirely in the Neotropical region — from southern Mexico through Central America and throughout South America. They are typically tiny, ranging from approximately 2 to 8 centimetres in length, and spend their lives in the canopy and mid-storey vegetation of humid tropical forests, often near streams where they breed. Males of many species guard egg clutches deposited on leaves overhanging water, a behaviour that gives observers an opportunity to observe their transparency from below.

The defining characteristic of the family — the feature that gives them the common name "glass frogs" — is the translucent or transparent ventral skin. In the most fully developed cases, particularly in the genus *Hyalinobatrachium* (to which the photographed species belongs), the ventral skin has almost no pigmentation, and the animal's organs — heart, liver, digestive tract, and often developing eggs in gravid females — are clearly visible when the frog is viewed from below. The dorsal (top) surface of the frog is typically green, ranging from uniform leaf green to spotted or mottled patterns depending on species, and appears entirely normal from above: these frogs look more or less like standard small green tree frogs when seen from above or from the side.

The photographer, Jaime Culebras, is a professional wildlife photographer specialising in Neotropical amphibians and herpetology outreach. In his September 2020 Instagram post identifying the species, Culebras provided taxonomic context: "With 156 species described and more than 50 undescribed, #centrolenidae, known as #glassfrogs, are fascinating because their varied dorsal patterns. However, its most notable characteristic is the ventral #transparency of some species, especially those of the genus '*#Hyalinobatrachium*'. Other species, such as those of the genus '*#Nymphargus*', don't have this peculiarity as well developed, but they are no less impressive for that."

**The camouflage function.** In May 2020 — seven months before the viral circulation of the Culebras image — a research team published findings on the function of glass frog translucency in *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*. The research, led by James Barnett of McMaster University's Department of Psychology, Neuroscience, and Behaviour, with co-authors from the University of Bristol, confirmed through experimental and field-observational methods that the glass frog's translucency functions as a form of camouflage — but through a mechanism that had not been previously documented.

The mechanism, described as "edge diffusion," works as follows. Glass frogs at rest on leaves hold their limbs folded close to their bodies. The outer portions of the limbs — the extremities, the parts closest to the leaf surface — are more translucent than the central body. This gradient of translucency, from somewhat opaque at the body centre to near-transparent at the limb margins, creates a gradual visual transition between the frog's green body colouration and the green leaf background. Rather than a sharp, salient edge (the edge of an opaque frog body against a leaf surface), predators looking at the frog see a diffuse gradient — the frog appears to blend into the leaf at its periphery.

As Barnett explained to the University of Bristol press office: "The frogs are always green but appear to brighten and darken depending on the background. This change in brightness makes the frogs a closer match to their immediate surroundings, which are predominantly made up of green leaves. We also found that the legs are more translucent than the body and so when the legs are held tucked to the frog's sides at rest, this creates a diffuse gradient from leaf colour to frog colour rather than a more salient sharp edge. This suggests a novel form of camouflage: 'edge diffusion.'"

The discovery of edge diffusion as a specific camouflage strategy was genuinely novel in 2020 and represents the epistemic core of this story: the glass frog's transparency is not simply a curiosity or an incomplete evolutionary development — it is a functional mechanism that confers survival advantage through a previously undescribed optical principle. Understanding it illuminates not just glass frog biology but theoretical questions about how camouflage can operate through translucency rather than colouration matching, with implications for broader work on animal optics.

## Why People Believe This

The category of disbelief — "this can't be real" — that greeted the glass frog image is one of the more philosophically interesting responses to encounter in the viral wildlife image genre. The image is real; the animal exists; and yet the response "this must be fake" is predictable and in some sense understandable.

**Biological intuitions about opacity.** Vertebrate bodies are opaque. This is such a consistent biological fact in the everyday experience of humans and the animals humans most commonly encounter (mammals, birds, most other large vertebrates) that it operates as something close to a categorical constraint in biological intuition: animals have skin, skin is opaque, and the contents of bodies are therefore hidden. The glass frog violates this assumption dramatically. Its ventral transparency makes visible things that should, by the general rule, be out of sight. The initial response of incredulity is, in this sense, a correct recognition that something genuinely unusual is happening — the disbelief is not the result of limited knowledge but of accurate pattern recognition that something anomalous has been presented.

**The manipulation default.** As with the fluffy cow, the glass frog image circulated in a media environment increasingly saturated with digitally manipulated or synthesised imagery. By December 2020, AI-generated imagery and deepfake technology had produced a background condition of uncertainty about visual evidence. A frog with visible organs is exactly the kind of image that might be created by someone with competent photo-editing software: select a frog image, apply transparency to the ventral surface, add organ-shaped objects as layers beneath. The technical plausibility of faking this image is real, and the suspicion of manipulation is therefore epistemically reasonable as a prior assumption. The fact that the image is genuine does not mean the scepticism was irrational — it means the evidence that grounds the scepticism was overcounted relative to the evidence of authenticity.

**Partial knowledge of biological transparency.** A viewer with some biological education might be aware that *some* organisms are transparent — jellyfish, certain larval fish, specific deep-sea invertebrates, glass eels — while being entirely unaware that vertebrates closely related to frogs (a familiar animal) share this property. The taxonomic distance between "glass-bodied invertebrate" and "glass-bodied frog" is, in biological terms, quite large: the evolution of transparency in a small arboreal vertebrate with green pigmented skin is a specifically unusual outcome that doesn't follow from general knowledge of biological transparency. This partial knowledge creates a gap that is filled by disbelief rather than correct inference.

**The "you can see its organs" quality.** The specific detail that makes the glass frog image phenomenologically arresting is not simply that the frog is pale or translucent — it is that the animal's internal organs are distinctly visible. The visibility of another creature's organs activates a psychological register associated with vulnerability, medical imaging, and the interior of the body as normally-hidden. Research on disgust and biological sensitivity — including work by Rozin, Haidt, and McCauley (Rozin et al., 2008, *American Psychologist*) on disgust as a moral emotion — suggests that visible viscera reliably activates both curiosity and a mild version of the disgust-adjacent response to bodily exposure. The glass frog image is fascinating precisely because it makes visible something that the general biological rule keeps hidden.

**The epistemic novelty of the optical mechanism.** One of the reasons the glass frog story is genuinely more interesting than a simple "yes it's real" verdict suggests is that the specific camouflage mechanism — edge diffusion, as documented by Barnett et al. (2020, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*) — was unknown to science before 2020. The timing of the viral image's circulation (December 2020) seven months after the publication of the research on edge diffusion is an interesting coincidence. The public was encountering the image, and asking whether it was real, at the same moment that scientists had just published novel findings on how the frog's visual strategy works. But because science communication about Barnett et al. 2020 had limited reach outside specialist channels, almost none of the social media viewers encountering the viral image had access to the newly published research. This represents a genuine gap between scientific knowledge and public knowledge.

## Verdict

**The claim is true.** The photograph shows a real animal — a glass frog of the species *Hyalinobatrachium valerioi* — captured by wildlife photographer Jaime Culebras at the Mashpi Reserve in Ecuador. The animal's transparency is genuinely biological, not the product of digital manipulation. The transparency is characteristic of the genus *Hyalinobatrachium* and reflects a functional camouflage strategy confirmed by Barnett et al. (2020, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*) to operate through "edge diffusion," a novel optical principle in animal camouflage research.

Snopes rated this claim **True**.

## The Wider Picture

The glass frog is an entry point into some of the most interesting current questions in animal optics, evolutionary biology, and the science of transparency in living systems.

**The taxonomy of Centrolenidae.** The family Centrolenidae has been the subject of substantial taxonomic revision in the late 20th and early 21st centuries, as molecular phylogenetics has clarified evolutionary relationships within the group. The family is now understood to comprise three subfamilies: Centroleninae, Hyalinobatrachinae, and Allophryninae. The genus *Hyalinobatrachium* — the "true" glass frogs, whose transparency is most fully developed — is the type genus for Hyalinobatrachinae and comprises approximately 33 described species distributed from Costa Rica to Bolivia. A 2020 description by Guayasamin et al. (2020, *ZooKeys*) formalised the description of *Hyalinobatrachium yaku*, the species shown in the Snopes article's header image, as a species new to science — discovered in the Ecuadorian Amazon and immediately threatened by oil pipeline development in its habitat.

The discovery of new glass frog species in the 21st century reflects both the wealth of undescribed biodiversity remaining in Neotropical forests and the specific challenge of discovering small, cryptically coloured arboreal amphibians in dense forest canopy. Glass frogs' effective camouflage — the characteristic that makes them such interesting subjects for optics research — simultaneously makes them difficult to survey and count. The number of undescribed Centrolenidae species (Culebras estimated more than 50 in his Instagram post) reflects this challenge, though molecular sequencing techniques are accelerating species delimitation.

**The biology of transparency in vertebrates.** The glass frog's rarity as a transparent vertebrate deserves emphasis. Among vertebrates — the group including fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals — transparency is very uncommon in adults. Larval stages of many species (glass eels, marine fish larvae) are transparent, but adult transparency is extremely rare. Two main examples exist: some deep-sea fish have evolved structural transparency involving extremely thin, highly modified scales or tissue layers; and glass frogs, uniquely among terrestrial vertebrates, have evolved functional transparency through mechanisms involving reduced pigmentation in ventral skin.

Research by Taboada et al. (2017, *PNAS*) on glass frog camouflage physiology examined the spectral properties of glass frog skin and confirmed that the ventral skin of *Hyalinobatrachium* species transmits visible light at high levels across the visible spectrum while maintaining green pigmentation on the dorsal surface. The mechanism involves both the absence of melanin-producing cells (melanophores) in the ventral skin and the presence of reflecting cells (iridophores) that contribute to the overall optical properties of the integument. The interplay between transmissive and reflective elements in the skin is more complex than simple unpigmented transparency would suggest.

**Edge diffusion as a general principle.** The edge diffusion mechanism documented by Barnett et al. (2020) in glass frogs has implications beyond frog biology. If translucency at the body margins can reduce visual salience by creating diffuse transitions between organism and background, this represents a fundamentally different mode of camouflage from the two previously well-documented modes: background matching (resembling the substrate in colour, pattern, and brightness) and disruptive colouration (using high-contrast markings to break up the body outline). Edge diffusion operates not by matching the background at the body surface but by creating a gradient of visual transition that makes the boundary between organism and background difficult to localise.

Whether edge diffusion operates in other organisms with translucent margins — certain insects, some marine invertebrates, specific fish species with translucent fins — is an open research question raised implicitly by the Barnett et al. findings. The general principle, once identified, invites comparative investigation. It also has potential applications in biomimetic engineering: materials that create diffuse transitions between objects and backgrounds, inspired by glass frog translucency, could have applications in optical camouflage design, sensor concealment, and visual display technology.

**Photography and science communication.** Jaime Culebras's role in this story is worth acknowledging specifically. Wildlife photographers working in biodiversity-rich but under-documented regions serve as de facto science communicators — their images reach public audiences that academic papers never reach, and they frequently provide the first widely distributed visual documentation of species or behaviours that are known to specialists but unknown to general audiences. Culebras's Instagram post identifying the species in his photograph, providing taxonomic context, and explaining the unusual feature of the image's genus demonstrates the kind of scientific literacy in wildlife photography that transforms singular images into genuine public education.

The fact that a wildlife photographer's Instagram post provided the primary identification evidence used by Snopes to authenticate the viral image reflects the evolving ecology of science communication in the social media era. Taxonomic identification, which previously required specialist consultation through academic or museum channels, can now be provided directly by experts working in the field through public social media platforms, with immediate accessibility. This democratisation of expertise has genuine epistemic value — though it also carries risks, as misidentification by apparently authoritative sources can propagate as readily as correct identification.

**The Mashpi Reserve context.** The location of Culebras's photograph — the Mashpi Reserve in northwestern Ecuador — is itself significant. The Mashpi area, part of the Chocó-Darien bioregion in the western slopes of the Andes, is recognised as one of the highest-biodiversity zones on Earth and one of the most threatened. The Chocó-Darien is a globally recognised biodiversity hotspot — a region of exceptional species richness and endemism facing severe habitat loss through deforestation. Research by Myers et al. (2000, *Nature*) identifying and ranking global biodiversity hotspots listed the Chocó-Darien among the most important priority areas for conservation. The glass frogs photographed by Culebras at Mashpi Reserve exist within this broader context of concentrated biodiversity under significant pressure.

Conservation of glass frog habitat is therefore not simply an issue of preserving photogenic small amphibians for social media — it is a component of the larger challenge of maintaining Neotropical biodiversity in the face of land conversion, agricultural expansion, and mineral extraction. The virality of the glass frog image, and the interest it generated, is in a small way ecologically useful: it draws public attention to a taxonomic group and a geographic region that otherwise receive limited prominence in mainstream conservation discourse.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Dan Evon's Snopes fact-check on the glass frog is a clean example of the authentication-and-context model.

**Species identification as the key verification step.** The critical step in this fact-check is connecting the viral image to a specific identified source — Culebras's September 2020 Instagram post — which identifies the species, the location, and the photographer, and provides expert taxonomic context. Without this connection, the fact-check could confirm only that glass frogs exist as a general category; with it, the specific image is directly authenticated and explained.

**The viewing-angle caveat.** Evon notes that glass frog transparency is primarily visible from below — from the ventral side — and that these frogs appear as relatively normal green frogs when viewed from above or other angles. This caveat is important for accurately characterising the extent of the transparency: the frog is not transparent in all directions, which means the viral image (showing the ventral view with organs visible) represents a specific perspective rather than the animal's general appearance. The header image of the Snopes article shows a *Hyalinobatrachium yaku* from above, appearing as an ordinary small green frog — providing the visual contrast that makes the ventral transparency more comprehensible.

**The research citation.** The fact-check includes a quote from researcher James Barnett about the edge diffusion camouflage mechanism, citing the University of Bristol press office coverage of the May 2020 research. The inclusion of the scientific research context is important: it transforms the fact-check from "yes, this weird frog is real" to "this weird frog is real, and here is why it has this property." The functional explanation elevates the article from authentication to scientific literacy.

**What the "True" verdict covers.** The claim being rated True is "a photograph shows a 'transparent' frog." This is confirmed with precision: the photograph is genuine, the frog is transparent (ventrally), and the specific species and location are identified. The broader scientific context — the taxonomy, the edge diffusion research, the conservation status of the habitat — extends beyond the narrow claim but is clearly relevant for a full understanding of what has been confirmed.

Glass frogs are one of those animals that genuinely exceed their initial impression. A first seeing is arresting: that cannot be real. A second seeing, with understanding: the translucent limbs fading at the edges precisely to dissolve the boundary between frog and leaf; the organs glowing greenish through the ventral skin; the dorsal surface a perfect leaf green from above. This is not illusion but biology — the product of evolution solving the problem of being a small, soft-bodied animal in a world of predators, through means that required millions of years to perfect and that scientists only fully articulated in 2020.
