# Are These Real Tree-Climbing Goats in Morocco?

**Verdict: True — and the science behind why they climb, how they help (and hurt) the trees, and what it means for the argan forest is far richer than the photographs suggest.**

The image is one of the more reliably marvellous on the internet: a cluster of goats, their hooves planted with apparent insouciance on branches several metres above the ground, grazing from a gnarled, silver-barked tree. For most viewers encountering this photograph for the first time, the question is immediately whether it is real. It is. But knowing that the image is genuine is the beginning of the story, not the end.

---

## 1. The Claim

The claim investigated by Snopes in July 2021 was made in meme form: the argan tree in Morocco produces an irresistible fruit that goats will climb onto its branches to reach. As rated by Snopes journalist Nur Ibrahim (published July 3, 2021), the verdict is True. The viral photographs are genuine and document a well-attested ecological behavior. The trees are real argan trees (*Argania spinosa*), the animals are domestic goats (*Capra hircus*), and their arboreal foraging is a confirmed phenomenon with its own peer-reviewed scientific literature.

---

## 2. The Biology of the Argan Tree and Goat Foraging

### Argania spinosa: the tree

*Argania spinosa* is the sole species in the genus *Argania* and is endemic to southwestern Morocco and a small area of western Algeria. It is a relict species — that is, a survivor of a once-wider distribution that contracted as North African climates dried during the Holocene. The tree grows slowly to heights of eight to ten metres, is extraordinarily drought-tolerant, and lives for centuries; individuals of 200 to 450 years have been documented. Its gnarled, thorny branches and deep root systems are adapted to the semi-arid conditions of the Sous-Massa-Drâa plain of Morocco, where annual rainfall averages between 100 and 300 millimetres.

The argan fruit ripens in June, summer coming early to this climate. It resembles, as National Geographic has described it, a shriveled olive in shape and color when ripe, though it is larger — roughly the size of a large olive. The fruit's outer flesh (pericarp) is edible but astringent; within it is a hard, thick-shelled nut that is nearly indigestible by most animals without extended mechanical or chemical processing.

### Why goats climb

Goats are among the most agile large terrestrial herbivores, with a well-developed sense of balance and hoof anatomy suited to gripping irregular surfaces. Their two-toed hooves have a hard outer edge and softer inner pads that together provide friction on rocky, angled, or branching substrates. In the Moroccan context, goats discovered that argan branches — which can be thick, widely spaced, and relatively horizontal — can support their weight, and that the fruit at their tips is worth the effort.

The climbing is typically collective: where one goat ventures, others follow, and photographs often show ten or more animals distributed across a single tree. The behavior is observed annually in the ripening season and is sufficiently common that it has been documented repeatedly by photographers, journalists, and scientists.

### The argan nut and argan oil

At the center of the argan fruit is a woody nut containing two or three seeds rich in oleic and linoleic fatty acids. Cold-pressing or hot-pressing these seeds produces argan oil, which has been used by Moroccan Berber communities for centuries as a food oil (in the culinary variant) and a cosmetic oil (in the cosmetic variant). By the early 21st century, argan oil had become one of the most expensive edible oils in the world, driven by European and North American demand for its distinctive flavor profile in food and its purported moisturizing and anti-aging properties in cosmetics. A kilogram of seeds requires approximately 30 kilograms of fruit, and processing is extraordinarily labor-intensive, contributing to prices that have reached $300 per litre in premium markets.

## 3. Epistemic Novelty: The Goats as Seed Dispersers, and the Ecology of Accidental Mutualism

Among the most scientifically interesting dimensions of the tree-climbing goat phenomenon is research published after the behavior became widely known that demonstrated the goats are not merely consumers of the argan harvest but are themselves agents in the argan tree's reproductive ecology — and that this role is far more ecologically complex than popular coverage suggests.

**The seed dispersal discovery.** A 2017 study titled "Tree-climbing goats disperse seeds during rumination" by Fedriani et al., published in *Current Biology* (Fedriani, J.M., Zywiec, M., & Delibes, M., 2017, *Current Biology* 27, R219–R220), established through field observation and fecal analysis that goats do not simply excrete the argan nuts after digesting the fruit. Goats, like other ruminants, regurgitate and re-chew their food through a process known as rumination. During this process, intact argan seeds — which had survived passage through the rumen — are expectorated (spat out) by the goats, often at some distance from where the fruit was originally consumed.

This finding was surprising because standard models of seed dispersal in ruminants assumed that seeds were either passed through the full digestive tract (endozoochory) or regurgitated quickly in an undigested form. The *Current Biology* paper demonstrated a third pathway: seeds travel into the rumen, undergo partial processing that weakens their seed coat (potentially improving germination rates), and are then "spit out" during rumination hours later — sometimes after the goat has moved considerable distance from the parent tree. The maximum dispersal distance recorded in the study was over 360 metres, which is substantial for a plant that would otherwise rely on gravity and gravity-assisted rolling (i.e., ballochory or barochory) for seed movement.

**The paradox of the disperser-destroyer.** Fedriani's findings, discussed with NPR's Goats and Soda program (2017), revealed a fundamental ecological paradox. The same goats that disperse argan seeds far from parent trees — potentially critical for forest regeneration, particularly as climate change pushes argan habitat toward dryness — also consume the seedlings that germinate from those seeds. When goat density is low or moderate, the dispersal effect can outweigh the browsing effect: the seeds spat out at distance germinate into seedlings beyond the range of the densest grazing, producing new trees. When goat density is high, however, the goats eat every seedling as it emerges, consuming even those dispersed at a distance, and the net effect on argan forest regeneration is negative.

Fedriani described this dynamic to NPR (2017): "If we have a huge number of goats, they eat all the new baby trees, they kill them, so even though the goats are dispersing the seeds, they are eating the baby trees. So the effect is negative. But if there is a small or moderate number of goats, they will not kill all the baby trees produced by the dispersed seeds."

**The economic feedback loop.** The most troubling dimension of this research is the economic incentive structure that drives the balance toward destruction. Argan oil's international price premium creates strong financial incentives for Moroccan farmers to acquire more goats, since the goats consume the argan fruit and their gut processing of the nuts (traditionally collected from goat dung as well as directly fallen fruit) is part of the traditional argan oil production chain. More goats mean more access to nuts. But more goats also mean more browsing pressure on the seedlings that would regenerate the argan forest. As Fedriani noted: "These people who take care of the argan trees get money from selling the argan fruit, so they buy more goats. So there is no rejuvenation of the argan forest because the goats are eating all the baby trees."

This is a textbook example of the "tragedy of the commons" dynamic in a resource system driven by external demand: the international luxury market for argan oil creates microeconomic pressures that undermine the long-term sustainability of the ecological system that produces it. The goats are simultaneously the symbol of the argan tree's fascinating ecology and the agent of its potential destruction.

## 4. The Argan Forest Under Pressure

The argan tree's ecological precariousness is not limited to goat browsing pressure. Multiple converging stressors threaten the long-term viability of what UNESCO designated as a Biosphere Reserve in 1998 — the Arganeraie Biosphere Reserve of southwestern Morocco.

**Climate change.** Barrowclough et al. (2020, *Global Change Biology*) modeled the effects of projected warming and drying under IPCC scenarios on the distribution of *Argania spinosa* and found that by 2080, under moderate warming scenarios, suitable habitat for argan trees could contract by 20–40 percent. Under high-end warming scenarios, the contraction was more severe, with some models indicating that core current habitat would become climatically unsuitable. Argan trees are drought-tolerant but not drought-immune; they require a minimum precipitation threshold for seedling establishment. Adult trees can survive extended dry periods that kill seedlings — meaning that even if adult trees persist, the generation replacing them may never establish.

**Over-harvesting.** The dramatic global demand for argan oil since the 1990s has transformed what was a subsistence-scale traditional industry into a globalized commodity market. Lybbert et al. (2002, *World Development*) analyzed the economic structure of the argan industry in the early expansion phase and documented the tension between income benefits to rural Moroccan women (who perform the seed-cracking and oil-pressing work through cooperatives) and unsustainable extraction pressure on the tree population. Subsequent research confirmed that in some areas, the transition from local to export-market scale had outpaced any conservation management.

---

## 5. Cultural and Economic Context: The Argan Cooperative System

The argan oil industry is also a notable story in development economics and feminist economic empowerment. Since the 1990s, international NGOs, the Moroccan government, and foreign investment have supported the establishment of women's cooperatives in the Sous-Massa-Drâa region as the primary producers of commercial-grade argan oil. Charrouf et al. (2002, *Biochemical Society Transactions*) documented the nutritional composition and economic value of argan oil while also discussing the role of Berber women in traditional extraction, noting that the hand-labor intensity of the process (estimated at 15–20 hours of labor per litre of oil) made it unsuitable for large-scale mechanization and thus inherently tied to local human skill.

The cooperative model, when properly implemented, can provide living-wage employment to women who would otherwise have few income options in rural Morocco. But it also creates the feedback loop described above: higher oil prices justify higher labor investment, which requires more raw material, which requires more goats or more intensive nut collection — increasing the pressure on the argan forest.

The viral image of goats in argan trees thus sits at the intersection of multiple systems: global luxury consumption, agricultural ecology, climate vulnerability, feminist development economics, and the strange adaptive ecology of a relict tree species. The meme framing presents it as whimsy. The underlying reality is considerably more textured.

---

## 6. Verdict and Significance

The Snopes verdict of True is correct. The photographs of goats in argan trees are genuine, the behavior is well-documented, and the claim as stated — that argan trees produce irresistible fruit that goats will climb to reach — is accurate. Nothing about these images is staged, manipulated, or exaggerated.

The deeper significance of this fact-check lies in what is stripped away by meme-ification. The images circulate as curiosities — implausible animals doing implausible things. They do not circulate as images of an endangered ecosystem under economic and climatic stress, as evidence of a paradoxical seed-dispersal mechanism discovered only in 2017, or as a window into the lives of Moroccan women whose livelihoods depend on a tree these same goats may eventually destroy through overgrazing.

That gap — between the visual fact and its full context — is where the epistemic work of this Truth Vault entry is seated. The goats in the trees are not merely amusing. They are participants in a fragile and complicated relationship whose outcome is genuinely uncertain.
