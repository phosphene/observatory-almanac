# "Does a Tree With Darker Leaves Than Neighboring Ones Mean a Body Is Buried Nearby?"


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: A widely circulated internet meme — originating from the meme-generating site iFunny and re-shared across multiple platforms beginning around September 2020 — features a photograph of a tree trunk accompanied by the text: "A tree with darker leaves than the surrounding trees can indicate that a body is buried beneath it." The implicit inference is that this is a reliable or at least plausible visual indicator: that the presence of a decomposing human body in or near a tree's root zone produces a detectable darkening of the tree's foliage visible to naked-eye observation.

**What "darker leaves" implies**: The claim as circulated suggests a direct, observable, practically useful signal — something a passerby, hiker, or investigator could notice in a forest or park: a single tree whose leaves are visibly darker than those of neighboring trees of the same species under the same ambient light conditions. The implication is a causal mechanism: decomposing human remains → chemical release into soil → tree uptake → leaf color change visible to observers.

**Context of circulation**: The meme gained significant traction as a piece of ostensibly educational content framed with the register of "weird science facts." It circulated on platforms like Reddit, Twitter, TikTok, and Facebook alongside other "things you didn't know" style claims — facts about the natural world that are surprising, creepy, or counterintuitive. Readers who encountered it typically expressed either fascinated belief ("I never knew this") or amused skepticism ("this sounds like something someone made up"). The claim's content — combining forensic imagery, the macabre, and botany — gives it immediate emotional salience.

**The reader reaction that prompted the Snopes investigation**: Snopes's investigation was triggered by a reader who submitted the meme for investigation, noting "sounds like bunk." The Snopes investigator's initial assessment agreed — and yet the subsequent research revealed a more nuanced situation than simple debunking would produce. The Snopes verdict of "Unproven" reflects genuine complexity in the underlying science, not a conclusion that the meme is obviously true.

**Scope of claimed practical utility**: Some versions of the claim frame it in explicitly practical law enforcement terms — that investigators searching for missing persons could use aerial observation of tree color variation to locate buried remains that would be invisible to ground-level searches. This frames the claim not just as a botanical curiosity but as an operational tool. It is this stronger, operational version of the claim that bears most directly on the question of truth.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The scientific question is real and being actively researched**: The most important preliminary finding in this investigation is that the general question — whether plants near decomposing human remains show detectable changes in their physical characteristics — is a legitimate scientific hypothesis being pursued by real researchers with proper methodology. This is not a case where the Snopes investigation found that the claim has no scientific basis whatsoever; rather, it found that the claim has a serious scientific basis, but that the research is preliminary and the evidence does not yet support the specific confident assertion made in the meme.

**The Body Farm research program**: The University of Tennessee's Forensic Anthropology Center, colloquially known as "The Body Farm," has operated since 1980 as the foremost research facility for studying human cadaver decomposition under naturalistic field conditions. Scientists at the Body Farm study how donated human cadavers decompose across various environmental conditions, burial types, and timescales. The research program has produced foundational forensic science knowledge about decomposition timelines, insect activity, chemical changes, and environmental impacts of human decomposition. It is the primary institutional context for the research that prompted the meme.

**The Brabazon et al. 2020 paper**: On September 3, 2020, researchers at the University of Tennessee, Knoxville published a formal paper in the scientific journal *Trends in Plant Science* proposing a hypothesis: that the spectral responses of tree and shrub canopies near decomposing human bodies could serve as aerial guides for locating cadavers using remote sensing technologies (Brabazon, H. et al., 2020, *Trends in Plant Science*, vol. 25, no. 10, pp. 947–949, doi:10.1016/j.tplants.2020.07.013). The paper's lead author, Neal Stewart, a plant biologist at UT Knoxville, proposed using drones equipped with spectral imaging sensors to scan forested areas and detect vegetation changes correlated with nearby human decomposition.

**The nitrogen mechanism**: The proposed biological mechanism is chemically plausible. Decomposing human bodies release large quantities of nitrogen-rich compounds into the surrounding soil as tissues break down. These compounds include ammonia, nitrates, and various amino nitrogen compounds released from protein degradation. Plants in the immediate vicinity of a decomposing body would be exposed to this nitrogen flux and might absorb elevated nitrogen through their root systems. Elevated foliar nitrogen content is known to affect leaf color and light reflectance — in many plant species, higher nitrogen correlates with deeper green chlorophyll-rich leaves, because nitrogen is a critical component of chlorophyll (Evans, J.R., 1989, *Oecologia*, vol. 78, pp. 9–19, on the relationship between leaf nitrogen content and photosynthetic capacity). This is the basic science underlying the hypothesis: body → elevated soil nitrogen → plant uptake → elevated leaf chlorophyll → spectrally detectable color change.

**Why human bodies specifically**: Stewart's rationale for focusing on human bodies rather than animal carcasses is a matter of scale. A decomposing human adult produces a significantly larger nitrogen input per unit area into the surrounding soil than a decomposing rodent, bird, or even most large wild mammals. The hypothesis is that the magnitude of nitrogen release from a human cadaver may cross a threshold that produces a detectable vegetation response, while smaller animal decomposition would not produce a clearly detectable change. This size-based reasoning is ecologically sensible and consistent with broader knowledge of how nutrient pulses affect local vegetation.

**What the Body Farm's research actually found**: As of the time of the Snopes investigation, Stewart communicated by email that his team's field research at the Body Farm had produced results that were suggestive but not conclusive. He wrote: "Some species of plants did have spectral changes in their leaves when human bodies decomposed, but the effect was subtle and not a clear cut color change that could be observed by eye." Critically, the observed changes required spectral sensing instruments — technologies that measure light wavelengths beyond what the human eye can detect — rather than being visually apparent as "darker leaves" visible to naked observation. Furthermore, some leaf browning and leaf dropping were observed, but this was characterized as anecdotal since it could not be reliably reproduced in controlled greenhouse settings using human decomposition fluids.

**The gap between spectral sensing research and naked-eye claims**: The difference between what the research investigates and what the meme claims is substantial. The research is about drone-based remote sensing with spectral cameras that detect changes in near-infrared or other non-visible wavelength reflectance. The meme's claim is about naked-eye observation of leaf color ("darker leaves"). These are fundamentally different sensory modalities. A spectral change detectable by a drone sensor may be entirely invisible to human observers. The jump from "spectral sensors might detect changes" to "you can look at a dark-leafed tree and infer a dead body" is an enormous evidentiary leap that the research, in its current preliminary state, does not support.

**The confounders are numerous**: Even if the research eventually proves that decomposing human bodies do produce detectable spectral changes in nearby vegetation, the practical utility of leaf-color observation for identifying graves would be severely limited by confounding factors. Trees may have darker leaves than their neighbors for many unrelated reasons: differential soil nitrogen from any organic source (not just human bodies), differential moisture, different microclimatic light exposure, different root access to subsurface water, age differences, fungal or insect damage, varietal differences within species, and dozens of other variables. Without controlled conditions that rule out these alternatives, darker leaves cannot rationally be interpreted as evidence of human burial in any specific instance.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty — the meme as distorted scientific echo**:

The tree-darker-leaves claim offers an unusual epistemic case because the question it raises is not entirely fictional. Unlike many viral memes that originate purely in invention — misattributed quotes, fabricated statistics, invented facts — this claim exists as a warped reflection of legitimate ongoing science. The research that inspired it was genuinely published, featured in major science journalism, and pursued by credentialed scientists using real data. Understanding why the meme departs from the real science and why that departure is invisible to most audiences requires examining how scientific claims transform as they travel from research to public discourse.

The transformation involves several stages of compression and loss. The original *Trends in Plant Science* paper by Brabazon et al. presented a formal hypothesis for empirical testing, with careful hedging about what is known, what is proposed for investigation, and what would constitute confirmation. The *Wired* article by Matt Simon accurately captured the hypothetical framing: the paper's authors were "formally floating the idea," and the research was "still theoretical." The piece in The Conversation by Stewart himself maintained the conditional framing: plants "might" signal the location of dead bodies, research is needed to find out.

The iFunny meme, which likely originated as a distillation of this reporting, stripped all conditional language. "Might indicate" became "can indicate." The specific sensory modality (drone spectral sensing) was dropped. The confirmation that the research was preliminary and still ongoing was dropped. The caveats about confounding factors were dropped. What remained was a clean, confident, visually vivid claim that any educated layperson could repeat, share, and feel intelligent for knowing. The claim gained memetic fitness precisely by becoming simpler, less qualified, and more immediately applicable than the actual science warranted.

This is a general mechanism of scientific claim degradation: accuracy and nuance have negative memetic fitness in viral content ecosystems. A claim like "under specific laboratory conditions and through spectral analysis rather than naked-eye observation, some plant species near human decomposition may show subtle changes in light reflectance that are still being investigated" cannot compete for shares with "darker leaves = buried body." The information ecosystem systematically selects against epistemic precision and in favor of confident simplicity. The residual is not the science itself but a confident assertion that sounds scientific.

The macabre element of the claim amplifies this effect. Claims involving death, crime, and hidden bodies activate a particular kind of narrative curiosity — the feeling of having insight into dark hidden things that ordinary people don't know. This is the same emotional register as crime fiction, true crime podcasts, and forensic television. The claim taps into the established cultural genre of forensic detection, where specialized knowledge reveals what naked eyes miss. The framing transforms botanical research into a detective skill — something you can use, something that gives you power to see what others cannot. This framing is compelling and memorable in ways that purely descriptive science claims are not.

There is also an implicit flattery in the meme's structure: it addresses the reader as someone sufficiently sophisticated to receive and deploy this knowledge. The register is "this is something you probably didn't know, but should" — implying that knowing this marks you as a certain kind of informed, perceptive person. This flattery activates motivated sharing: people share the meme partly to signal their own sophistication, not merely because they are certain of its truth.


## 4. The Broader Pattern

The tree-darker-leaves claim represents a specific genus of scientific misinformation: the **premature fact**. Unlike outright fabrications with no scientific basis, premature facts are real hypotheses or preliminary findings that have been promoted from uncertain possibility to confident factual claim before adequate evidence has accumulated. They are particularly resistant to correction because some element of the underlying science is genuinely interesting and some version of the research is genuinely real.

Premature facts have precedents in forensic science specifically. The field has a documented history of forensic techniques being introduced into court and professional practice before adequate validation — bite mark analysis, hair microscopy, blood spatter pattern interpretation, and others. The common pattern is that practitioners develop confidence in their techniques based on experience rather than rigorous controlled testing, and this confidence then propagates through the field and into public perception before systematic testing is conducted. The darker-leaves claim follows a similar logic, though it has not yet entered forensic practice: the hypothesis sounds plausible and evocative, and it jumps into circulation as confident fact before methodological validation is complete.

The ongoing research at the Body Farm is real and scientifically worthwhile regardless of whether it ultimately confirms or disconfirms the hypothesis in its strong form. Finding that certain species show subtle spectral changes near human decomposition — even if those changes require instruments rather than naked eyes — would be a meaningful contribution to aerial forensic search methodology. Stewart's team's research continues, and updated findings may eventually shift the evidentiary picture. If confirmed at a statistically robust level with adequate species characterization, the research could have genuine practical applications for search-and-rescue and missing persons investigation — just not in the naked-eye, any-dark-tree-is-a-grave form the meme suggests.


## 5. Evidentiary Assessment

**EARC assignment: C (Contested)**

The claim is appropriately rated **Unproven** by Snopes. This rating reflects a genuine epistemic situation rather than a hedged verdict: the underlying science is real and ongoing, observed effects are preliminary and subtle, and the specific claim as stated in the meme — visually detectable darker leaves indicating burial — is not supported by available evidence but has not been formally refuted.

**Key evidence**:
- Brabazon et al. (2020) *Trends in Plant Science* formally published the hypothesis that cadaver decomposition may produce detectable spectral changes in nearby vegetation; this is a peer-reviewed paper proposing a hypothesis for empirical investigation, not a confirmation of the hypothesis
- Stewart (2020) communicated to Snopes that preliminary results showed "subtle" spectral changes in some species that were "not a clear cut color change that could be observed by eye"
- No published peer-reviewed study has confirmed the hypothesis at a level that would support the practical claim made in the meme
- The nitrogen enrichment mechanism is biologically plausible and consistent with established plant physiology (Evans, 1989, *Oecologia* 78:9–19)
- The confounding variables (multiple causes of leaf color variation) make naked-eye observation unreliable even if the underlying effect is real

**Gap category: forensic-botany**

This entry addresses a gap in public understanding of forensic botany and the distinction between preliminary scientific hypotheses and established empirical facts, particularly as applied to forensic search methodology.


## 6. Verdict and Implications

**Verdict**: The claim is **unproven**. It is not simply false — there is a legitimate scientific research program investigating a related but more specific and technically demanding question. But as stated in the meme ("darker leaves indicate a buried body"), it goes well beyond what available evidence supports, conflates naked-eye observation with specialized spectral sensing, and ignores numerous confounding causes of leaf color variation. Until the Body Farm research is completed and peer-reviewed in its conclusions, the naked-eye version of the claim lacks empirical support.

**What is genuinely interesting here**: The science underlying the hypothesis is remarkable and underreported. The idea that plants might serve as biomonitors for subsurface forensic evidence — essentially acting as living instruments detecting the chemical signatures of hidden remains — is a genuinely novel and potentially important development in forensic methodology. If drone-based spectral sensing of vegetation can be validated as a reliable indicator of cadaver location in forested environments, it could meaningfully aid missing persons searches and crime scene investigations in ways that current ground-search techniques cannot. This is a case where the real science, properly reported, is at least as interesting as the meme's oversimplified version.

**For investigators and curious readers**: The honest answer to the question is: we don't yet know whether this works. Some version of the effect may be real; the research apparatus to confirm it is being built. What we can say with confidence is that if you see a darker-leaved tree in a forest, you cannot and should not interpret it as evidence of a buried body — there are dozens of more plausible explanations for leaf color variation, and the specific effect proposed by the research (if it exists) requires specialized instrumentation to detect, not naked-eye observation.
