# Trees Communicate and Share Nutrients Through Underground Fungal Networks (The "Wood Wide Web")


## 1. The Claim

The "Wood Wide Web" has become one of the most culturally potent scientific images of the early twenty-first century. In the version that has propagated through popular science books, documentary films, newspaper features, TED talks, and school curricula, forests are cooperative communities of communicating trees — networked not by telephone lines but by underground mycorrhizal fungi that connect root systems across whole forest stands, enabling trees to send chemical signals to neighbours, warn each other about pest attacks, and share sugars and nutrients with seedlings, injured companions, and their own offspring. Mother trees, identified specifically by forest ecologist Suzanne Simard, are portrayed as anchors of these networks, selectively directing carbon to their seedlings and sustaining the community through altruistic resource sharing.

This image is compelling, emotionally resonant, and — in its popular form — substantially embellished beyond what the underlying science documents. The claim exists in a spectrum from minimally claimed to maximally claimed. At the minimal end: mycorrhizal fungi form physical connections between plant root systems, and molecules including sugars and nutrients move through these networks in measurable quantities. These statements are well-supported and essentially uncontested in the peer-reviewed literature. At the maximal end: individual trees communicate intentionally, recognise their kin, maintain cooperative social relationships, and preferentially support biological relatives through shared network infrastructure. These stronger statements were the currency of the popular Wood Wide Web narrative and are the ones that subsequent scientific review has most extensively challenged.

The trajectory of this claim through popular culture is also a case study in how scientific findings travel from peer-reviewed journals to bestselling books, how popularisation introduces systematic distortions, and how the social and commercial structures of science communication can amplify a compelling narrative faster than the specialist literature can evaluate it. The Wood Wide Web has generated enormous public enthusiasm for ecology, forest conservation, and plant biology — a genuine good — while simultaneously spreading a version of those disciplines' findings that specialist ecologists have increasingly identified as misleading.

Understanding the claim requires holding two truths simultaneously: mycorrhizal networks are real, the physical connections and molecular transfers through them are real, and the underlying science is genuinely fascinating. And: the popular narrative built on this science contains multiple interpretivelayers that go significantly beyond what the experiments demonstrate, flattening the distinction between physical connection and intentional communication, and between statistical transfer patterns and deliberate cooperative behaviour.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Mycorrhizal symbiosis — the validated baseline:**

Mycorrhizal fungi form mutualistic associations with the roots of approximately 85–90% of terrestrial plant species (Smith and Read, 2008, *Mycorrhizal Symbiosis*, 3rd ed., Academic Press). The two major types relevant to forest ecology are ectomycorrhizal fungi (ECM), which colonise roots of many Northern Hemisphere trees including pines, spruces, oaks, and birches without penetrating root cells, and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AM), which penetrate root cells and are the dominant type in tropical forests and most agricultural crops. In both systems, fine fungal hyphae dramatically increase the effective soil exploration volume of plant roots, facilitating uptake of phosphorus, nitrogen, water, and micronutrients in exchange for photosynthetically fixed carbon supplied by the host plant.

This is foundational, well-established plant biology with an extensive experimental literature spanning over a century. The mutualism operates at the individual plant-fungus level and is not in question.

**Common mycorrhizal networks — also validated:**

What is genuinely novel is the demonstration that mycorrhizal fungi can simultaneously colonise multiple plant hosts, creating physical hyphal connections between different individuals — Common Mycorrhizal Networks (CMNs). These connections were demonstrated in controlled laboratory and greenhouse experiments beginning in the 1980s and confirmed in field settings through the 1990s. Using isotopically labelled carbon molecules (¹³C or ¹⁴C), researchers showed that labelled carbon assimilated by one plant could subsequently be detected in a connected plant — the physical movement of carbon through hyphal connections is empirically established (Simard et al., 1997, *Nature*).

Simard et al.'s 1997 paper in *Nature* — the single most influential paper in the Wood Wide Web literature — used isotopic labelling to demonstrate bidirectional carbon transfer between Douglas fir and paper birch via ectomycorrhizal networks in field plots in British Columbia. This genuine finding provided the empirical seed from which the popular narrative grew. What made the paper significant was the bidirectionality and the field context; previous demonstrations had been laboratory-based or unidirectional.

**What the research actually shows about "sharing" and "communication":**

Here the epistemological complexity begins. The movement of carbon through mycorrhizal networks is measured as net flux — the balance of transfer in and out over a given period — rather than as directional gifting from a donating tree to a receiving one. Bidirectional transfer (Simard et al., 1997) means carbon moves in both directions, with the net flux depending on the photosynthetic vigour of each connected plant, atmospheric CO₂ concentrations, season, light availability, and many other factors. A plant in shade receives more carbon from the network than it sends; a plant in sun sends more than it receives. This is an emergent property of the diffusion physiology of a shared fungal network — not necessarily evidence of directed gifting.

Hoeksema (2010, *New Phytologist*, review paper) noted that much of the transfer detected in carbon labelling studies may reflect fungal carbon cycling rather than plant-to-plant transfer sensu stricto: the fungus acquires carbon from one host, metabolises it for its own growth, and some of that carbon later reaches another host plant as a byproduct of normal fungal physiology. Distinguishing "tree-to-tree transfer via fungal pipe" from "fungi acquiring carbon from multiple hosts and redistributing it as part of their own metabolism" is experimentally challenging and has not been definitively resolved.

Beiler et al. (2010, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*) used DNA barcoding to map the network structure of mycorrhizal connections among Douglas fir trees in old-growth British Columbia forest, confirming that large, old trees ("hub" trees or "mother trees") connected to more individuals than young or small trees. This network topology finding is genuine and interesting. It does not, by itself, demonstrate that hub trees direct carbon preferentially toward connected seedlings; it establishes connection pattern, not transfer directionality or intentionality.

**The kin recognition and preferential support claims:**

Among the most widely repeated popular claims is that mother trees recognise and preferentially support their own seedling offspring underground. This claim tracing to later research by Simard and colleagues (Pickles et al., 2017, *Ecology Letters*; Simard's popular book *Finding the Mother Tree*, 2021) has been the focus of specific critical appraisal.

Karst et al. (2023, *Nature Ecology & Evolution*) published a systematic review specifically examining the evidence base for mycorrhizal network effects on plant performance and kin discrimination. The authors reviewed the experimental literature and found that effect sizes for mycorrhizal network benefits on seedling performance were highly variable, many studies showed null effects or negative effects, replication was often limited, and publication bias toward positive results created difficulty in assessing the true size of any benefit. On kin discrimination specifically, they concluded the evidence was weak and inconsistent, with many studies confounded by spatial proximity (relatives are often close together, and proximity itself affects network access) and methodological limitations.

Beiler et al.'s network topology work was explicitly addressed: connection to a hub tree does not demonstrate carbon outflow from hub to seedling; the directionality and selectivity of transfer remain empirically unresolved. The "mother tree" framing — trees recognising offspring and directing resources to them — is an interpretive narrative that the network topology and transfer data do not directly support.

**Defence signalling claims:**

The popular narrative also includes claims that trees warn each other via mycorrhizal networks when attacked by pests or pathogens, transmitting chemical defence signals underground that prime neighbouring trees to mount immune responses. This claim has some experimental basis — laboratory studies using Arabidopsis (not a tree) and some mycorrhizal plant species have shown that fungal networks can transmit defence-priming signals (Song et al., 2010, *PLOS ONE*) — but the translation of laboratory results using small herbaceous plants to multi-decade old-growth forest dynamics involves large inferential steps that the field evidence does not yet bridge.

The most methodologically rigorous field experiments on forest mycorrhizal networks have generally found more modest, context-dependent, and variable effects than the popular Wood Wide Web narrative suggests. The biology is genuinely interesting and the networks are real; the strong cooperative community claims are extrapolations from limited and sometimes contested experimental evidence.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The Wood Wide Web illustrates a specific mechanism of scientific oversimplification that operates differently from most myth-generation processes documented in this series. In most Truth Vault entries, a claim either originates in folklore and later receives or fails to receive scientific support, or originates in science and is distorted by popular transmission. The Wood Wide Web narrative is distinctive because the distortion was introduced not by popular misreading but by a specific popularisation pathway: the scientific populariser (Simard's bestselling memoir and related talks), the popular press, and documentary filmmaking all operated on a compressed timescale that precluded the specialist literature's ability to provide calibration.

The mechanism is worth describing in some detail because it recurs in contemporary science communication. Simard's 1997 *Nature* paper demonstrated carbon transfer through mycorrhizal networks — a genuine finding of significant interest. Over the following decade and a half, the finding attracted popular science interest. By the time Simard published *Finding the Mother Tree* (2021) and appeared on popular podcasts, in film documentaries, and in school curricula, the popular account had accumulated interpretive layers — cooperative communities, mother tree altruism, kin recognition, deliberate communication — that the specialist literature had not validated and that critical ecologists were beginning to question.

The critical response — particularly Karst et al. (2023) in *Nature Ecology & Evolution* — arrived after the popular account was already deeply embedded in public understanding. The specialist critique cannot propagate through the same channels that the popular narrative used — bestselling books, TED talks, Netflix documentaries — because specialist critique is not inherently dramatic. The asymmetry of popularisation channels and criticism channels means that a compelling positive claim travels through the culture far faster than a methodological reassessment can follow.

Psychologically, the Wood Wide Web narrative benefits from activating several strong human cognitive biases simultaneously. The figure of cooperative, communicating trees resonates with deep intuitions about nature as purposeful and interconnected — what biologists sometimes call "teleological bias," the tendency to attribute purpose and intentionality to biological systems. Trees "wanting" to help their offspring maps onto human parent-child relationships in a way that makes the claim feel intuitively right. Popular ecology narratives have historically been most successful when they frame natural phenomena in terms of cooperation, community, and care rather than competition and resource depletion — creating a systematic favouritism for cooperative interpretations in science popularisation.

The genuinely impressive underlying science also creates a credibility halo that extends to the more speculative claims. If mycorrhizal networks are real and transfer does occur (both true), then it is easy to accept that the full cooperative narrative is also true. Readers who lack the specialist knowledge to distinguish "carbon transfer has been measured" from "trees deliberately direct care to offspring" have no mechanism to challenge the additional interpretive layer.

The epistemic novelty here is a warning about popularisation asymmetry: the social and commercial infrastructure of popular science communication — publishers, speaker agencies, documentary filmmakers — systematically favours compelling positive narratives over careful accounts of uncertainty, effect size heterogeneity, and methodological qualification. This structural bias will recur in any field where discoveries are genuinely interesting and where public enthusiasm creates market demand for a popular account of those discoveries. The Wood Wide Web is a case study to return to when thinking about how genuinely good science can generate genuinely misleading popular beliefs.


## 4. Verdict

**TRUE at the documented baseline — AMPLIFIED BEYOND EVIDENCE at the level of popular interpretation**

The factual core of the Wood Wide Web claim is well-established: ectomycorrhizal and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi form real physical connections between plant root systems; molecules including carbon compounds, nitrogen, phosphorus, and water move through these connections in measurable quantities; large old trees often form more connections than smaller younger trees; and the networks can, under some conditions, facilitate the establishment of seedlings in forest understories by providing access to fixed carbon.

The amplified popular version — cooperative communication, deliberate resource sharing, maternal kin recognition, active forest community governance — substantially exceeds what the experimental literature validates. Karst et al.'s 2023 systematic review in *Nature Ecology & Evolution* provided the most comprehensive evidential assessment to date, finding variable and often small effect sizes, evidence of publication bias, methodological limitations in kin discrimination studies, and absence of direct evidence for directed, intentional carbon transfer from "mother" trees to "offspring" seedlings.

The honest verdict is that mycorrhizal networks are a genuine and fascinating ecological phenomenon that almost certainly play some role in forest dynamics — but that the specific mechanisms, the magnitude of effects under field conditions, the degree to which transfer is directional versus diffusive, and the plausibility of "communication" and "intention" language applied to fungal-mediated molecular transfer are all questions more open than the popular account suggests.

The EARC classification "amplified beyond evidence" is appropriate: the underlying phenomenon exists, is documented, and is important. The cultural frame applied to it — the "Wood Wide Web," mother trees, cooperative forest societies — has been built up through successive popularisation steps that each added interpretive claims unsupported by the original experiments. This kind of layered amplification is particularly difficult to correct because each layer seems to follow naturally from the one below, and the original genuine finding provides insulation against challenges at any level.

For practical science communication, the honest account emphasises what is known — mycorrhizal networks exist, molecular transfer occurs, network topology matters — while declining to claim intentionality, deliberate communication, or cooperative social structure for which the mechanistic evidence is absent. This deflated account is still genuinely interesting; the mistake is to have concluded that "ordinary" fascinating ecological connectivity was insufficient and required inflation into the language of social community.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The science underlying mycorrhizal networks belongs to a vigorous and expanding research area that will continue to produce important findings over the coming decades. The actual biology of mycorrhizal symbiosis has implications extending far beyond forest ecology: mycorrhizal fungi have potential roles in agricultural productivity, carbon sequestration, and ecosystem resilience under climate change that are of direct practical relevance to land management and food security.

The forest ecology context connects to some of the most consequential conservation debates of the current era. Claims that old-growth forests possess network structures that cannot be reconstituted on decadal timescales — that the mycorrhizal infrastructure of ancient forests represents irreplaceable ecological capital — have political and policy implications for timber industry regulation, forest preservation law, and carbon accounting frameworks. These arguments do not depend on the amplified popular narrative being correct in its strongest claims; the existence of complex, species-rich mycorrhizal communities in old-growth that differ structurally from plantation forests is documented (Simard et al., 1997; Beiler et al., 2010; Twieg et al., 2007, *Forest Ecology and Management*). The conservation case stands even if the cooperative communication narrative is deflated.

The history of plant biology's engagement with communication and sociality has other chapters that illuminate the Wood Wide Web case. Volatile signalling among plants — the release of airborne chemical compounds that prime defence responses in neighbouring plants — has been documented since the 1980s (Karban and Baldwin, 1997, *Induced Responses to Herbivory*, University of Chicago Press). These volatile-mediated interactions are mechanistically well-characterised and provide well-established evidence for plant-to-plant signalling through the air that does not require mycorrhizal network framing. Their relative obscurity in popular science discourse, compared with the Wood Wide Web narrative, illustrates how network imagery — the underground internet metaphor — adds a cultural resonance that the equally valid volatile signalling research lacks.

The mycorrhizal network literature also exists within the broader context of the microbiome revolution in biology, which has reshaped understanding of multicellular organisms as ecological communities rather than discrete individuals. Trees, from this perspective, are more accurately understood as holobionts — composite organisms whose functioning depends on associated microbial communities including mycorrhizal fungi, bacteria, endophytes, and dozens of other interacting organisms. The Wood Wide Web narrative captured part of this genuine conceptual shift — the dissolution of sharp boundaries between individual organisms — while expressing it in an anthropomorphic vocabulary that the underlying biology does not require.

The Karst et al. (2023) review prompted significant discussion in the ecology community. Simard and colleagues published responses contesting aspects of the review's methodology and scope. This ongoing specialist debate — which involves genuine disagreements about experimental design, effect size interpretation, and how to weigh publication bias — is precisely the kind of productive scientific controversy that popular accounts routinely elide. The Wood Wide Web is a living scientific controversy as well as an established popular narrative, and the popular account has frozen a contested and evolving picture into something that looks more resolved than it is.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not published a direct examination of the Wood Wide Web claim. The platform's coverage of plant and forest biology is limited compared to its coverage of health, politics, and consumer topics, and the mycorrhizal network story has circulated primarily through science media, documentary, and popular book channels rather than the social media misinformation pathways that Snopes is best positioned to address. The absence of Snopes coverage does not reflect a verdict on the claim; it reflects the claim's trajectory through elevated rather than tabloid media channels.

Science journalism has given the Wood Wide Web narrative extensive and largely credulous coverage since approximately 2015. The BBC, National Geographic, *The Guardian*, and *The New York Times* have all published sympathetic features emphasising the cooperative communication framing without systematically engaging with the specialist literature's reservations. The documentary *Fantastic Fungi* (2019) and multiple nature series segments adopted the popular narrative as given, reaching large audiences with the amplified version of the claims.

The specialist critical response — primarily published in ecology journals including *New Phytologist*, *Ecology Letters*, and *Nature Ecology & Evolution* — has not migrated effectively to popular channels. Karst et al. (2023) received some popular science coverage, primarily in specialist-adjacent outlets like *Quanta Magazine* and *Science News*, but this coverage was brief and reached a small fraction of the audience that had received the positive narrative through documentaries and bestselling books.

The Wood Wide Web case suggests a concrete challenge for fact-checking organisations: claims that propagate through high-prestige science communication channels (bestselling books, flagship documentary films, prominent science journalists) are structurally less likely to attract fact-checking scrutiny than claims from tabloid or social media sources, even when the substantive evidential questions are equally significant. Prestige source heuristics — the tendency to trust information from culturally elevated sources — protect the Wood Wide Web narrative from the same scrutiny applied to lower-prestige claims. A systematic expansion of fact-checking scope to include popular science communication, particularly in claims with direct policy implications for conservation and land management, would be valuable and is not currently consistently practised.

The most defensible summary verdict for informed communication: mycorrhizal networks exist, connect trees, and facilitate measurable molecular transfer — say this confidently. Trees "communicate," "cooperate," and "recognise kin" via underground networks — say this only with heavy qualification and acknowledgment that these are contested interpretations built on quite limited direct evidence. The biology is fascinating enough without the anthropomorphic frame, and the anthropomorphic frame does real epistemic damage to public understanding of what plant biology actually knows and does not yet know.
