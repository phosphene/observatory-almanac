## 1. The Claim

In the spring of 2024, a black-and-white image began spreading rapidly across social media platforms, captivating users with what appeared to be an extraordinary historical document. The photograph showed a man — identified in captions variously as Theodore Roosevelt, the 26th President of the United States, and as an individual named "Earl David Cook" — standing over the lifeless body of a large, multi-horned prehistoric-looking animal. The image had the texture and tonal qualities associated with late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century photography: grainy, slightly blurred, with the period quality that lends old photographs their air of documentary truth.

The claims attached to the image varied in detail across different posts and platforms, but several consistent narrative threads emerged. One widely shared version, originally posted in German on X (formerly Twitter), claimed that the last triceratops was killed in 1906 by "Earl David Cook" in Texas, that triceratops had been considered agricultural pests throughout the 1800s, and that what had once been vast herds of thousands of animals had been hunted to extinction within a few decades by the early twentieth century. A different caption, circulating simultaneously on Facebook, identified the man in the photograph as Theodore Roosevelt and dated the image to 1909, naming it a photograph of Roosevelt with "the last known Triceratops."

By the time fact-checkers examined the posts in May 2024, one version of the image on X had accumulated more than 410,000 views, and a Facebook post carrying the same image had received over 93,000 interactions. The claim spread in multiple languages and was shared in communities spanning natural history enthusiasts, conspiracy theory circles, alternative history forums, and general popular culture audiences who were drawn to the compelling image of a famous historical president posing triumphantly over a creature that, as far as established science is concerned, should not have existed within millions of years of Roosevelt's lifetime.

The claim, in every variant, invited the same fundamental assertion: that triceratops survived, unknown to mainstream science, into recorded human history, and that this photograph was the suppressed evidence.

## 2. What's Actually True

The image is a deliberate digital composite — a Photoshop creation — made not in 1906 or 1909 but in February 2015 by an Instagram artist operating under the username @dr_deviny, whose real name is Jon Deviny.

Deviny posted the image on his Instagram account on 23 February 2015, with an accompanying caption that was entirely transparent about its nature from the first day of its existence: "Fun fact: Theodore Roosevelt (the 26th US president) was an avid big game hunter. #nofilter #unedited #biggame #hunting #teddy #Roosevelt #president #theodoreroosevelt #dinosaur #triceratops #photoshop #funny #jurassicpark #jurassicworld #dino." The hashtags "#photoshop" and "#funny" were included from the outset. The "#nofilter #unedited" tags appear to be ironic, playing on the Instagram conventions of the period.

When a user on Instagram asked Deviny about the rights to the image in the original post's comments, he explained the construction clearly: "I did the Photoshop magic but I don't own the rights to the images I used. The one of Roosevelt is public domain but the triceratops is a shot from the set of Jurassic Park." When Snopes journalist Nick Hardinges contacted Deviny directly in May 2024, Deviny confirmed again that the image was his own Photoshop creation, assembled from a public-domain hunting photograph of Theodore Roosevelt combined with an image of a prop triceratops from the set of the Jurassic Park film franchise.

The image had a long afterlife between its 2015 creation and its 2024 viral moment. It appeared on Imgur shortly after being posted in February 2015; in January 2017, Reddit circulated it with a completely different caption claiming it showed Roosevelt with a "now-extinct Eurasian giant rhino"; in November 2023, an Instagram account dedicated to dinosaur content posted it claiming it showed footage from 1904. By 2024, when it went maximally viral, the image had shed all traces of its creative origins and was being presented with confident false authority as genuine historical documentation.

The palaeontological baseline is unambiguous: Triceratops horridus, along with the entire non-avian dinosaur lineage, went extinct approximately 65 to 66 million years ago at the end of the Cretaceous Period, in the mass extinction event caused primarily by the Chicxulub asteroid impact. This timeline is not a rough estimate or a contested scientific approximation; it is one of the most thoroughly dated events in geological history, confirmed through multiple independent lines of evidence including rock stratigraphy, radiometric dating of geological layers, fossil distribution, and geochemical signatures of the K-Pg boundary. The gap between 66 million years ago and Theodore Roosevelt's 1906 hunting expeditions is approximately 65.9 million years. There are no credible peer-reviewed findings, no fossil records, no credible historical accounts, and no physical evidence of any kind suggesting that any non-avian dinosaur survived into recorded human history.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The triceratops Roosevelt photograph spread with extraordinary velocity across multiple language communities and platform types despite — or perhaps partly because of — its obvious implausibility. Understanding why requires examining several intersecting factors.

**Visual authority of period-style photography.** The human brain is not equipped by evolution or education to critically evaluate manipulated photographs. When an image possesses the visual characteristics associated with authentic historical photography — the grain, the tonal range, the slightly imperfect focus, the monochrome palette — we extend it a degree of automatic trust that colour digital images do not receive. The "old photo" aesthetic carries an implicit credibility signal that has been the foundation of photographic disinformation for as long as image manipulation has been technically feasible. Deviny's composite is skilled work that successfully mimics the period aesthetic.

**The appeal of alternative prehistory.** A persistent and extremely popular subculture of "cryptozoology," "alternative history," and "forbidden archaeology" posits that mainstream science has systematically suppressed evidence of creatures and civilisations that don't fit the accepted narrative. Within these communities, an image like this is not merely entertaining; it is confirming evidence of a worldview they have already adopted. The framing "they want you to think dinosaurs went extinct 66 million years ago" requires only a photograph that looks plausible to activate complete belief in audiences primed to receive it.

**The Theodore Roosevelt anchor.** Roosevelt is an exceptionally well-chosen historical figure for this kind of composite. He was, factually, a passionate and prolific big-game hunter who participated in large-scale African safaris and documented his expeditions extensively with photography. The idea of Roosevelt standing over an unusual large animal in the early 1900s is fully consistent with what he actually did throughout his career. His hunting photography is genuinely part of the historical record, and incorporating him into a photograph with a prehistoric beast borrows credibility from the authentic documentary tradition of his actual expeditions. The Roosevelt "anchor" makes the claimed scenario feel vaguely plausible to people who know something about him.

**Meme and irony drift.** Deviny created the image as obvious humorous Photoshop content — the "#photoshop #funny" hashtags were there from the beginning — and the initial audience understood and appreciated it in that spirit. But internet content erodes its context as it travels. Each reshare strips another layer of framing. Within months, the image could be exported from a joke context into a sincere claim with the simple deletion of the hashtags and the addition of confident assertive captions. This is not unique to this photograph; it is a general feature of how internet memes mutate and how satire gets laundered into sincerely held false belief.

**The "suppressed by scientists" frame.** The viral versions of the claim often implied or stated that mainstream paleontology and history had suppressed awareness of triceratops' survival into the twentieth century. This framing activates a familiar epistemic immune response in audiences who distrust institutional science: the absence of official acknowledgement becomes evidence of cover-up rather than evidence of nonexistence. For audiences in that framing, the fact that mainstream science says triceratops went extinct 66 million years ago is the expected response of a system invested in its own narrative, not a reason to dismiss the claim.

**Multi-platform proliferation with mutating captions.** The image's circulation across platforms over nine years with continuously changing captions — sometimes calling the animal a triceratops, sometimes a Eurasian giant rhino, sometimes identifying the man as Roosevelt, sometimes as a fictional "Earl David Cook" — demonstrates how digital images become detached from their origins and reattached to whatever narrative serves the moment. Each new caption configuration reaches a new audience subset. By 2024, many of the people sharing it had never encountered any previous iteration and had no reason to suspect they were looking at a nine-year-old piece of comedic Photoshop.

## 4. Verdict

The photograph is **fake**. It is a Photoshop composite created in February 2015 by digital artist Jon Deviny, assembled from a public-domain hunting photograph of Theodore Roosevelt and a behind-the-scenes prop image from the Jurassic Park film franchise. Deviny has confirmed this publicly on multiple occasions, including directly to Snopes.

The claims attached to the image — whether that the man is Roosevelt, that the man is "Earl David Cook," that the year is 1906, 1909, or any other — are all fabricated. More fundamentally, the ecological premise underlying all versions of the claim is false: Triceratops went extinct approximately 66 million years ago, along with all other non-avian dinosaurs, and there is no credible scientific evidence of any kind suggesting they survived into the historical period in any population, region, or ecological niche.

The image is a skilled and charming piece of digital creative work that was clearly and honestly labelled as such by its creator from the moment of its posting. Its subsequent trajectory from labelled joke to sincere viral claim illustrates a well-documented pattern of context-stripping and narrative reattachment that characterises misinformation in digital information ecosystems.

**EARC classification: E (Empirical).** The claim is falsifiable through straightforward empirical investigation: the photograph is either authentic or composite, and triceratops either survived into the twentieth century or did not. Both questions admit definitive answers based on verifiable evidence.

**Gap category: distorted-but-grounded.** The image uses real historical components — a genuine photograph of Roosevelt, a real film production prop — combined through real digital technology into a fake whole. The distortion operates by layering plausible real elements onto an impossible claim.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The Roosevelt-triceratops photograph is a case study in what might be called the lifecycle of viral fabrication: a piece of content created with clear creative intent and full transparency about its artificial nature, which is then stripped of its framing and recycled through successive resharing with increasingly confident false claims attached to it. Understanding this lifecycle has significant implications for how we think about both media literacy and the ethics of digital image creation.

The phenomenon of authentic-seeming fake historical photographs is not new to the digital age. Nineteenth-century photographers staged and manipulated images, and the history of official photography includes cases of deliberate alteration — Stalin's systematic erasure of purged figures from official photographs being the most notorious example. What is new is the speed and ease with which digital manipulation can produce convincing period-authentic results, and the platform architecture through which a single compelling image can reach tens of millions of people across geographic, linguistic, and cultural contexts within a matter of days.

The "triceratops survived to the modern era" claim specifically belongs to a genre of content sometimes called "cryptopaleontology" or "alternative prehistory." This genre draws heavily on genuine mysteries and anomalies in the fossil record — the Coelacanth, a "living fossil" thought long extinct before its rediscovery in 1938, is cited constantly — to argue that scientific consensus on extinction timelines is fundamentally unreliable. The Coelacanth example is instructive both as a legitimate scientific surprise and as a case study in how exceptions are extrapolated far beyond what the evidence supports. A fish surviving in deep water for 65 million years is interesting; it tells us nothing about whether large terrestrial megafauna requiring enormous territories and food supplies survived undetected on the American Great Plains into the twentieth century.

There is also a broader phenomenon of what might be called "dead celebrity + impossible thing" memes, in which recognisable historical figures — Abraham Lincoln, Tesla, Einstein, Roosevelt — are attached to anachronistic or impossible imagery to generate engagement. These composites function partly as jokes and partly as a kind of informal cultural mythology-building. The problem is not with the jokes; it is with the conditions under which the jokes cease to be jokes and become taken as evidence by audiences who encounter them de-contextualised.

Jon Deviny's image was created without malicious intent and with full transparency about its nature. The viral spread of the image as a sincere historical claim is not his responsibility. But the case raises real questions about whether creators of convincing fake historical imagery have an ethical interest in watermarking or otherwise indelibly marking their work, given that the internet's contextual stripping is well-documented and foreseeable.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes investigation, published on 14 May 2024 by reporter Nick Hardinges, rated the claim as **Fake**, the platform's designation for content that is a deliberate fabrication rather than an honest error or an ambiguous claim.

Hardinges' methodology centred on reverse image search and source tracing. By identifying the original Instagram post from February 2015, the investigation was able to establish not only that the image had been created as a transparent Photoshop composite but that the creator was still identifiable and contactable. Hardinges confirmed the image's origins directly with Deviny, who corroborated his original public statements about the image's construction.

This approach — tracing an image to its chronological origin and contacting the apparent creator for confirmation — is the gold standard for evaluating digitally manipulated images. Reverse image search tools (Google Reverse Image Search, TinEye, and others) allow investigators to identify the earliest known online appearance of an image, which often exposes the gap between a claimed historical origin and the actual date of creation. An image claimed to have been taken in 1906 but first appearing online in 2015 is, at minimum, not the original photograph from 1906.

The palaeontological dimension of the claim required only the most basic reference to established science: Britannica, National Geographic, and the American Museum of Natural History all confirm the widely accepted 65-66 million year extinction timeline. No specialised expertise was required to dismiss the temporal claim; the scientific consensus is so well-established and so ubiquitously documented that citation of any credible natural history source suffices.

The Snopes investigation also catalogued the image's earlier iterations — on Imgur in 2015, Reddit in 2017, Instagram in 2023 — demonstrating the image's long travel across the internet with mutating claims. This historical cataloguing performs a useful function: it shows that the 2024 viral spread was not the "genuine discovery" of a previously obscure image but the latest iteration in a nine-year cycle of decontextualised reshares. For readers who encountered the image from a 2024 share, this history provides important context: the image is old, well-examined, and well-debunked; its 2024 spread is simply the latest wave.

Fact-checkers assessing similar "historical photograph" claims should attend to: (1) the earliest verifiable online appearance rather than the claimed date; (2) metadata where available; (3) the specific stylistic features of the image that might indicate digital manipulation; (4) the plausibility of the underlying claimed scenario against independently documented facts; and (5) the possibility of directly contacting the image's creator or tracing the source components. The Roosevelt-triceratops case is particularly useful as a teaching case because Deviny's transparency and continued availability for contact enabled unusually clean verification — a luxury not always available but worth pursuing.
