# Trigger warnings help people with trauma avoid harmful content


## 1. The Claim

The claim is pervasive across university campuses, publishing platforms, social media, streaming services, and contemporary journalism: trigger warnings — brief advisories placed before content that may be distressing, such as descriptions of sexual violence, abuse, war, or self-harm — help people who have experienced trauma to make informed decisions about whether and when to engage with that content. The assumed mechanism is intuitive and operates on two levels. First, the warning gives a person with trauma the option to avoid content that might provoke a traumatic stress response, thereby protecting them from unnecessary distress. Second, even for those who do not avoid the content, the warning is thought to function as a preparatory buffer — giving the reader or viewer time to steel themselves, activate coping resources, and approach the material from a position of psychological readiness rather than sudden shock.

This logic has been institutionalised rapidly across higher education. Dozens of universities in the United Kingdom, United States, Australia, and elsewhere introduced or recommended trigger warning policies in the 2010s. Publishers began prepending advisories to reprinted classic novels. Podcast platforms adopted content labelling systems citing mental health protection as justification. Streaming services developed elaborate content descriptor systems partly on the basis that forewarning vulnerable audiences is a proportionate protective measure.

The claim does not typically rest on cited evidence when it is made in popular discourse; it rests on its intuitiveness. Of course, the argument runs, knowing that difficult content is coming allows a sensitive person to prepare or step back. The alternative — removing warnings — sounds protective only of the content creator's right to surprise an audience, not of the audience's wellbeing. This framing has made trigger warnings politically and emotionally difficult to scrutinise: opposing them can be read as callous toward trauma survivors, which creates a chilling effect on critical evaluation precisely in the spaces — academic psychology, evidence-based journalism — where critical evaluation is most needed.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The research base: experimental and meta-analytic findings.** Since approximately 2018, a substantial number of preregistered controlled experiments and several meta-analyses have examined whether trigger warnings produce the benefits claimed for them. The convergent finding across this literature is that trigger warnings do not produce measurable emotional benefit for trauma survivors and may have small counterproductive effects.

Bellet, Jones, and McNally (2020, *Clinical Psychological Science*) conducted one of the earliest and most widely discussed preregistered experiments. They recruited participants online and measured responses to passages with and without trigger warnings, using validated anxiety measures. They found that trigger warnings had no significant effect on emotional reactions overall. Critically, among participants who held strong beliefs in the harmfulness of traumatic content (measured by a scale they developed), trigger warnings slightly increased anxiety rather than reducing it. The authors concluded that warnings may inadvertently reinforce a mental model in which traumatic content is acutely dangerous, an effect that directly contradicts therapeutic exposure-based approaches to trauma treatment.

Jones, Bellet, and McNally (2020, *Psychological Science*) extended this work in a larger preregistered study examining avoidance behaviour specifically. They found that trigger warnings did not increase rates of content avoidance among trauma survivors compared with control conditions — meaning warnings did not achieve their most concrete stated purpose of helping people with trauma avoid potentially harmful material. The effect size for any difference was negligible.

Sanson, Strange, and Garry (2019, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*) conducted a series of experiments examining whether trigger warnings alleviated emotional distress. They found no evidence of benefit across multiple outcome measures including anxiety, negative affect, and post-reading distress. They did find a small but consistent pattern in which trigger warnings heightened anticipatory anxiety before content exposure — the period during which warned participants were waiting to encounter the material they had been told was distressing.

In 2023, Bridgland and colleagues published a preregistered meta-analysis (*New Ideas in Psychology*) spanning 12 experimental studies examining the effects of trigger warnings on diverse outcome measures. The meta-analytic synthesis found no significant benefit of trigger warnings on distress, avoidance, or educational engagement outcomes. Effect sizes across studies were consistently near zero or small-negative. The authors concluded that the empirical basis for mandating trigger warnings is not established by the available experimental literature.

**What the research does not establish.** It is important to be precise about what this body of evidence does and does not show. It does not prove that trigger warnings are harmful in catastrophic or clinical terms — the observed negative effects where detected are small. It does not establish that no possible benefit could exist for any specific individual in any specific context. And several of these studies used online samples and self-report measures, which carry their own methodological limitations: they expose participants to relatively mild written content rather than immersive visual media; they measure short-term emotional states rather than long-term psychological wellbeing; and they may not adequately capture the experience of people with severe PTSD rather than subclinical trauma histories.

**Clinical perspective on avoidance.** An additional dimension emerges from the clinical psychology of trauma treatment. The dominant evidence-based treatments for post-traumatic stress disorder — prolonged exposure therapy (Foa et al., 2007, *Emotional Processing Theory*), cognitive processing therapy (Resick & Schnicke, 1992, *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*), and Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing (Shapiro, 1989, *Journal of Traumatic Stress*) — all rest on the theoretical foundation that therapeutic improvement requires graduated confrontation with trauma memories and related stimuli, not avoidance of them. Clinically, avoidance of trauma-related content is a symptom of PTSD, not a coping strategy. The aim of trauma treatment is to help patients develop the capacity to encounter reminders without overwhelming distress, not to permanently shield them from triggers.

This creates a clinical tension around trigger warnings that their proponents rarely engage: if the warnings function as intended and increase avoidance, they may, from a clinical standpoint, be reinforcing avoidance patterns that effective treatment aims to reduce. McNally (2016, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*) — a prominent clinical psychologist who has written extensively on the empirical basis of trigger warnings — made this argument explicitly, noting that preparation to avoid is clinically distinct from preparation to engage and that the latter is what exposure-based models predict to be beneficial.

**What the research cannot cleanly address.** The experimental literature focuses primarily on emotional outcomes over short time horizons. It does not comprehensively address autonomy arguments for trigger warnings — the position that people deserve content information to make informed choices, independent of whether warnings reduce acute distress. Nor does it address the distinct but related context of journalism about ongoing traumatic events (war, atrocity, natural disaster), where content advisories have a longer professional history and a different functional logic than the university syllabi and literary fiction contexts where most trigger warning research has been conducted.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The avoidance heuristic.** The most powerful driver of belief in trigger warnings is a nearly universal cognitive heuristic: that knowing something bad is coming allows you to prepare for or avoid it, which is better than encountering it unawares. This heuristic is generally accurate in ordinary life. If someone warns you that a film contains scenes of extended surgical detail before you sit down to watch it, you might choose to look away or choose a different film, and you are better off for knowing. The heuristic extrapolates naturally to trauma: a person with a rape trauma history knows, if warned, to decide consciously about whether to read a passage involving sexual violence.

The error is not in the heuristic itself but in the assumption that trauma works the same way ordinary aversive experience does, and specifically that the same person who benefits from warning about distasteful ordinary content will benefit equally when the content relates to their trauma history. Trauma processing involves a more complex psychological ecology in which avoided stimuli maintain their potency precisely because they are avoided — the clinical observation that underlies exposure therapy. The warning, in this model, changes the proximate choice without changing the underlying relationship between the person and their trauma material.

**Intuitions about care and protection.** Trigger warnings are also embedded in a broader cultural grammar of care. Recommending them is a way of signalling sensitivity to vulnerability, empathy with trauma survivors, and willingness to modify one's own practices to reduce the risk of harm. Opposing or questioning them can therefore function as a social signal in the opposite direction — indifference to trauma survivors — regardless of the actual empirical basis. This makes it socially costly in many communities to raise questions about their efficacy. The social function of endorsing trigger warnings has become partially independent of the empirical question of whether they work, which insulates the claim from normal evidence-based correction.

**The absence of a visible null effect.** When a policy produces no effect, the absence of visible benefit is not salient. When students or readers with trauma histories use trigger warnings to make choices about content, they experience whatever emotional outcomes follow, but they do not have access to the counterfactual — what would have happened without the warning. In the absence of that comparison, a person who receives a trigger warning, decides to engage with the content, experiences manageable distress, and concludes that the warning helped has interpreted their experience entirely plausibly but without controlled comparison. Individual subjective experience is not well-positioned to detect null effects.

**Epistemic novelty: the belief-reinforcement mechanism.** The most striking finding in the empirical literature — and the one that receives insufficient attention in popular discussions — is not simply that trigger warnings fail to help, but that they may selectively worsen outcomes for people who already hold strong beliefs in the harmfulness of traumatic content. This belief-reinforcement pathway suggests that trigger warnings could function as a kind of nocebo effect: by converting environmental cues into reminders of danger, they prime people who are already inclined toward harm-expectation to experience greater anticipatory distress. This is a substantively different concern than the simpler "they don't help" finding. It implies that in psychologically vulnerable populations — the very people trigger warnings are most intended to protect — exposure to the warnings may itself constitute the adverse event. A warning about potentially upsetting content upsets the person before the content arrives.

This pathway is not conclusively established, the effect sizes are small, and the findings need replication in more clinically severe samples. But it represents a genuine epistemic novelty: the possibility that a widely institutionalised protective measure achieves the opposite of its stated purpose through a mechanism involving belief reinforcement rather than direct emotional transmission.

**Media coverage asymmetry.** The cultural debates around trigger warnings — particularly in the context of university censorship, "cancel culture," and free speech — have generated substantial media attention. That attention has largely been structured around the political question (are trigger warnings an infringement on academic freedom?) rather than the empirical question (do they work?). Studies demonstrating null or negative effects have received coverage in the psychological literature and in science journalism, but they have not penetrated the popular and policy discourse with the same force as the intuitive claim, partly because the political framing crowds out the empirical one.


## 4. Verdict

**Intuitively plausible, empirically unsupported.** The claim that trigger warnings help people with trauma avoid harmful content and manage their emotional responses is not supported by the preponderance of current controlled experimental evidence. Multiple preregistered experiments and a meta-analysis have found that trigger warnings produce no significant reduction in distress, anxiety, or negative emotion among participants including those with trauma histories; no significant increase in avoidance of potentially distressing content; and possible small increases in anticipatory anxiety, particularly for those who hold strong beliefs about content harmfulness.

The claim remains contested. Effect sizes in most studies are small, and the typical experimental conditions may not fully represent the lived experience of people with severe clinical PTSD encountering immersive trauma-adjacent media. Arguments from autonomy — that people deserve information about content regardless of whether that information reduces distress — constitute a partially independent justification for warnings that empirical studies on emotional outcomes cannot entirely address.

However, the straightforward claim as commonly made — that trigger warnings help people with trauma avoid harmful content in ways that are measurably beneficial to wellbeing — does not have adequate evidential support. The institutional adoption of trigger warnings across universities, publishers, and media platforms has substantially outpaced the evidence base that would justify it.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The trigger warning evidence base is a case study in how policies can become institutionalised through intuitive logic and social signalling before the empirical work needed to evaluate them has been conducted. The widespread adoption of trigger warning policies in universities from approximately 2013 to 2018 was primarily driven by student advocacy, faculty responses to that advocacy, and administrative risk management — not by peer-reviewed psychology. By the time well-designed preregistered studies examining outcomes were published (primarily post-2018), the policies were already deeply embedded in institutional culture and surrounded by strong normative pressures that made revision difficult.

This sequence — institutionalise first, evaluate later — is not unique to trigger warnings. It reflects a broader pattern in which mental health interventions adopted through social-political channels rather than clinical channels bypass the clinical trial infrastructure that would ordinarily gate their adoption. Critical incident stress debriefing, widely deployed after traumatic events in the 1990s, followed a similar path: substantial institutional adoption followed by controlled trials finding no benefit and some harm, followed by difficult and incomplete policy revision (van Emmerik et al., 2002, *The Lancet*).

The more fundamental issue is that the intuitive model of trauma that underlies trigger warning advocacy — a model in which trauma is fragile, trauma survivors are best protected by shields rather than coping resources, and exposure to reminders is intrinsically dangerous rather than potentially therapeutic — is in tension with the clinical model that generates effective trauma treatments. That clinical model, grounded in exposure therapy and cognitive processing approaches, treats trauma not as a wound that must be kept clean but as a memory structure with anomalous processing properties that can be normalised through graduated safe exposure. A policy environment that institutionalises avoidance as protection may inadvertently work against the conditions under which therapeutic recovery proceeds.

None of this implies that content advisories have no legitimate function across all contexts. Journalism standards bodies have long maintained guidelines for content advisories before graphic violent or sexual imagery in news contexts, and there is a defensible practice of content description that functions as consumer information without claiming therapeutic benefit. The specific empirical problem is with the claim that trigger warnings, as deployed in educational and literary contexts, buffer trauma survivors against harm.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Trigger warning efficacy does not fit the standard Snopes-style fact-check format neatly because it is an empirical question in active scientific investigation rather than a specific verifiable factual claim. Major fact-checking platforms have not formally rated it. The empirical literature has been covered primarily in psychology and science journalism (The Atlantic, Pacific Standard, various academic news outlets) with increasing scepticism from 2019 onward as the null-effect studies accumulated.

For fact-checkers and media literacy educators approaching this claim, key steps include: (1) distinguishing the autonomy argument for trigger warnings from the efficacy argument — the question "do people deserve content information?" is separate from "does that information reduce distress?"; (2) identifying that the evidence base consists of preregistered experiments rather than only theoretical arguments; (3) noting that the clinical psychology literature on trauma treatment provides conceptual grounds for scepticism independent of the experimental studies; (4) acknowledging the methodological limitations of current studies (mostly non-clinical samples, short time horizons, written content) without using those limitations to dismiss the convergent null findings; and (5) noting that the policy adoption timeline was reversed from evidence-based clinical practice norms.

The epistemically demanding position — one that the research currently supports — is that trigger warnings are a well-intentioned intervention resting primarily on intuitive logic rather than empirical demonstration, that the controlled evidence so far consistently fails to show the promised benefit, and that the clinical framework most grounded in trauma science raises principled doubts about the protective value of avoidance-oriented interventions. This is a harder message to communicate than either "trigger warnings work" or "trigger warnings are censorship," but it is where the evidence points.
