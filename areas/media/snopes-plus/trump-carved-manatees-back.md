## The Claim

On January 11, 2021, wildlife officials in Florida announced that they were investigating a case of illegal harassment of a threatened Florida manatee. The manatee had been discovered on January 10 in the Homosassa River, a tidal river in Citrus County, Florida, with the word "TRUMP" etched into its back. The story was first published by the Citrus County Chronicle, which described the incident as involving someone having "scraped" the president's name into the manatee. The same day, the Center for Biological Diversity issued a news release describing the word as "carved" into the mammal's back, and stating that the animal had suffered "serious scarring in the form of the president's name."

Media personality Exavier Pope shared video of the manatee on Twitter. The footage went viral, garnering global coverage. The story arrived at a moment of heightened political temperature — the Capitol breach had occurred on January 6, five days earlier — and the manatee's injury, as widely reported, was interpreted by many as an instance of political violence displaced onto a protected animal, a bizarre coda to a turbulent political week.

The claim being evaluated — the one that required correction — was both specific and general. Specifically: was the word "carved" into the manatee's flesh, causing "serious scarring"? More broadly: was the harm to the animal as severe as initial reporting suggested?

---

## What's Actually True

The word "TRUMP" genuinely appeared on the manatee's back. That much was not disputed. Wildlife officials confirmed the incident, opened a federal investigation, and the Center for Biological Diversity offered a $5,000 reward for information leading to a conviction. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service confirmed the incident in an official statement.

However, the specific claim that the word had been "carved" into the manatee, causing "serious scarring," was incorrect. The word was traced into the algae growing on the manatee's back — not etched into the animal's skin or underlying tissue. Manatees (*Trichechus manatus latirostris*) are slow-moving aquatic mammals that spend much of their time at or near the water's surface in warm, shallow coastal waterways, estuaries, and rivers. Their large, paddle-shaped bodies accumulate algal growth on the dorsal skin surface, which is routinely exposed to sunlight and ambient water conditions conducive to algal colonisation. This green, mossy coating is a common feature of live manatees in their natural habitat and is visually distinguishable from the animal's underlying grey skin.

Writing on this algal surface is mechanically simple: dragging a finger or other implement through the soft algal mat leaves visible marks, in the same way that writing in condensation on a window leaves a mark. The marks do not penetrate or abrade the skin beneath, any more than clearing condensation injures the glass. Multiple news publications reported that wildlife officials had confirmed no serious injuries to the animal.

The Center for Biological Diversity issued a revised statement the following day, January 12, replacing the word "carved" with "written" in its news release, and removing the reference to "serious scarring." In a follow-up email to Snopes, the organization acknowledged that its original release had been based on "incorrect published reporting" and that the update was made in response to new information from wildlife experts.

The manatee in question was in North Florida's Homosassa River, a well-known manatee congregation area. Manatees congregate around warm-water springs in winter months because they are physiologically sensitive to cold — sea-surface temperatures below approximately 20 degrees Celsius (68 degrees Fahrenheit) cause cold stress syndrome in manatees, a debilitating hypothermic condition that can be fatal. The Homosassa Springs area hosts documented manatee populations and receives conservation attention accordingly.

The incident remained under federal investigation at the time of Snopes' reporting. The perpetrator was not identified in the published record. Anyone with information was directed to the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission hotline (888-404-3922) and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service tips line (844-397-8477).

## Why People Believe This

The Cardi Bee episode and this manatee incident both involve viral images that prompt rapid and emotionally engaged sharing. The manatee story, however, involves a genuine incident — something actually happened to this animal — and the question of how severe it was carries direct implications for legal and ethical assessments of the act. Understanding why the initial framing ("carved," "serious scarring") was so widely accepted, and why it required correction, reveals important dynamics in how media organisations and advocacy groups communicate about wildlife incidents.

**The political charge of the moment.** The Homosassa River manatee story broke five days after January 6, 2021, when a mob stormed the United States Capitol. The political atmosphere was electric with violence, and stories framing Trump-associated incidents as physically harmful were congruent with the emotional register of the moment. The framing of the manatee injury as "carving" — a verb connoting deliberate, forceful, blade-level damage — matched the psychological template of the week in which it occurred. Audiences were primed to interpret Trump-associated incidents in terms of serious physical harm, and the "carved" framing fit that priming.

**The advocacy framing of the initial source.** The Center for Biological Diversity is a legitimate and well-regarded conservation advocacy organisation, but its institutional role orients it toward presenting wildlife harm in its most serious available form. The initial news release describing the injury as "carved" with "serious scarring" reflected this institutional orientation — stronger framing serves the organisation's core mission of communicating wildlife threat — rather than deliberate fabrication. The factual error appears to have arisen from relying on published reports (specifically the Citrus County Chronicle's "scraped" description) without direct access to biological expertise about manatee skin and algal coating. The error was acknowledged and corrected within 24 hours, which is relatively fast for institutional communications.

**The "scraped" ambiguity of the original Citrus County Chronicle description.** The original reporting used the word "scraped," which is somewhat more accurate than "carved" but still implies skin-level contact. The ambiguity of "scraped" — does it mean scraped-through-algae or scraped-through-skin? — was resolved in the wrong direction by subsequent amplifiers, including the Center for Biological Diversity. Language choices in the primary reporting created downstream amplification in the more severe direction because more-severe framing is typically regarded as safer (underestimating harm is worse than overestimating it, in the dominant moral framework of wildlife journalism).

**Legitimate grievance and genuine illegality.** The fact that the ultimate harm was less severe than initially reported does not make the act acceptable, legal, or trivial. Harassing a manatee — irrespective of whether the harassment causes permanent physical injury — is a federal crime under the Marine Mammal Protection Act of 1972 and the Endangered Species Act of 1973, as well as a state crime under the Florida Manatee Sanctuary Act of 1978. Penalties for conviction include fines of up to $100,000 and up to one year in prison. The act of touching, tracing letters in, or otherwise interfering with a manatee constitutes illegal harassment under these statutes regardless of whether the algal scratching produced scarring. People who maintained that the act was serious and required investigation were correct, even if the specific characterisation of "carved with serious scarring" was not accurate.

**The dynamics of viral correction.** Lewandowsky, Ecker, and Cook (2017, *Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition*) found in a range of experimental contexts that corrections of misinformation are less effective when the corrected item is emotionally engaging and politically charged. The manatee story carried high emotional and political charge. The correction — downgrading "carved with serious scarring" to "written in algae, no serious injury" — ran against the emotional grain of the initial framing. This is a documented pattern: corrections of vivid, emotionally engaging initial claims are less likely to fully displace the initial representation from memory, even among audiences who intellectually accept the correction.

## Scientific Evidence: What the Literature Says

Manatee biology is relevant to understanding both why algal writing is possible and why it does not cause the injury "carving" implies.

West Indian manatees (*Trichechus manatus*) of the Florida subspecies (*T. m. latirostris*) are among the most thoroughly studied marine mammals in North America, given their threatened status and the extensive management interventions directed at their population. Marsh, O'Shea, and Reynolds (2011, *Ecology and Conservation of the Sirenia: Dugongs and Manatees*, Cambridge University Press) provide a definitive technical treatment of manatee biology including skin and integumentary system characteristics. Manatee skin is thick (reported at approximately 0.5–1.5 cm in some measures), relatively tough, and covered in fine hairs that are thought to serve mechanosensory functions (described by Reep, Finlay, and Stoll, 1998, in *Brain, Behavior and Evolution*). The dorsal surface of manatees in shallow warm-water environments routinely accumulates epiphytic algae, primarily green algae, forming a macroscopically visible coating that is entirely external to the skin surface.

The distinction between "written in algae" and "carved into skin" is not trivial from a welfare perspective. Propeller strike injuries to manatees — the most common category of serious human-caused manatee injury — produce deep lacerations through skin, subcutaneous tissue, and sometimes underlying musculature, visible as permanent scarring patterns that researchers use to identify individual animals over time (Beck, Bonde, and Rathbun, 1982, *Florida Scientist*). The scars from propeller strikes are permanent; they alter tissue structure and remain visible across decades of photographic monitoring. Writing in algae leaves no such mark on the underlying integument, and the algal coating regrows in the weeks following any disruption.

Reynolds and Odell (1991, *Manatees and Dugongs*, Facts on File Press) document the legal protections surrounding Florida manatees and note that the "harassment" standard in wildlife protection law is deliberately broad: it is intended to encompass disturbance that does not necessarily cause physical injury, because the energetic costs of disturbance — flight behaviour, altered foraging, cold-stress exacerbation through forced movement — can contribute to mortality even absent direct physical harm. This legal framework explains why the incident warranted federal investigation even after the absence of serious physical injury was confirmed.

Bossart et al. (2004, *Diseases of Aquatic Organisms*) documented the broader threat landscape for Florida manatees, noting that the species faces chronic pressure from watercraft strikes, entanglement in fishing gear, harmful algal blooms, and cold stress — each individually capable of causing injury or mortality, and collectively comprising a persistent population-level threat. In this context, the political salience of the "TRUMP" writing episode, while capturing significant media attention, was not reflective of the primary mortality drivers for the species. As of 2021, Florida manatee mortality from watercraft strikes was annually in the range of 100+ animals; the algal writing incident was a federal crime but not a significant biodiversity event by comparison.

The investigation status matters for a different reason: it tests whether wildlife protection laws, which cover harassment regardless of physical severity, are enforced consistently. The Center for Biological Diversity's Jaclyn Lopez characterised the act as reflective of someone "capable of doing grave violence," which may have been an overstatement of the immediate event but not necessarily of the character of intent involved.

## The Epistemic Gap: What Fact-Checking Doesn't Settle

Snopes confirmed the central factual question — the word was written in algae, not carved into skin — and rated the claim "Mixture" to reflect the compound truth: something real happened; the animal was genuinely harassed; but the characterisation of that harassment as "carved" and "seriously scarring" was incorrect.

What the fact-check does not address, and what remains genuinely unresolved, is the perpetrator question. The investigation was opened by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission, but there is no public record of a suspect being identified, charged, or convicted. This is epistemically significant for several reasons. Federal wildlife harassment cases are difficult to prosecute absent direct witness testimony or surveillance footage. The Homosassa Springs area hosts considerable ecotourism activity and has cameras at various conservation facilities, but the open waterway does not guarantee surveillance coverage.

The unresolved perpetrator question also means the political interpretation of the act — widely framed as an act of Trump-supporting political expression directed at a vulnerable animal — cannot be confirmed or disconfirmed. The political timing invited that inference; the absence of identified perpetrators leaves it permanently uncertain. It is possible the act was anti-Trump (writing the name as a form of desecration, not celebration), pro-Trump (writing the name in the same spirit as a tag or graffiti endorsement), or entirely unrelated to political ideology in any meaningful sense.

The media correction dynamics also deserve note. The Center for Biological Diversity issued a correction within 24 hours of its original release — this is relatively rapid and represents responsible organisational behaviour. However, corrections of widely-shared initial claims rarely achieve the same reach as the original. The "carved/serious scarring" version of the story reached a larger global audience than the "written in algae/no serious injury" correction, and this asymmetry is a structural feature of contemporary media ecosystems rather than a specific failure by any particular organisation.

---

## Verdict

**Mixture.** The word "TRUMP" genuinely appeared on a Florida manatee's back in January 2021. An endangered and legally protected animal was illegally harassed. A federal investigation was opened. Reward money was offered for information. These elements of the story are confirmed and true.

However, the specific characterisation that the word was "carved" into the manatee's back, causing "serious scarring," was incorrect. The word was written in the algae on the manatee's dorsal surface — a layer external to the skin — and wildlife officials confirmed no serious physical injury. The Center for Biological Diversity corrected its original statement within 24 hours of publication. The harassing someone was committing a federal crime regardless, as Florida manatees are protected under the Marine Mammal Protection Act, the Endangered Species Act, and the Florida Manatee Sanctuary Act. The perpetrator was not publicly identified.
