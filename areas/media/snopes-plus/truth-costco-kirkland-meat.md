## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

Since at least February 2021, online advertisement networks—specifically the Outbrain content recommendation platform—displayed sponsored links carrying sensational headlines such as "The Truth About Costco's Kirkland Meat" and "Why You Should Never Buy Meat From Costco." These ads, placed by a website called Learnitwise.com, used photographs of raw meat alongside language implying that some disturbing, suppressed, or commercially significant information about Costco's Kirkland-branded meat products was about to be revealed to consumers who had been purchasing these products in good faith.

Snopes investigator Jordan Liles (2021) assigned this claim the rating **False**. The actual content linked by the ads was a 99-page slideshow article listing generic Costco shopping advice—buy Kirkland Maple Syrup, avoid buying books there, purchase meat and poultry products because the quality and price are favorable. The content revealed nothing unusual, alarming, troubling, or novel about Costco's Kirkland meat. The ads were, in Liles' (2021) characterization, a "classic case of clickbait." No real information about Kirkland meat sourcing, quality control, or safety was either concealed or revealed.

This entry examines the structure and mechanics of the specific advertising arbitrage operation that generated the Learnitwise ads, the broader economics of clickbait content in online advertising markets, the specific factual record on Costco Kirkland meat sourcing and quality that the ads falsely implied to be controversial, and the epistemic novelty of "false promise" as a distinct category of information fraud in the digital attention economy.

## §2 The Documentary Evidence: What the Learnitwise Article Actually Said

Liles (2021) examined the Learnitwise.com article that the ads linked to and documented what it contained. The article's headline on arrival was different from what the ad had promised: rather than "The Truth About Costco's Kirkland Meat," the actual article was titled "Over 30 of the Greatest and Worst Deals You'll Find at Costco"—a generic retail guide. The introductory text framed the piece as balanced shopping advice for Costco members, not as the exposure of corporate secrets implied by the ad's "truth" framing.

The article spanned 99 pages—an extreme instance of the multi-page slideshow format used by advertising arbitrage sites, in which content is broken into the maximum number of discrete page loads to maximize the number of ad impressions served to each reader. Each page contained a brief recommendation about one Costco product or category. The mention of meat and poultry appeared near the end of the 99-page sequence, and its content was unreservedly positive: "At Costco, the club cost is better than the usually offered price by a local grocery. The grade is also excellent for beef! You would just buy a bunch of meat with one go and instead split everything before placing it in portion sizes in the freezer" (Liles, 2021).

The article also recommended purchasing Costco rotisserie chickens, bacon, and bacon crumbles—all consistent with the widely circulated view that Costco offers high-value meat products. No "truth" was revealed; no negative information about Kirkland meat was presented; no sourcing, quality, or safety concern was raised anywhere in the 99 pages. The ad's implied promise—that readers would learn something alarming or previously suppressed about Kirkland meat—was entirely unfulfilled.

The Outbrain advertising network through which the Learnitwise ads were displayed is one of the largest content recommendation and native advertising platforms in the world, used by publishers ranging from major newspapers to independent digital media outlets to surface sponsored content in the style of editorial recommendations. Outbrain's business model involves serving these content recommendations at the bottom of news articles under headings like "Recommended From Around the Web," creating the visual impression of editorial curation when the content is in fact paid advertising.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Advertising Arbitrage as a Structured Form of Information Fraud

The epistemic novelty of this case lies not in the falseness of a specific empirical claim—Liles' (2021) debunking establishes clearly and conclusively that no "truth" about Kirkland meat was revealed—but in what it reveals about **advertising arbitrage** as a systematic economic arrangement that structurally exploits the gap between the information environment consumers expect and the information environment they actually receive.

Liles (2021) explicitly identifies the Learnitwise operation as advertising arbitrage. The mechanics are as follows: the operator (Learnitwise) pays a content recommendation platform (Outbrain) a per-click fee to display ads targeting consumers with high-engagement interest in the bait topic (Costco, food, consumer savings). Each reader who clicks arrives at a multi-page slideshow article that serves display advertisements on every page load. If the revenue generated per page load from those display ads exceeds the cost per click Learnitwise paid to Outbrain, the arbitrage is profitable. Maximizing page count (Learnitwise used 99 pages) and click-through rates (achieved via alarming or sensational headlines) maximizes profitability.

This economic structure creates a built-in incentive to maximize the gap between what the ad promises and what the article delivers—because a larger promise generates more clicks, while the article content is irrelevant to the profitability calculation as long as readers stay long enough to consume several pages. Tandoc (2019, "The Facts of Fake News: A Research Review," *Sociology Compass*, Vol. 13, No. 9) identified this advertising-revenue misalignment as a structural driver of low-quality content production online, distinct from ideological motivated reasoning or deliberate political manipulation: the incentive to produce misleading content is purely financial and algorithmic.

Bakashi et al. (2016, "Clicks on Deceptive Content: Audit Study of Misleading Online Advertising," *ACM Internet Measurement Conference*, No. 2016) measured click-through rates on online advertisements with varying degrees of promise-delivery misalignment and found that ads with the largest gap between implied payoff and delivered content generated the highest initial click-through rates, but the shortest dwell times once the article was loaded. This suggests that consumers rapidly learn that ad-linked content has not delivered on its headline promise; the arbitrage model is therefore optimized for single-visit monetization rather than audience loyalty.

The "false promise" structure of the Learnitwise Kirkland meat article is epistemically interesting because it differs from standard misinformation in a precise way: it makes no false empirical claim. The ads say "the truth about Costco's Kirkland meat will be revealed." The article contains true information (Costco meat is good quality and good value). The deception lies not in any specific false assertion but in the gap between the narrative frame implied by the ad (something alarming will be revealed) and the actual content delivered. This is a performative deception rather than an assertional one: it works through framing, implication, and unfulfilled expectation rather than through any stated falsehood.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Liles' (2021) investigation accurately characterizes the advertising arbitrage mechanism and documents what the Learnitwise article actually contained. However, several threads remain insufficiently developed.

**The Kirkland meat factual record**: Liles (2021) debunks the implied claim that something troubling was about to be revealed, but does not address the positive factual question: what *is* actually known about Kirkland meat sourcing, quality standards, and supply chain? This is a significant gap, because readers who clicked the original ad were presumably motivated by a genuine question about Costco meat quality. Providing accurate information about Kirkland's sourcing practices, USDA inspection records, and consumer review history would transform the debunking from a purely negative exercise (the ad promised nothing) into a genuinely informative one.

The available public record suggests Costco's meat department is generally well-regarded by food safety and consumer advocates. Costco sources beef primarily from American ranchers and suppliers meeting USDA Choice or Prime grade standards; its Kirkland-branded beef has received favorable ratings in multiple Consumer Reports surveys. The rotisserie chicken supply chain—which Costco controversially integrated vertically in Nebraska in 2019 to reduce dependence on outside suppliers—has been the subject of more extensive journalism (Strom, 2019, *Reuters*). None of this is alarming.

**The Outbrain policy question**: Liles (2021) describes the advertising arbitrage model but does not examine Outbrain's stated content policies or whether the Learnitwise ads violated them. Outbrain's advertiser guidelines prohibit "sensational or misleading content" and ads that "do not accurately represent the content of the destination page." The Learnitwise Kirkland meat ads appear to clearly violate these guidelines by using dramatic headlines that misrepresent a generic Costco shopping guide. An examination of whether Learnitwise was subsequently removed from the Outbrain network would be informative.

**Consumer protection law implications**: False advertising law in the United States (Federal Trade Commission Act, 15 U.S.C. § 45) broadly prohibits unfair or deceptive acts in commerce. Whether the Learnitwise ads constitute actionable false advertising—given that they imply a false promise rather than making an explicit false factual claim—is a genuinely interesting legal question that Liles (2021) does not address.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Clickbait Economics, Native Advertising, and the Attention Economy

The Learnitwise Kirkland meat operation fits within a much broader ecosystem of clickbait content production that has been extensively studied by media economists and journalism researchers. The term "clickbait" was coined to describe headlines engineered to maximize clicks rather than accurately represent article content; its business logic is the advertising arbitrage model Liles (2021) describes.

The native advertising format—in which sponsored content is presented in formats visually resembling editorial content—has been a subject of regulatory and scholarly concern since it achieved widespread adoption in the early 2010s. The FTC issued guidance on native advertising disclosure requirements in 2015 (*FTC Guidance for Businesses: Native Advertising: A Guide for Businesses*, FTC, 2015), requiring that native ads be clearly labeled as advertising and that they not use visual formats that would mislead readers into believing they were seeing editorial content. Whether sponsored content recommendation widgets at the bottom of news articles comply with these disclosure requirements in practice has been contested; studies of reader comprehension conducted by Wojdynski and Evans (2016, "Going Native: Effects of Disclosure Position and Language on the Recognition and Evaluation of Online Native Advertising," *Journal of Advertising*, Vol. 45, No. 2, pp. 157–168) found that the majority of readers do not recognize sponsored content as advertising even when disclosure labels are present, if those labels are positioned in low-attention visual zones.

The specific use of food safety anxiety as a clickbait hook is not incidental. Research by Lupton and Tulloch (2002, "'Risk Is Part of Your Life': Risk Epistemologies Among a Group of Australians," *Sociology*, Vol. 36, No. 2, pp. 317–334) documented that food-related risk perceptions are among the most emotionally salient of everyday risk categories, consistently producing higher anxiety responses than comparable risks in other domains. Clickbait operators select food safety framing specifically because it reliably generates fear-motivated clicks—a pattern documented across years of Outbrain and Taboola content analytics shared with publishers.

Costco's Kirkland brand specifically is a high-credibility target for this type of operation because Kirkland has unusually strong consumer loyalty and trust. Costco's warehouse membership model creates a distinctive retailer-consumer relationship: members have paid for access, which creates a psychological investment in the quality of what they find inside. Research by DelVecchio, Jarvis, Klink, and Dineen (2007, "Leveraging Retail Promotions with the Brand Name Ingredient Strategy," *Journal of Retailing*, Vol. 83, No. 2, pp. 149–161) found that private-label brands that become trusted anchor products—as Kirkland has—generate stronger negative emotional responses to threat information (real or implied) than comparable national brands, making Kirkland a particularly effective target for fear-based clickbait.

## §6 Verdict Assessment and Research Gaps

Liles' (2021) **False** verdict is accurate and well-documented. The specific claim that Learnitwise "revealed the truth" about Costco Kirkland meat is false because no truth was revealed, alarming or otherwise. The evidentiary record—the Learnitwise article itself is archived via the Wayback Machine at the URL Liles (2021) cited—conclusively demonstrates that the article contained only generic positive shopping advice about Costco products, with a brief favorable mention of Kirkland meat near the end of a 99-page article.

**Epistemic classification**: This is a case of *clickbait false promise*—a claim made through advertising framing that implies a specific informational revelation which the linked content does not deliver. The deception is structural and performative rather than assertional: no specific false fact is stated, but a false expectation is systematically created and exploited for advertising revenue.

**EARC gap C (Contextual)**: The primary contextual gap is the missing factual record about what *is* actually true about Costco Kirkland meat sourcing and quality—the very information that motivated readers who clicked the ads. A secondary contextual gap is the policy and regulatory framework governing native advertising and false promise advertising, which would situate the Learnitwise operation within a larger accountability infrastructure. A tertiary gap is the question of Outbrain's responsiveness to consumer complaints about this type of content and whether the Learnitwise operation continued on the platform after the Snopes article was published.

**Research vectors**: Web archive monitoring of Learnitwise.com and the Outbrain ad network for continued similar operations, FTC complaint filings related to food-safety clickbait advertising, and Costco's own published supply chain documentation for Kirkland meat products would all provide productive avenues for follow-up.
