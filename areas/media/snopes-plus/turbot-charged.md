## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

An image depicting a food can bearing the label "Fish Assholes in Tomato Sauce" circulated widely on the internet from at least 2012 onward, typically appearing in listicles and image galleries under headings such as "Crazy Foods," "Disgusting Products You Won't Believe Are Real," and "Bizarre Supermarket Finds." The image was often presented without commentary, relying on viewers to draw their own conclusions about whether they were looking at a genuine commercial product from a real food manufacturer.

Snopes investigator David Mikkelson (2012) assigned this claim the rating **False**, in the sense that the item is not what it appears: the can does not contain actual fish anuses sold by a legitimate food company. Rather, it is a novelty or gag product—specifically a relabeled can of ordinary pasta in tomato sauce (typically a SpaghettiOs-style product) sold in novelty and souvenir shops, or a label-only product designed to be affixed by consumers to their own cans of supermarket pasta.

This entry examines the taxonomy of novelty food packaging as a commercial and social phenomenon, the anatomy of the specific "fish assholes" product and related novelty can formats, the mechanisms by which such images achieve viral traction despite (or because of) their absurdity, and the broader question of what "real" means when applied to novelty products intentionally designed to blur the line between authentic commercial goods and joke merchandise.

## §2 The Novelty Food Can Phenomenon: Anatomy and Commerce

The "fish assholes" can belongs to a well-established genre of consumer novelty goods. Novelty food packaging exploits the cognitive dissonance between familiar, trustworthy packaging formats—the standardized commercial can, with its structured label zones, brand typography, ingredient list, and net weight—and maximally inappropriate or absurd product descriptors. The humor derives from the collision between form (authoritative commercial container) and content (deliberately offensive or ridiculous labeling).

Mikkelson (2012) identified several related examples of the genre: bags of chocolate-covered nuts sold as "moose droppings" in tourist gift shops, tinned green beans sold as "porcupine peckers," and cans of condensed milk sold as "jackalope milk." These products form a coherent commercial category in the novelty and souvenir retail space, exploiting tourists' desire for humorous, regionally or thematically specific keepsakes that provoke a shock-and-laugh response. The "fish assholes" can fits squarely within this taxonomy.

The specific product as identified by Mikkelson (2012) is typically one of two things: (1) a commercially produced novelty item in which a standard can of SpaghettiOs-type pasta product is professionally relabeled with the "Fish Assholes in Tomato Sauce" label for retail sale in novelty shops; or (2) a standalone replacement label sold on eBay and similar platforms (a specific listing was identified by Mikkelson in the original Snopes article) that consumers purchase and affix to their own cans of pasta, producing a gag item suitable as a stocking stuffer or office prank. In either form, the contents are not fish anuses but ordinary circular pasta in a tomato-based sauce—SpaghettiOs being the paradigmatic example, because the "O" shaped pasta rings admit an anatomical reading that is part of the joke.

The commercial viability of such products rests on their function as social objects. As analyzed by Belk (1988, *Journal of Consumer Research*) in the context of gift-giving, novelty items derive value not from utility or even aesthetic pleasure but from their capacity to create a specific social experience—in this case, a moment of shared amusement or shock. The humor is predicated on the contrast between the implication of "fish assholes" as a literal product description and the actual harmlessness of pasta in tomato sauce; consuming or displaying the can becomes a social performance.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Authenticity Problem in Viral Product Images and the Ambiguity of "Real"

The viral spread of the "fish assholes" can image raises an underappreciated epistemological problem: the question of what it means for a novelty product to be "real." Snopes rated the item False as a food product—meaning it does not represent an actual commercial offering of fish anuses for human consumption. But the novelty item itself was and is purchasable. Physical cans bearing this label were produced, sold in novelty shops, and shipped to buyers on eBay. In one meaningful sense, the product is entirely real.

This ambiguity is not trivial. Standard fact-checking frameworks presuppose a binary between real products (legitimately sold, commercially produced, consuming entities) and fabricated images (photoshops, computer-generated mockups, entirely fictional compositions). The novelty food can occupies a third category: it is a real physical object commercially produced and sold, but it is not what its label describes—and, critically, it is designed to not be what its label describes. The humor depends on the gap between label and contents being legible to the purchaser. The "deception" is the joke; the product "is real" in the sense that it exists and was sold, but it is a real fake by design.

When such an object is photographed and circulated without its promotional context—stripped of the novelty shop shelf, the gag-gift listing, the "for entertainment purposes" framing—it enters a new evidential context in which viewers lack the cues needed to classify it correctly. Viewers who encounter the image in a "Crazy Foods" listicle face genuine uncertainty: Is this a regional specialty from an unfamiliar market? Is it a product from a foreign country with different culinary norms? Is it a deliberate joke product? This uncertainty is what generates engagement and sharing. The image's viral power comes precisely from the classificatory ambiguity it embodies.

This dynamic has been theorized in the context of internet humor and digital folklore. Shifman (2014, *Memes in Digital Culture*, MIT Press) notes that memes and viral images frequently derive their propagation energy from semantic ambiguity—content that admits multiple readings simultaneously generates more engagement than content that resolves clearly into a single interpretation. The "fish assholes" image is funny if it is fake (novelty absurdism), funny in a different way if it is somehow real (cross-cultural disgust humor), and funny yet another way if viewers are uncertain (shared bewilderment). The image functions across all three registers simultaneously.

The broader epistemological issue is that novelty products designed to mimic official commercial packaging exploit the cognitive shortcuts that consumers and fact-checkers use to evaluate authenticity. Professional label design, realistic printing, standard can formats, and plausible nutritional-information panels all serve as heuristic signals of authenticity that fact-checkers routinely use. When those signals are deliberately replicated on a joke product, the heuristics fail. This is structurally similar to the challenge posed by deepfakes in video content: the markers of authenticity (here, professional packaging cues; there, realistic facial rendering) are reproduced without the underlying authentic object.

## §4 The Turbot Question: Why "Turbot-Charged"?

The Snopes article's slug, "turbot-charged," is a pun that refers to the turbot (*Scophthalmus maximus*), a large flatfish native to European Atlantic coastal waters and the Mediterranean, considered a premium commercial seafood species. "Turbo-charged" is a familiar idiom meaning enhanced or amplified; the slug substitutes the fish name for the prefix. This punning title suggests the article author associated the "fish assholes" claim with turbot specifically, though the claim as examined by Mikkelson (2012) does not identify the specific species of fish described on the novelty label.

Turbot has an interesting commercial history that is tangentially relevant here. As a premium fish commanding high market prices in European markets—turbot achieved near-iconic status in French haute cuisine through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries (Davidson, 1999, *The Oxford Companion to Food*)—it would be unexpected as the named species on a novelty low-brow product. The joke inherent in attaching the name of a prestige fish to an anatomical-humor product exploits the incongruity between high-status and low-status culinary registers. This register collision was already present in the original novelty product as a deliberate comedic strategy.

The formal taxonomy of fish processing waste is also relevant. During industrial fish processing, various parts of the fish that are not conventionally consumed in Western markets—heads, tails, viscera, swim bladders, and yes, cloacal tissue—are separated from the edible fillet. In many global culinary traditions, however, these "waste" parts of fish are considered delicacies or staple ingredients. Dried fish maws (swim bladders) are expensive luxury ingredients in East Asian cuisines. Roe from multiple species is commercially valuable. Cod tongues are a traditional Newfoundland food. The entire category of "fish assholes" as an imaginary product category actually has a real-world referent in global food culture: the rectum and cloacal regions of various fish species are consumed in some culinary traditions, though not typically in North American or Northern European mainstream markets. This adds another layer to the image's resonance—the revulsion it triggers in Western viewers is partly a culturally specific disgust response, not a universal one (Rozin & Fallon, 1987, *Psychological Review*; Haidt, McCauley & Rozin, 1994, *Psychological Assessment*).

## §5 Novelty Goods, Platform Economics, and the Misinformation Pipeline

The journey of the "fish assholes" image from novelty shop shelf to viral internet claim illustrates a specific pathway in the modern misinformation pipeline that involves commercial novelty goods. Unlike straightforwardly fabricated images, novelty product images originate with real objects that were intentionally manufactured and sold. They then pass through multiple platform contexts—eBay listings, novelty shop websites, photographed by customers, uploaded to social platforms—and at each transition, the original commercial context (and the clear signal "this is a joke product") tends to be stripped away.

By the time an image reaches a mainstream "Weird Foods" listicle or is shared without caption on a general social media feed, the commercial context that would allow correct classification has been systematically removed. The image has been laundered through the platform stack from commercial object to decontextualized visual claim. This stripping of context is not necessarily intentional deception at any specific step; it may result from platform norms around image sharing that do not encourage citation of sources, from screenshot cropping that removes URL or platform cues, or simply from the low friction of image forwarding.

From a public epistemics standpoint, this pathway represents a distinct challenge from intentional disinformation. No individual actor deliberately created false information about the contents of the can or manufactured slander about a food manufacturer. The deception, such as it is, is an emergent property of platform context-stripping applied to a product designed to be ambiguous. Efforts to counter it through individual fact-check articles (as Snopes did) are effective at a micro level but structurally insufficient given the asymmetry between claim-generation rates (images spread continuously across platforms) and fact-check production rates.

Wardle and Derakhshan (2017, *Information Disorder*, Council of Europe) would classify this type of content as *mis*information rather than *dis*information—content that creates false impressions without necessarily involving malicious intent to deceive. The novelty product manufacturer's intent was humor, not deception. The platform users who shared the image typically intended amusement or curiosity-sharing, not fabrication. Yet the downstream informational effect is that viewers form false beliefs about whether a product called "fish assholes" is commercially marketed.

## §6 Conclusion, EARC Assignment, and Epistemic Gap

**EARC Rating: C (Contextually Misleading)**

The "fish assholes" can image is not a falsified or fabricated photograph—it depicts a real physical object that was commercially produced and sold. However, as a representation of ordinary commercial food offerings, it is false: no legitimate food manufacturer sells fish anuses in tomato sauce at retail. The item is a novelty or gag product, typically a relabeled can of pasta in tomato sauce or a standalone novelty label designed to be affixed to such a can. The Snopes rating of False is therefore accurate with respect to the implied claim that this represents a genuine food offering, while acknowledging that a real object underlies the image.

The C (Contextually Misleading) rating is assigned because the deception is not embedded in the image itself, which faithfully depicts a real object, but in the decontextualized circulation contexts that strip away the novelty-product framing. In its original commercial context, the product is honest and transparent about its joke function. In viral circulation, that transparency is lost.

**Epistemic Gap:** The novelty product-to-misinformation pipeline—whereby genuine commercial novelty goods lose their context through platform circulation and come to be perceived as authentic commercial products—is inadequately documented and studied. A systematic analysis of this pipeline would require cataloging the life cycle of novelty product images across platforms, from original commercial listing through successive re-shares, measuring the rate of context loss at each platform transition and the resulting rate of misclassification in viewer audiences. No such study appears to have been conducted as of this writing.
