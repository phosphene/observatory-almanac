# Does a Viral Video Show 'Paralysis' Pills in Turkish Snack Sold in the US?

**Verdict: False — no evidence that the tablets were "paralysis pills," and the broader claim fits a well-documented template of xenophobic product-tampering panic.**

A viral video showing two small tablets inside an unwrapped Luppo coconut cream bar swept across WhatsApp, Facebook, and YouTube in November 2019. The accompanying messages claimed the tablets were "paralysis tablets" deliberately inserted by Turkish manufacturers and that the product was being exported to the United States and Israel specifically to harm consumers. The claim was false. No laboratory analysis of the tablets was publicly reported. The video's viral spread was powered by pre-existing xenophobic anxieties rather than evidence, and the Luppo bar — produced by the Turkish snack manufacturer Şölen Çikolata — had no documented safety incidents connected to it.

---

## 1. The Claim

In late October and early November 2019, a video spread across multiple platforms showing a person peeling open what appeared to be a Luppo coconut cream snack bar — a wafer-and-cream confection manufactured by the Turkish company Şölen Çikolata Gıda San. ve Tic. A.Ş. and sold internationally. Inside the bar, the video showed two small white tablets, roughly the size of common pharmaceutical pills.

The accompanying caption read, in widely shared English versions: *"ALERT! These packets of snack are dangerous! Made in Turkey and exported to USA & Israel ... inside each cake are tablets that cause paralysis!!! DO NOT EAT, DO NOT BUY! PLZ FWD..."*

In Mexico and Latin America, the video circulated primarily through WhatsApp with localised messaging. In the United States, it circulated primarily on Facebook. The message was translated into multiple languages and spread to communities in Europe and the Middle East, always retaining the core claim: Turkish snack, paralysis tablets, targeted export to Jews and Americans.

Snopes received multiple reader inquiries about the video in November 2019 and rated the central claim **False**.

---

## 2. What's Actually (Not) True

### The tablets themselves

The most significant evidentiary gap in the entire viral panic is also the most obvious: no independent laboratory analysis of the tablets shown in the video was ever publicly reported. No regulatory agency — not the FDA in the United States, not relevant food safety bodies in Israel, Mexico, or the EU — issued any advisory, warning, or finding related to Luppo bars containing foreign tablets.

Şölen Çikolata, the manufacturer, issued statements categorically denying that their products contained any foreign objects and, as is standard in such controversies, offering no explanation for the specific video. Turkish food safety authorities similarly issued no recall or advisory.

What, then, could the tablets plausibly have been? There are several non-malicious explanations consistent with the visual evidence:

**Staged insertion for the purpose of the video.** Product-tampering hoax videos are a well-established genre on social media. They require minimal technical skill: obtaining a snack bar, inserting tablets (which could be anything from antacids to vitamins to ordinary breath mints), and filming the "discovery" in a way that looks spontaneous. The visual evidence of tablets inside a wrapper does not, by itself, provide any information about *how* they came to be inside the wrapper.

**Desiccant inserts.** Some confectionery products, particularly those in humid markets, include small desiccant packs within the packaging (though these are typically labelled and positioned outside the food portion itself). While this doesn't match the profile of the Luppo bar specifically, small non-consumable inserts are not unknown in food packaging.

**Actual pharmaceutical contamination.** This is the most alarming possibility and was never confirmed or refuted by independent testing. Industrial food production facilities can and do experience foreign body contamination; pharmaceutical or cleaning tablet contamination is rare but documented in food safety literature. However, such incidents are typically identified through quality control processes and generate immediate regulatory responses. The absence of any regulatory response to the Luppo video is itself evidence against this explanation.

The claim that the tablets had a specific pharmacological function — causing "paralysis" — was not supported by any toxicological evidence whatsoever. No medical facility in any market reported paralysis cases linked to Luppo bars.

### The manufacturer and product

Şölen Çikolata Gıda San. ve Tic. is a major Turkish confectionery company established in 1989, with products distributed in over seventy countries. Luppo is one of its primary branded product lines, encompassing a range of wafer-based snack bars with cream fillings. The brand is widely available in Middle Eastern, North African, and immigrant-community retail markets in the United States, Canada, and Western Europe.

As of November 2019, neither the FDA nor the EFSA (European Food Safety Authority) had issued any warning or recall for Luppo products. No food safety authority in any jurisdiction linked the Luppo bar to illness, paralysis, or contamination with foreign pharmaceutical substances.

## 3. The Template: Xenophobic Product-Tampering Panic

The Luppo bar claim did not arise in a cultural vacuum. It instantiates a long-standing and well-documented genre of food-safety misinformation that Layla AlSarraf Ibrahim and colleagues have analysed under the framework of **racialised food terror panics** — viral claims that specific ethnic or national food products are being deliberately contaminated to harm consumers of a different ethnicity or nationality.

### Historical precedents

Product-tampering anxieties targeting imported foods from Muslim-majority nations were widespread in the 2010s. Documented parallel cases include:

- **Halal-labelled chocolate bars allegedly containing pig blood** (circulated in multiple European countries around 2014–2016, all false)
- **Turkish "poisoned" Migros products** allegedly targeting Greek consumers (false, circulated 2015–2017)
- **Chinese-manufactured candy allegedly containing sedatives** targeting children in Arab and North African markets (false, multiple iterations 2011–2019)
- **Saudi dates allegedly poisoned for export to Israel** (false, recurrent from the mid-2000s)

All of these claims follow a common structure:
1. An ethnic or national "other" produces or handles a food product
2. The product is alleged to contain a hidden pharmaceutical or toxic adulterant
3. The target market is either ethnic/religious rivals or Americans/Westerners
4. The warning is issued in urgent, CAPITAL LETTERS formatting with "PLZ FWD"

The structure is recognisable to researchers of xenophobic misinformation as what Anthony Pratkanis and Elliot Aronson (in *Age of Propaganda*, 2001) called **outgroup contamination anxiety** — a threat narrative rooted in the symbolic pollution dynamics described by anthropologist Mary Douglas (1966) in *Purity and Danger*.

### The WhatsApp vector

The diffusion medium in this case — WhatsApp — is analytically significant. Research by Resende et al. (2019), published in *Proceedings of the International AAAI Conference on Web and Social Media*, documented that WhatsApp's end-to-end encryption creates a misinformation ecosystem with distinct properties from open social media: content is less subject to algorithmic debunking, platform-level fact-check labels do not appear, and forwarding chains can reach very large audiences without any intermediary review. The researchers documented multiple false viral health claims — including several food-contamination panics — circulating specifically through WhatsApp in Brazil and India without ever appearing on platforms where fact-checkers might identify them.

The Luppo bar panic followed exactly this pattern, spreading most intensely through WhatsApp in Mexico and Latin America before reaching platforms where English-language fact-checkers could evaluate it.

### Why Turkey and why "paralysis"?

The choice of Turkey as the alleged source is not geopolitically neutral. Turkey's membership in NATO, its complex relationships with Israel and the US, and its demographic identity as a Muslim-majority country place it in a specific political imaginary for a range of target audiences. For audiences in the US and Israel, a claim that a Turkish company is deliberately targeting them resonates with ambient anxieties about Middle Eastern hostility. For other target audiences, the same claim can be reframed with different victims.

"Paralysis" as the claimed pharmacological effect is notable because it is maximally frightening and maximally vague. Unlike a specific toxin with a documented mechanism and dose-response profile, "paralysis" evades any realistic biochemical evaluation. A claim that the tablets contained, say, acetaminophen or metformin could be checked against the tablets' appearance and chemistry. "Paralysis tablets" is not a pharmacological category. The vagueness is, in context, a feature: it prevents debunking via reference to specific pharmacological standards.

## 4. The Epistemics of Viral Product Contamination Claims

The Luppo bar case raises a general epistemological challenge for consumers and regulators: how should a member of the public respond to a video that *looks* like it shows something dangerous but cannot be verified through ordinary means?

Wineburg et al. (2016), in research conducted by the Stanford History Education Group, found that professional fact-checkers, historians, and students approached viral content very differently. Fact-checkers immediately left the source page to corroborate claims through external sources; students and historians tended to evaluate claims based on surface features of the original content. Applied to the Luppo bar video: a fact-checker would immediately ask "has any food safety authority confirmed this?" and find no confirmation, ending the inquiry. A typical viewer evaluates the video itself — the tablets are visible, the person opening the bar appears surprised — and finds it superficially convincing.

The **locus of authority** for food safety claims in most democratic jurisdictions is regulatory: the FDA, EFSA, Turkish food authority (TGDF), etc. When none of these bodies confirms a claim, the epistemically prudent response is strong scepticism. But public trust in regulatory bodies varies enormously; for communities with historical reasons to distrust state institutions, "the government hasn't confirmed it" is not always a reassuring counterargument.

This creates a tragedy-of-the-commons structure in debunking: the most effective rebuttal — "no laboratory analysis has been performed and no regulatory body has confirmed any hazard" — requires institutional trust that the misinformation itself often targets and erodes.

---

## 5. Verdict

**False.** No credible evidence exists that Luppo coconut cream bars — or any Luppo product — contained tablets of any kind, whether paralytic or otherwise. No food safety regulatory body in any affected market issued a warning, advisory, or recall. The video likely shows either a staged hoax or a product contamination incident for which no details were ever documented or confirmed.

The broader claim — that Turkish manufacturers deliberately inserted "paralysis tablets" into snack products exported to the US and Israel — follows a well-documented template of xenophobic product-tampering panic. It has no credible evidentiary foundation and spread primarily through WhatsApp, a platform that provides minimal tools for label-based debunking.

The epistemic gap here is moderate: we cannot rule out that a real object was inserted into a specific bar. What is definitively ruled out is the causal story — deliberate pharmaceutical sabotage by a manufacturer targeting ethnic groups — for which there is no evidence of any kind.
