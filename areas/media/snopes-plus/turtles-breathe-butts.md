# Can Some Turtles Breathe Through Their Butts?


## 1. The Claim

The claim that some turtles can "breathe through their butts" has enjoyed a long and intermittent internet circulation, periodically re-erupting into viral attention. It achieved a significant popular culture moment in November 2019 when Disney's animated film *Frozen 2* was released in the United States: the snowman character Olaf, during a monologue delivering miscellaneous curiosities about the world, mentions that turtles breathe through their butts. The claim thereby entered the consciousness of millions of movie-goers as either a delightful zoological fact or a cartoon non-sequitur, depending on the viewer's prior knowledge.

The claim was revived again in early May 2021 through a Reddit thread titled "What is the weirdest fact you know?" — a genre of community Q&A that regularly surfaces obscure but accurate trivia alongside complete fabrications. The specific formulation returned in this context directed attention back to the turtle claim, prompting sufficient social media traffic and verification requests that Snopes addressed it directly.

The claim is true, though technically imprecise in exactly the way that makes it memorable: turtles do not breathe "through their butts" in the sense of performing lung-equivalent respiration via the anus in a way that is anatomically or functionally equivalent to pulmonary breathing. What some turtle species do is absorb dissolved oxygen from water via a specialized pair of thin-walled, highly vascularized pouches (bursae) that open off the cloaca — performing aquatic gas exchange through posterior anatomy in a process called *cloacal respiration* or *cloacal bursae respiration*.

The distinction between "butts" and "cloaca" (addressed in companion entry on frog cloacal anatomy) is relevant here too: the cloaca is the single multipurpose posterior opening through which excretory, reproductive, and in specific turtles, respiratory functions occur. "Butt breathing" is a colloquial shortcut for a specific physiological process, and its technical inaccuracy does not affect the claim's essential truth: dissolved oxygen uptake through posterior cloacal bursae is documented in multiple turtle species, confers genuine survival advantages, and has been confirmed through multiple lines of physiological research.


## 2. What's Actually True

Turtles typically breathe oxygen via their lungs — air enters through the mouth and is drawn into the lungs by expansion of the body cavity (complicated in turtles by their rigidly fused rib cage, as detailed in the companion entry on turtle shell biology). Metabolic carbon dioxide crosses the lung tissue into the bloodstream and is exhaled. This pulmonary respiration is standard vertebrate respiratory physiology.

However, several aquatic turtle species have developed an additional or supplementary respiratory mechanism that operates during extended periods of aquatic submersion. The relevant structure is the cloaca, which in aquatic turtles connects to a pair of thin-walled, highly vascularized accessory respiratory chambers called *cloacal bursae*. Water is pumped in and out of these bursae by specialized cloacal muscles; oxygen dissolved in the water diffuses across the thin bursal wall and into the bloodstream, while carbon dioxide diffuses in the reverse direction. This constitutes genuine aquatic gas exchange — functionally analogous to gill respiration in fish, though anatomically distinct and structurally far less elaborate.

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS), in documented educational materials about the bog turtle, provides the clearest official description: "Some aquatic turtles, including the bog turtle, have openings off the cloaca, called bursae, which are densely laced with blood vessels," and water entering these chambers allows oxygen uptake sufficient for extended underwater survival. The bursae function as accessory respiratory organs during aquatic torpor (the metabolic slowdown that allows turtles to overwinter submerged in iced-over ponds and bogs).

The most well-studied species in North America for this ability is the *Glyptemys muhlenbergii* (bog turtle) — the smallest turtle species in North America at less than 4 inches as an adult and identified by distinctive orange-yellow patches on the head and neck. The bog turtle's range extends from New York south to Georgia, with isolated populations in several Appalachian states. It is federally listed as Threatened under the Endangered Species Act due to habitat loss, illegal collection for the pet trade, and the ongoing degradation of the calcareous wetland habitats (fens and boggy meadows) in which it specializes.

During winter, bog turtles burrow into the mud at the bottom of their bog habitats, sometimes to depths of up to two feet, under water that may be frozen at the surface. In this condition — cold, low-metabolic-rate, surrounded by only partially oxygenated mud-water — pulmonary breathing is impossible and supplementary oxygen uptake through cloacal bursae becomes essential for survival. The cloacal bursae provide enough oxygen exchange to sustain the minimal metabolic demands of a cold-torpid turtle, enabling overwintering in conditions that would asphyxiate an animal relying exclusively on lungs.

Snopes author Madison Dapcevich (May 2021) confirms all the relevant facts: cloacal respiration is real, the anatomy is as described (bursae, fimbriae), and the FWS confirms the mechanism. The piece also notes the claim's first major popular culture moment in *Frozen 2* and the Reddit thread that revived it in 2021. The verdict of "True" is unambiguously correct.

Additionally, Dapcevich notes that cloacal respiration is not unique to turtles among aquatic animals: sea cucumbers and aquatic juvenile dragonflies (dragonfly nymphs) perform analogous posterior gas exchange, suggesting that this mechanism has evolved multiple times independently in animals that benefit from supplementary aquatic respiration using posterior anatomical structures.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The Snopes fact-check verified the claim and provided a clear anatomical explanation with appropriate scientific sourcing. But the biological and evolutionary depth of cloacal respiration — including the physiological mechanism in more detail, the comparative biology across diverse animal taxa, the ecological function during overwinter survival, and the conservation crisis facing the bog turtle — is almost entirely absent from the fact-check and from popular coverage of this claim.

**The physiological mechanism in detail**: The Snopes article describes the anatomy correctly but does not explain the physiological mechanism of gas exchange in the bursae. Gas exchange in biological systems — whether in lungs, gills, or cloacal bursae — operates on the same basic physical principle: gases diffuse down concentration gradients across thin, highly vascularized membranes. For cloacal bursae to function as respiratory organs, they require three architectural features: (1) a thin membrane with high permeability to dissolved gases, (2) a dense capillary network on the tissue side of the membrane, maintaining a steep concentration gradient for oxygen uptake, and (3) a mechanism for renewing the water in contact with the membrane to prevent local depletion of dissolved oxygen.

The bog turtle's bursae achieve all three: the bursal wall is extremely thin (a few cell layers), is dense with capillaries, and is refreshed by the rhythmic muscular pumping of water in and out of the cloacal opening. The fimbriae — small, finger-like projections described in the Snopes article — increase the surface area available for gas exchange, analogous to the alveoli of mammalian lungs or the secondary lamellae of fish gills, both of which achieve high gas exchange rates through surface area maximization (Schmidt-Nielsen, 1997, *Animal Physiology: Adaptation and Environment*, Cambridge University Press, Chapter 7: Respiratory systems; Ultsch & Jackson, 1982, "Long-Term Submergence at 3°C of the Turtle *Chrysemys picta bellii*," *Journal of Experimental Biology*, 96(1), 11–28).

The efficiency of cloacal respiration is insufficient to support normal activity metabolism — a turtle trying to swim, forage, or engage in thermoregulatory basking cannot meet its oxygen needs through cloacal exchange alone. Cloacal respiration is a supplement to pulmonary breathing in cool, aquatic, low-activity conditions — specifically the cold winter submersion during which metabolic rate is drastically reduced by low temperature. At near-zero temperatures, metabolic oxygen demand falls precipitously (governed by the Q₁₀ principle, by which metabolic rate roughly halves for every 10°C decrease in temperature), and the limited but continuous oxygen supply from cloacal bursae is sufficient to maintain basic cellular function indefinitely.

**The comparative biology of posterior respiration — a convergent evolutionary solution**: The fact-check briefly notes that sea cucumbers and aquatic juvenile dragonflies also perform posterior gas exchange. This comparative observation deserves deeper examination because it reveals a genuinely interesting pattern in the evolution of aquatic respiration.

Sea cucumbers (class Holothuroidea, phylum Echinodermata) perform *cloacal respiration* via a structure called the *respiratory trees* — paired, branched organs extending into the body cavity from the cloaca, through which seawater is pumped to provide gas exchange. Sea cucumbers are not vertebrates and their "cloaca" is not homologous to the vertebrate cloaca — it is an independently evolved posterior opening. Yet the functional solution is strikingly parallel: a posterior opening connected to vascularized respiratory tissue through which water is pumped to extract dissolved oxygen.

Aquatic insect larvae have independently evolved analogous mechanisms multiple times. Dragonfly nymphs (*Anisoptera*) possess a modified rectal chamber (*rectal branchial chamber* or *proctodeal branchial chamber*) through which water is pumped in and out by muscles, while oxygen is extracted from the water across the wall of the chamber lined with gill tissue (= *rectal gills*). This is functionally equivalent to whether you call it cloacal breathing (turtles) or rectal gill respiration (dragonfly nymphs) — dissolved oxygen from water is extracted through posterior anatomy (Corbet, 1999, *Dragonflies: Behavior and Ecology of Odonata*, Cornell University Press; Eriksen, 1986, "Respiratory Roles of the Caudal Lamellae in Coenagrionidae," *Journal of the North American Benthological Society*, 5(4), 278–288).

Some aquatic moth larvae (Lepidoptera: Pyralidae, subfamily Acentropinae) and even some aquatic beetle larvae have independently evolved posterior or cutaneous gas exchange mechanisms. The repeated, independent evolution of posterior aquatic respiration across phylogenetically distant animal groups — echinoderms, insects, and vertebrate turtles — is a remarkable instance of convergent evolution, reflecting that the physical constraints of aquatic gas exchange (where oxygen concentration is much lower than in air, diffusion rates are slower, and access to the atmosphere may be restricted) favor similar architectural solutions regardless of the organism's evolutionary history.

The fact-check mentions the sea cucumber and dragonfly examples but does not identify this as an instance of convergent evolution or explain why the same solution has been independently invented multiple times in such different organisms. This is the most scientifically interesting observation in the entire domain of "butt breathing" biology and it is entirely absent from popular coverage.

**The winter survival ecology of bog turtles and the "ice free" paradox**: The specific ecological context in which bog turtle cloacal respiration is most functionally important — winter submersion under frozen ponds and bogs — raises an interesting physiological sub-problem. When the surface of a bog or fen freezes, the water below the ice becomes sealed from atmospheric oxygen. Over extended periods, biological oxygen demand (from decaying organic matter and remaining aquatic organisms) can deplete dissolved oxygen in the sealed water body. This creates the risk that the water into which the bog turtle is pumping for cloacal respiration may itself become hypoxic (low oxygen) or anoxic (no oxygen), eliminating the benefit of cloacal breathing.

Research on extended winter submersion in turtles has found that species face this challenge and have evolved complementary strategies. Several aquatic turtle species (notably *Chrysemys picta*, the painted turtle) can sustain winter submersion in partially anoxic water through *anaerobic metabolism*, tolerating the buildup of lactic acid that results from anaerobic glycolysis to a degree that would be fatal in most vertebrates (Ultsch & Jackson, 1982, cited above). The painted turtle is the most studied species for anoxia tolerance, but bog turtles in appropriate habitats (bogs with continuous cold-spring-fed water inflow) typically have access to better-oxygenated water than stagnant pond turtles and rely more heavily on cloacal bursae respiration and less on full anoxia tolerance.

**The cloacal bursae and reproductive physiology — a dual-use structure**: The cloacal bursae in turtles are not solely respiratory in function. The Snopes article mentions fimbriae — the small finger-like projections increasing surface area — identifies these as serving "reproductive purposes," which is correct. In female turtles, the cloacal bursae are involved in sperm storage: turtles are among the vertebrates capable of storing viable sperm for extended periods after mating, with documented sperm storage times exceeding four years in some species (Gist & Congdon, 1998, "Reproductive Tract Secretions and Sperm Storage in Female Turtles," *Journal of Experimental Zoology*, 281(5), 399–405). The cloacal bursae and associated tubules provide a protected, isothermal environment for sperm storage, utilizing the same vascularized tissue that performs gas exchange in a structurally distinct but overlapping anatomical region.

This dual-use character of the cloacal bursae — respiration during winter submersion and sperm storage year-round — is a remarkable example of structural multifunctionality and is entirely absent from popular accounts of "butt breathing." It means that the same anatomical structure that allows a bog turtle to survive under ice for months also allows female bog turtles to delay fertilization until conditions are appropriate for nest construction and egg development, providing both direct winter survival value and reproductive strategy flexibility.

**Conservation urgency — the bog turtle's crisis**: The fact-check correctly notes that the bog turtle is federally listed as Threatened, but does not convey the urgency of its conservation situation. Bog turtles are among the most imperiled turtle species in the United States. Their decline is driven by:

Habitat loss and degradation: Calcareous fens and boggy meadows — the specific wet meadow ecosystems in which bog turtles specialize — have been dramatically reduced in extent across the eastern United States through drainage for agriculture, development, and hydrological alteration. The specialized hydrology of these habitats (cold spring-fed seeps, sphagnum moss coverage, low nutrients) is difficult to recreate once disrupted. Remaining habitat is fragmented into small, isolated patches between which bog turtles have limited dispersal capacity.

Illegal collection: Bog turtles are among the most sought-after species in the illegal turtle trade due to their small size, attractive coloration (the distinctive orange patches on head and neck), and rarity. A single individual fetches several hundred to several thousand dollars on the black market. This price premium has driven intensive collection from some wild populations. The federal Threatened listing and CITES Appendix I listing (which prohibits international commercial trade) have not eliminated illegal collection.

Succession and vegetation change: Bog turtle habitat requires open, low-canopy conditions — the vegetation is naturally sparse, kept open by historical grazing and periodic flooding. Without active management, productive bog turtle habitat succeeds to shrub and forest, reducing the open wet meadow that bog turtles require for basking and nesting. Active management of bog turtle habitat typically involves periodic brush clearing, water level management, and controlled grazing to maintain appropriate vegetation structure (Ernst & Lovich, 2009, *Turtles of the United States and Canada*, Johns Hopkins University Press).

The cloacal respiration that allows bog turtles to survive under ice for months is not just a biological curiosity — it is a physiological adaptation essential to survival in an increasingly threatened ecosystem. Understanding this biology is part of understanding what the bog turtle is and why its loss would represent an irreplaceable ecological and evolutionary tragedy.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** True (cloacal respiration via bursae is well-documented in bog turtles and related species)  
**Snopes Verdict:** True  
**Confidence:** High (multiple independent scientific and governmental sources confirm the mechanism)  
**Epistemic Basis:** Herpetological physiology, FWS documentation, comparative respiratory biology  
**Phenomenological Status:** Counterintuitive but true; the folksy framing ("breathe through their butts") is technically imprecise but functionally captures the essential reality  
**Mechanistic Status:** Dissolved oxygen uptake via cloacal bursae during aquatic overcwintering is confirmed by physiological measurement  
**Folk Wisdom Value:** High — the "butt breathing" formulation, while imprecise, successfully communicates genuine and surprising biology and is memorable as a gateway to deeper learning

The claim is true: some turtle species can absorb dissolved oxygen from water via cloacal bursae — thin-walled, highly vascularized pouches opening off the cloaca — during winter submersion in cold water. This process, called cloacal respiration, enables extended underwater survival when air breathing is impossible. The North American bog turtle (*Glyptemys muhlenbergii*) is among the most-studied species exhibiting this capability. The *Frozen 2* and Reddit viral moments accurately represent genuine biology, and Snopes' "True" verdict is correct.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The bog turtle's cloacal respiration is one component of a broader biological story about how turtles have adapted to temperate freshwater environments over millions of years. The Order Testudines comprises approximately 360 extant species showing an extraordinary range of morphological and physiological adaptations to diverse habitats — marine (sea turtles), freshwater (most North American freshwater turtles), terrestrial (tortoises and box turtles), and transitional environments.

**Anoxia tolerance in turtles — a comparative physiology landmark**: The painted turtle (*Chrysemys picta*) is the most intensively studied turtle for physiological responses to low oxygen, having been a model organism for anaerobic metabolism research since the 1980s. Work by Herbert Ultsch and Donald Jackson established that painted turtles can survive complete anoxia (zero dissolved oxygen) for weeks to months at 3°C, far exceeding the tolerance of any other vertebrate studied (Ultsch & Jackson, 1982, cited above; Jackson, 2000, "Living Without Oxygen: Lessons from the Freshwater Turtle," *Comparative Biochemistry and Physiology Part A: Molecular and Integrative Physiology*, 125(3), 299–315). The turtle's strategy involves buffering the lactic acid accumulation of anaerobic glycolysis with calcium carbonate from the shell — the shell literally dissolves marginally to buffer metabolic acidity, another fact that connects the shell biology described in a companion entry to the respiration biology described here.

**Sea turtles and cloacal respiration**: The best-known application of posterior ventilation in sea turtles specifically is not cloacal bursae respiration but cloacal *electrolyte management* (sea turtles excrete excess salt through lacrimal glands rather than through the cloaca), but some sea turtle species are believed to supplement aquatic gas exchange through cutaneous (skin-based) oxygen uptake from seawater during extended diving. Sea turtles can remain submerged for hours during sleep, with oxygen consumption suppressed through physiological mechanisms including selective perfusion of tissues and metabolic rate reduction (Lutz & Musick, 1997, *The Biology of Sea Turtles*, Volumes I and II, CRC Press).

**The *Frozen 2* science communication moment**: The inclusion of the "turtles breathe through their butts" fact in *Frozen 2* represents an interesting science communication case study. The script decision to include this biological fact in a major animated children's film — where it was delivered by a snowman character in a comic context — produced educational impact at a scale that no scientific publication or nature documentary could achieve: hundreds of millions of viewers worldwide. Post-release surveys and casual observation of children's curiosity suggest many viewers looked up the claim, discovering it to be accurate.

This is a positive science communication case: an animated film used a genuine and surprising biological fact for comedic effect, and the fact's inherent interestingness motivated independent verification, which confirmed its accuracy and expanded viewers' understanding of turtle physiology. The *Frozen 2* team presumably consulted biological sources in including the claim, and their decision to embed accurate science in entertainment served a public understanding of science function that formal science communication rarely achieves at comparable scale.

The contrast with the "polar bears yell when they poop" case is instructive: the polar bear claim originated as a joke and was mistaken for fact through repeated exposure; the turtle cloacal respiration claim is an actual fact that appears in a comedic context in *Frozen 2* and is verified by independent research. Both travel through identical social channels (viral sharing, "fun facts" compilation contexts), but their epistemic status is opposite. The challenge for media literacy education is precisely that the distribution channels and presentation formats of accurate and inaccurate "fun facts" are identical.

**The broader Appalachian wetland conservation context**: Bog turtles depend on calcareous fen habitats that are among the rarest wetland types in the eastern United States. These habitats are characterized by groundwater discharge from calcareous (calcium carbonate-rich) bedrock, producing low-nutrient, high-calcium, circumneutral-pH wetland conditions that support a distinctive hydrophilic plant community including sedges (*Carex* spp.), sundews (*Drosera* spp.), grass-of-Parnassus (*Parnassia glauca*), and various orchid species. Many of the plant species associated with bog turtle habitat are themselves rare or state-listed.

The conservation of bog turtle habitat therefore represents a form of umbrella species conservation: protecting the specific hydrological and vegetation conditions that support bog turtles simultaneously protects an entire community of rare plant and invertebrate species associated with calcareous fens. Several state and federal programs specifically target bog turtle habitat for conservation acquisitions and management, reflecting the species' role as both federally protected and an indicator of calcareous wetland health.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check by Madison Dapcevich (May 2021) is one of the more scientifically substantive entries in the natural history fact-check genre. Dapcevich correctly identifies the mechanism (cloacal bursae, dissolved oxygen uptake), provides appropriate institutional sourcing (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service), includes a diagram of the cloacal anatomy from Wikimedia Commons, and accurately describes the bog turtle's size, range, and Threatened status. The piece also correctly identifies the comparative biology note (sea cucumbers and dragonfly nymphs) and traces the claim's viral history through *Frozen 2* and the 2021 Reddit thread.

The fact-check's brevity reflects the genre: it establishes that the claim is true and provides enough mechanism to make the truth intelligible. What it does not provide is the depth of comparative respiratory biology (the convergent evolution framework), the physiological detail of how the bursae achieve gas exchange, the conservation urgency of the bog turtle situation, or the dual-use nature of the bursae for both respiration and sperm storage. These omissions are appropriate for the genre but represent the gap between fact-check-level and full-science-communication-level treatment of the claim.

The verdict of "True" is unambiguous and correct. The claim that some turtles can absorb oxygen through their posterior anatomy is one of the more reliably true "fun facts" in circulation — confirmed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, described in the peer-reviewed literature, and documented through direct physiological measurement of dissolved oxygen uptake from water via cloacal bursae in cold-water-submerged turtles. Its counterintuitive character reflects genuine evolutionary innovation rather than internet mythology, and its persistence in the "fun facts" ecosystem is well-deserved.

The comparison with the polar bear yelling claim, which is false, and the cicada toxicity claim, which is mostly false, illustrates the heterogeneity of "weird animal facts" viral content: some are accurate (turtles, "butt breathing"), some are anatomy-imprecise-but-real (frogs and their cloacal posterior), and some are pure internet mythology (polar bears and their defecation vocalizations). Distinguishing among these requires either domain knowledge or systematic fact-checking — which is precisely the function that Snopes and comparable organizations perform for claims that achieve sufficient viral traction to merit investigation.
