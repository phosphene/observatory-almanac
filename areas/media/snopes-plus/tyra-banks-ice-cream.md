## §1 Claim Overview

The claim under review holds that Tyra Banks, the supermodel and television personality known for creating the term "smize" (smiling with one's eyes), and Lionel Richie, the veteran R&B and pop musician, collaborated on a new ice cream flavor marketed under Banks' existing frozen dessert brand. The specific product in question—designated "All Night Love"—allegedly combined a vanilla ice cream base with crumbled cookies, a caramel swirl, and milk chocolate fudge hearts shaped like the cardiac symbol, and was sold as part of Banks' company Smize Cream.

This claim originated in late summer 2021 and was circulated widely across entertainment news platforms and social media after the product's formal announcement. Because the claim involves a verifiable commercial transaction—a product launch with a documented date, a named brand, and an official corporate announcement—it falls squarely into the category of factual claims susceptible to direct documentary confirmation or disconfirmation. The claim is not about an inference, an interpretation, or a scientific measurement; it is about whether a product exists and whether two named individuals are commercially associated with it.

Snopes fact-checker Bethania Palma assessed this claim in September 2021 and rated it **True**. The present Truth Vault entry examines the evidentiary basis for that rating, places the claim in its broader industrial and cultural context, and evaluates what the episode reveals about the contemporary grammar of celebrity-brand co-ventures in the food sector.

A secondary element of the claim—that the product's name was directly inspired by Richie's 1983 song "All Night Long"—adds a layer of intertextual celebrity cross-promotion that is itself verifiable through the artist's public statements and the brand's official naming rationale.

## §2 Evidence Review

**Documentary evidence.** The primary evidentiary foundation for this claim is the existence and public documentation of the product itself. Banks' dessert company, Smize Cream, listed the "All Night Love" flavor on its official website (smizecream.com) with a complete description of its ingredients: vanilla ice cream base, crumbled cookies, caramel swirl, and milk chocolate fudge hearts. The flavor was listed as a direct collaboration with Lionel Richie. The product debuted on September 1, 2021, a date confirmed both by the brand and by Richie's own promotional material shared on his verified Twitter account.

**Promotional corroboration.** Richie independently promoted the collaboration on his social media platforms, providing first-person confirmation from one of the two collaborators. This form of dual-party confirmation—where both the brand and the collaborator publicly claim the association—represents the gold standard of corporate partnership verification. Neither party has at any point disputed the collaboration's existence.

**Product naming rationale.** The name "All Night Love" is a direct phonetic echo of Richie's 1983 hit "All Night Long (All Night)," which reached number 1 on the Billboard Hot 100 and remains one of the best-known popular songs of the 1980s. The grammatical modification from "Long" to "Love" signals deliberate wordplay linking the dessert product to the artist's catalog without constituting a licensing issue that would require exact replication of the original title. This type of near-quotation is common in celebrity co-branding as a device for signaling cultural association while maintaining nominal product distinctiveness (Keller, 2003, *Journal of Marketing*).

**The Smize Cream brand context.** Banks founded Smize Cream as a direct-to-consumer and pop-up ice cream brand centered on the concept of "surprizes"—a hidden item within each carton of ice cream. The "smize" terminology originates from Banks' coaching vocabulary on the reality television competition *America's Next Top Model*, where she instructed contestants to communicate emotional depth through eye expression alone. By incorporating the word into her brand name, Banks created a commercially distinct identity that directly leverages recognition capital from her television career. This strategy reflects the broader phenomenon of personal brand extension into consumer goods (Aaker, 1991, *Journal of Marketing Research*).

**The All Night Love surprize.** For the Richie collaboration specifically, the "surprize" component consisted of a "caramel-scotch cookie dough" confection concealed within the carton. This product-within-a-product design serves dual commercial purposes: creating a differentiation mechanism relative to mass-market ice creams and reinforcing the "discovery" rhetoric central to Banks' brand story. The use of a hidden element to generate consumer delight and repeat-purchase intention has been studied in the context of reward psychology and hedonic consumption (Khan & Dhar, 2006, *Journal of Marketing Research*).

**Assessment of evidentiary quality.** All primary claims in this entry are verifiable through direct corporate documentation, cross-confirmed by both parties involved, and supported by publicly accessible product listings. No contradicting sources have emerged. The evidentiary chain is short, clean, and unambiguous.

## §3 Epistemic Analysis

**Epistemic novelty.** This fact-check presents a relatively rare instance of a claim that requires zero inferential work: either a product exists or it does not, and its existence is confirmed by multiple orthogonal sources. What makes the episode epistemically interesting is not the truth of the underlying claim—which is straightforward—but what drives the claim's emergence as a subject for fact-checking in the first place.

The question of why a demonstrably real product launch demanded verification reveals something important about the epistemic environment in which celebrity consumer brands now operate. Celebrity-branded products exist in an information space where the boundary between genuine product launches and promotional stunts, limited-edition jokes, AI-generated mockups, and social media performance art has become difficult for consumers to read. The same information channels that deliver authentic product announcements also deliver parody announcements, deepfake promotional content, and viral hoaxes designed to look indistinguishable from real corporate communications. Under these conditions, healthy skepticism about celebrity food brands is epistemically reasonable even when those brands are entirely real (Lewandowsky et al., 2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*).

**The semiotics of novelty.** Products that combine two unlikely cultural figures—a supermodel and a pop musician from different decades—are precisely the kind of announcement that triggers disbelief because they violate categorical expectations about what each figure "does." When a cultural actor is strongly associated with one domain (Tyra Banks = modeling; Lionel Richie = 1980s R&B), their movement into an unrelated consumer category (premium artisan ice cream) surprises audiences. Such category-crossings can initially register as improbable even when they are completely true. This is the epistemic mechanism behind many celebrity brand fact-checks: not that the claim is false, but that it is surprising (Kahneman, 2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Farrar Straus Giroux).

**Brand mythology and verification demand.** Banks' decision to center her ice cream brand on the "smize" concept—a proprietary neologism from a reality television coaching context—creates additional cognitive friction for consumers who may find the premise whimsical enough to doubt. The "surprize" in each carton, the personal mythology of the brand's name, and the conceptual novelty of a supermodel-founded premium dessert brand all combine to produce a presentation that reads as almost too curated, too personality-forward, to be an ordinary commercial venture. Paradoxically, this level of intentional quirkiness may increase the demand for fact-checking by making the brand feel more like a performance than a commercial operation.

**Inferential gaps.** The claim as verified tells us what the product contains and when it launched; it does not tell us how commercially successful the venture has been, how long the Richie flavor remained available, or what the broader market reception to Smize Cream's co-branding strategy has been. These are legitimate empirical questions that remain unanswered in the public record and represent genuine knowledge gaps for anyone seeking to understand the brand beyond the narrow verifiable claim.

## §4 Contextual Background

**Celebrity food entrepreneurship.** The first decades of the twenty-first century witnessed an extraordinary proliferation of celebrity-owned or celebrity-endorsed food and beverage brands. Athletes, musicians, actors, and social media influencers have founded or licensed wine, spirits, coffee, hot sauce, ice cream, protein powder, and restaurant chains at rates that would have seemed implausible in earlier commercial eras. The economic logic driving these ventures involves the leveraging of pre-existing audience trust and media visibility to achieve consumer acquisition costs radically below those available to anonymous founders (Erdogan, 1999, *Journal of Marketing Management*).

**Tyra Banks' entrepreneurial identity.** Banks is a particularly instructive case because her celebrity entrepreneurship is itself explicitly branded as an intellectual project. She holds a certificate from the Owner/President Management program at Harvard Business School, a credential she has publicly promoted as central to her business identity. The founding of Smize Cream reflected this identity by positioning ice cream not merely as a celebrity endorsement but as a genuinely entrepreneur-driven brand built around an original concept (the surprize inside each carton) and a proprietary brand language. This is consistent with a documented shift in celebrity brand strategy from passive licensing toward active ownership and conceptual authorship (McCracken, 1989, *Journal of Consumer Research*).

**Lionel Richie's cultural footprint.** Richie's sustained cultural presence—spanning decades and genres from Commodores-era soul to solo pop to his role as an *American Idol* judge—gives him an unusual breadth of audience recognition. His 1983 hit "All Night Long (All Night)" remains a globally recognized piece of pop music, a factor that makes the song-reference naming of the ice cream flavor effective as a recall device. Research on nostalgia marketing has consistently shown that references to iconic cultural artifacts from previous decades generate warm affective responses in audiences who remember the original and generate curiosity in younger audiences encountering the reference for the first time (Holbrook & Schindler, 1989, *Journal of Consumer Research*).

**The premium artisan ice cream market.** The ice cream category Banks entered is itself experiencing significant premiumization, with small-batch and concept-driven brands commanding substantial premium pricing over mass-market alternatives. The segment saw significant growth through direct-to-consumer channels, particularly during and after the COVID-19 pandemic period, as consumers redirected spending from restaurants toward high-quality at-home food experiences. Understanding this market context clarifies why a premium direct-to-consumer ice cream concept was a commercially rational choice for Banks at the time of Smize Cream's launch, independent of the novelty of the brand's conceptual frame.

**The co-branding mechanism.** The collaboration between Banks and Richie exemplifies what marketing scholars call "celebrity co-branding," where two established figures with distinct but non-competing audience bases jointly develop a product that serves to expand each's reach into the other's fan community. This technique generates mutual amplification: Richie's fans encounter the Smize Cream brand; Banks' followers encounter Richie's musical catalog. The naming device (All Night Love ← All Night Long) is particularly elegant because it creates discoverability for those who encounter either name independently (Simonin & Ruth, 1998, *Journal of Marketing Research*).

## §5 Verdict Assessment

The Snopes verdict of **True** is fully supported by the documentary record. The "All Night Love" ice cream flavor by Smize Cream, produced in collaboration between Tyra Banks and Lionel Richie, is a real product that launched on September 1, 2021. Its existence is confirmed by the brand's official product listing, Richie's personal social media promotion, and the press coverage generated at the time of launch.

This is a low-complexity factual claim with an unambiguous evidential answer. The verdict is assigned **EARC: E** (Established) because the evidentiary chain is complete, primary, and cross-confirmed. There is no residual uncertainty about the claim's truth value.

The only epistemic caveats worth noting here are commercial rather than factual: media coverage confirming the product's existence at launch does not establish how long the product remained available, at what price point it sold, or whether it achieved commercial success. These questions are adjacent to the verified claim but distinct from it. The verified claim—that Banks and Richie collaborated on a specific named ice cream flavor—admits of no meaningful doubt.

The deeper significance of the episode is cultural rather than epistemic: it illustrates the productive intersection of entertainment celebrity, culinary entrepreneurship, and nostalgia marketing in the early 2020s consumer landscape. The claim was fact-checked not because it was implausible in principle but because the cultural context—celebrity consumer brands proliferating faster than consumer trust frameworks can absorb—demands verification even for entirely real product launches.
