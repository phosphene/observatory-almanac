# Is "Unobtainium" a Real Element in the Periodic Table?

*Series: The Truth Vault · Verdict: FALSE*

---

## 1. The Claim

A persistent online assertion holds that "unobtainium" is a genuine chemical element — a real entry, or at least a plausible candidate for entry, in the periodic table of elements maintained by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC). Variants of the claim range from the straightforward ("unobtainium is element 120" or "unobtainium is the rarest element on the periodic table") to subtler formulations that conflate real-world materials science with the fictional substance popularised by blockbuster cinema.

The claim was investigated by Snopes fact-checker Alex Kasprak and published on December 17, 2020 (Kasprak, 2020, Snopes). The verdict was unambiguous: **FALSE**. No element named unobtainium exists, has existed, or is pending recognition in any chemical nomenclature system. The word is a science fiction trope — nothing more and nothing less.

But the very fact that the question arises frequently enough to require formal debunking tells a richer story. This is not merely a case of someone misreading a periodic table poster. It is a case study in how fictional naming conventions can migrate from entertainment into perceived scientific reality, how the suffix "-ium" carries implicit ontological authority, and how the cultural appetite for miraculous materials creates fertile ground for false belief. Understanding why people believe unobtainium is real is at least as important as confirming that it is not.

---

## 2. What the Record Actually Shows

### 2.1 No Such Element Exists

The periodic table, as of 2026, contains 118 confirmed elements. Each has been either observed in nature or synthesised in laboratory conditions, subjected to rigorous verification protocols, and given a permanent name by IUPAC — the sole international authority on chemical nomenclature. "Unobtainium" does not appear on this list. It has never appeared on this list. No research group has ever proposed it as a candidate element, temporary or otherwise.

The term's origins are not scientific but colloquial. Engineers and aerospace researchers in the 1950s began using "unobtainium" informally to describe any material that would be ideal for a particular application but which did not yet exist or could not be procured (Kasprak, 2020, Snopes). It was, from the start, a joke — a self-aware acknowledgement of the gap between theoretical need and practical availability. The "-ium" suffix was deliberately chosen to give the placeholder a mock-scientific ring, parodying the Latinate naming convention used for metallic elements since the eighteenth century.

### 2.2 Science Fiction Popularisation

While the engineering community treated unobtainium as a knowing in-joke, science fiction and Hollywood cinema gave the word a second, far more visible life. Two films in particular cemented its place in popular culture and, crucially, created the conditions for the claim under investigation.

**The Core (2003).** In Jon Amiel's geophysical disaster film, a team of scientists must drill to the Earth's core to restart its rotation. Their vessel is constructed from "unobtainium," which is described in the film as a material that becomes stronger under extreme heat and pressure — the exact inverse of how most real materials behave. The film needed a hand-wave to explain how any vessel could survive temperatures exceeding 5,000°C and pressures in the millions of atmospheres, and rather than invent a plausible-sounding compound, the screenwriters leaned into the absurdity of the premise by using the engineers' old placeholder.

In reality, the most heat-resistant substance known to science is hafnium carbide (HfC), with a melting point of approximately 3,890°C. Research by a team at Imperial College London confirmed this figure through laser heating experiments (Cedillos-Barraza et al., 2016, *Scientific Reports*, vol. 6, article 37962). Even hafnium carbide, impressive as it is, falls far short of the temperatures at the Earth's inner core, which have been measured at approximately 6,000°C (Anzellini et al., 2013, *Science*, vol. 340, issue 6131, p. 464). No known material — let alone a fictional one — can maintain structural integrity at those temperatures and pressures.

**Avatar (2009).** James Cameron's record-breaking science fiction epic placed unobtainium at the centre of its narrative. In the film, unobtainium is a room-temperature superconductor mined from the moon Pandora, so valuable that humanity is willing to wage war against an indigenous population to extract it. The material is depicted as levitating through the Meissner effect without refrigeration — a property that, if real, would transform energy generation, transportation, and computing overnight.

In reality, superconductivity is a quantum mechanical phenomenon that, for most of its history, has required temperatures far below zero. The first superconductors, discovered by Heike Kamerlingh Onnes in 1911, only exhibited their properties near absolute zero (Onnes, 1911, *Communications from the Physical Laboratory of the University of Leiden*). The "high-temperature" superconductors discovered in the 1980s by Bednorz and Müller (1986, *Zeitschrift für Physik B*, vol. 64, pp. 189–193) still required cryogenic cooling, typically with liquid nitrogen at −196°C. As of 2020, the closest approach to room-temperature superconductivity had been achieved in hydrogen sulphide systems, but only under pressures of approximately 267 gigapascals — roughly comparable to the pressure at the Earth's core (Snider et al., 2020, *Nature*, vol. 586, pp. 373–377). A room-temperature superconductor at ambient pressure remains, in the most precise sense, unobtainable.

### 2.3 The Ununoctium Confusion

Part of the persistence of the unobtainium claim may stem from a genuine visual and phonetic similarity with real temporary element names. "Ununoctium" was the provisional systematic name for element 118, assigned under IUPAC's naming protocol for newly discovered elements. The element was first synthesised — a handful of atoms at a time — by a team of Russian and American scientists at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna, Russia, in 2002 (Oganessian et al., 2006, *Physical Review C*, vol. 74, article 044602). In 2015, IUPAC gave element 118 its permanent name: oganesson (Og), honouring nuclear physicist Yuri Oganessian for his contributions to the synthesis of superheavy elements (IUPAC, 2016).

The resemblance between "unobtainium" and "ununoctium" is superficial — both are polysyllabic, both end in "-ium," and both sound vaguely exotic. But the similarity is enough to seed confusion, particularly for people encountering either term casually online. One is a real, if extraordinarily unstable and short-lived, element. The other is a fiction. The gap between them is the gap between science and storytelling.

## 3. The Epistemic Mechanism: How Fiction Acquires the Weight of Fact

This section addresses the deeper question: why would anyone believe unobtainium is real? The answer lies not in individual gullibility but in a set of well-documented cognitive and linguistic mechanisms that blur the boundary between scientific nomenclature and fictional invention.

### 3.1 The "-ium" Suffix and the Periodic Table Schema

Human cognition relies heavily on pattern recognition and schema activation. A schema, in cognitive psychology, is a mental framework that organises and interprets information based on prior experience (Bartlett, 1932, *Remembering: A Study in Experimental and Social Psychology*, Cambridge University Press). The periodic table of elements represents one of the most deeply ingrained scientific schemas in public education. Even people with no formal chemistry training beyond secondary school can recognise the structure: a grid of boxes, each containing a symbol and a name, the vast majority of which end in "-ium."

This is the crucial leverage point. The suffix "-ium" is not merely a common word ending; it is, for the lay public, a *signifier of elemental reality*. Helium, lithium, sodium, uranium, plutonium — the repeated pattern establishes a strong associative link between the "-ium" suffix and "genuine chemical substance." When a word like "unobtainium" is encountered, the suffix automatically activates the periodic table schema. The listener's cognitive system flags the word as *belonging to the category of elements* before conscious evaluation has a chance to intervene. This is an instance of what psychologists call the "representativeness heuristic" (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974, *Science*, vol. 185, pp. 1124–1131) — judging category membership based on superficial resemblance to a prototype.

The effect is compounded by the fact that many real element names sound strange to non-specialists. Praseodymium, ytterbium, einsteinium — these are genuine entries on the periodic table that, to the uninitiated, sound no more or less plausible than "unobtainium." When the entire category is populated with unfamiliar, Latinate terms, the bar for distinguishing real entries from fictional ones rises considerably.

### 3.2 The Cinematic Realism Effect

Films like *Avatar* and *The Core* do not merely mention unobtainium in passing. They embed the substance within detailed fictional contexts that mimic the texture of real scientific discourse. In *Avatar*, characters discuss unobtainium's superconducting properties, its market value, and its geological distribution on Pandora. In *The Core*, characters explain — in pseudo-scientific but confidently delivered dialogue — how the material behaves under extreme conditions. These films supply a narrative environment that makes the fictional substance feel *researched*, even *documented*.

Research in cognitive science has demonstrated that vivid, detailed narratives increase the perceived truth of embedded claims, a phenomenon known as the "illusory truth effect" (Hasher, Goldstein, and Toppino, 1977, *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, vol. 16, pp. 107–112). When people encounter a claim multiple times across different sources — a film, a Wikipedia summary, a social media post — each repetition increases its perceived credibility, regardless of whether the original source was fictional. The migration path from science fiction screenplay to apparent scientific fact is shorter than most people assume.

### 3.3 The Cultural Desire for Impossible Materials

There is a deeper cultural current at work. Humanity has always yearned for miraculous substances — the philosopher's stone, adamantium, vibranium, mithril. These materials represent a persistent archetype: the substance that solves the unsolvable problem, that breaks the rules of physics as we understand them. This desire is not irrational; it is a reflection of genuine material constraints that limit technological progress. We *need* better materials. We need conductors that work at room temperature, shielding that withstands core temperatures, structures that strengthen under stress. The gap between what we need and what we have is the precise space that "unobtainium" was coined to describe.

When real scientific research makes progress toward what was previously impossible — as when Snider et al. (2020, *Nature*) demonstrated near-room-temperature superconductivity, or when Cedillos-Barraza et al. (2016, *Scientific Reports*) pushed the boundaries of heat-resistant ceramics — the boundary between "fictional wish" and "emerging reality" genuinely narrows. This narrowing makes it easier to believe that unobtainium might have crossed the line. The claim thrives in the ambiguity between "we can't do this yet" and "we can't do this ever," because that ambiguity is real and is constantly shifting.

### 3.4 The Naming Authority Problem

Finally, there is the question of who gets to name things. The periodic table is maintained by IUPAC, a body most non-chemists have never heard of. Element naming is governed by a formal process involving discovery verification, priority assessment, and committee deliberation (Koppenol et al., 2016, *Pure and Applied Chemistry*, vol. 88, pp. 401–405). But this process is invisible to the general public. Most people have no idea how new elements are named, how many elements have been added in their lifetime, or what the criteria for inclusion are. In the absence of knowledge about the naming process, any sufficiently scientific-sounding name can seem as legitimate as any other.

## 4. The Real Science of Extreme Materials

If unobtainium is fictional, what does real materials science actually offer at the extremes? The answer is both humbling and remarkable.

### 4.1 Hafnium Carbide: The Most Heat-Resistant Known Substance

Hafnium carbide (HfC) holds the record for the highest melting point of any known compound. The figure most commonly cited is approximately 3,890°C, though some earlier measurements placed it slightly higher or lower depending on methodology. The definitive modern measurement came from Cedillos-Barraza et al. at Imperial College London, who used laser heating techniques to precisely determine the melting transitions of tantalum carbide, hafnium carbide, and their mixed compositions (Cedillos-Barraza et al., 2016, *Scientific Reports*, vol. 6, article 37962). The mixed compound Ta₄HfC₅ was found to have a melting point potentially exceeding that of either pure carbide, approaching 4,000°C.

These materials are of enormous interest for aerospace applications — particularly for thermal protection systems on re-entry vehicles and hypersonic aircraft. But even at nearly 4,000°C, hafnium carbide falls 2,000 degrees short of the temperature at the Earth's inner core. The fictional "unobtainium" from *The Core* would need to withstand conditions that no real material approaches.

### 4.2 Superconductors: The Long Road to Room Temperature

Superconductivity — the complete absence of electrical resistance — was first observed in mercury cooled to 4.2 Kelvin (−269°C) by Onnes in 1911. For decades, the phenomenon was confined to temperatures achievable only with liquid helium. The 1986 discovery of cuprate superconductors by Bednorz and Müller (1986, *Zeitschrift für Physik B*) raised operating temperatures to the liquid nitrogen range (around 77 K, or −196°C), a landmark achievement that earned a Nobel Prize and opened new practical applications.

The search for room-temperature superconductivity has been one of the great quests of condensed matter physics. In 2020, Snider et al. reported superconductivity in a carbonaceous sulphur hydride system at approximately 15°C — room temperature — but under a pressure of 267 gigapascals (Snider et al., 2020, *Nature*, vol. 586, pp. 373–377). This pressure is comparable to conditions deep within the Earth's interior, rendering the achievement a scientific milestone but not yet a practical one. (The Snider et al. result was later subject to scrutiny and retraction by *Nature* in 2022 due to data handling concerns, further illustrating the contested frontier of this research.)

Subsequent work has continued to push boundaries. Drozdov et al. (2019, *Nature*, vol. 569, pp. 528–531) demonstrated superconductivity in lanthanum hydride at −23°C under approximately 170 GPa — still extreme pressure, but a lower temperature than previous hydride results. The trajectory is encouraging, but the dream of *Avatar*'s ambient-pressure, room-temperature superconductor remains distant.

### 4.3 The Earth's Core: A Real Extreme

The conditions at the Earth's centre provide a useful reality check for fictional materials. Seismic and experimental evidence indicates that the inner core is a solid iron-nickel alloy at approximately 6,000°C and pressures exceeding 330 GPa (Anzellini et al., 2013, *Science*, vol. 340, issue 6131, p. 464). These conditions are achieved in laboratories only transiently, using diamond anvil cells or shock compression experiments, and never in volumes larger than a few cubic millimetres.

No vessel has ever been built, or is likely to be built with foreseeable technology, that could physically travel to the Earth's core. The deepest borehole ever drilled — the Kola Superdeep Borehole in Russia — reached only 12.2 kilometres, less than 0.2% of the distance to the centre, before being halted by temperatures of approximately 180°C that exceeded the drill equipment's operational limits (Kozlovsky, 1987, *The Superdeep Well of the Kola Peninsula*, Springer-Verlag). The premise of *The Core* requires not a better material but a fundamentally different physics.

### 4.4 Oganesson: The Real Element That Sounds Fictional

Element 118, oganesson (Og), occupies the position at the bottom-right corner of the periodic table that might otherwise be filled by a substance of imagination. It was first synthesised in 2002 at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia, through the bombardment of californium-249 targets with calcium-48 ions (Oganessian et al., 2006, *Physical Review C*, vol. 74, article 044602). Only a few atoms have ever been produced, and each existed for less than a millisecond before decaying.

Oganesson is predicted, based on relativistic quantum mechanical calculations, to have highly unusual properties. Unlike the other noble gases in its column, it may not behave as a gas at standard conditions; some theoretical models suggest it could be a solid with semi-metallic properties (Jerabek et al., 2018, *Physical Review Letters*, vol. 120, article 053001). Its electron shell structure is expected to be significantly disrupted by relativistic effects, making it one of the most extreme elements in terms of the gap between its periodic table position and its likely physical behaviour.

Oganesson is, in some ways, stranger than fiction — a real element whose properties may defy the patterns that the periodic table was built to encode. Its existence demonstrates that the boundary of real chemistry is already exotic enough without needing an imaginary addition.

## 5. From Placeholder to Pop Culture: The Linguistic Life of Unobtainium

### 5.1 Engineering Origins

The word "unobtainium" first circulated in American aerospace engineering circles in the 1950s. It was coined — independently, by multiple communities, as is common with informal jargon — to describe any material whose properties were desired for a design but which did not exist or could not be obtained within budget and timeline constraints (Kasprak, 2020, Snopes). The word was a compound of "unobtainable" and the suffix "-ium," transparently humorous in its construction. It signalled professional resignation: *we know what we need, and we know we can't have it*.

The term served a genuine communicative function. In early-stage design, engineers often need to reason about ideal materials before worrying about procurement. Saying "assume we use unobtainium for the thermal shield" was a shorthand for "assume the thermal shield problem is solved" — a way of isolating one design challenge from another. It was never intended to be taken literally.

### 5.2 The Pop Culture Inflection Point

The term crossed from specialist jargon to popular awareness primarily through cinema. While earlier science fiction literature had used similar coinages, it was *The Core* in 2003 and especially *Avatar* in 2009 that brought "unobtainium" to a global audience numbering in the hundreds of millions. *Avatar* grossed over $2.9 billion worldwide and became a cultural touchstone; its use of the term was so prominent that it appeared in countless reviews, analyses, and internet discussions.

This massive exposure changed the word's epistemic status. In engineering, "unobtainium" was universally understood as a joke. In cinema, it was presented with a straight face — embedded in worldbuilding that was internally consistent and visually persuasive. For viewers unfamiliar with the engineering tradition, the word arrived without its original ironic framing. It sounded like a real substance because it was presented as one, in a medium that offered no cues for scepticism.

### 5.3 Internet Amplification and Decontextualisation

The internet further accelerated the decontextualisation of the term. Social media posts, memes, and casual references stripped away whatever remaining context might have flagged the word as fictional. A screenshot of a periodic table with "unobtainium" photoshopped into an empty cell could circulate as readily as any other image. Wiki-style knowledge aggregation sites sometimes mentioned unobtainium alongside real elements without adequate framing. The result was a term that had lost its original irony and gained a patina of factuality through sheer repetition and context collapse.

This process — the stripping of epistemic markers through platform-mediated redistribution — is not unique to unobtainium. It is a general feature of how misinformation propagates in digital environments. A claim's journey from clearly fictional context to ambiguous status to apparent fact requires nothing more than successive removal of framing cues, which each act of sharing and reposting naturally accomplishes (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017, *Information Disorder: Toward an Interdisciplinary Framework for Research and Policy Making*, Council of Europe).

## 6. Verdict and Significance

### 6.1 The Verdict

The claim that "unobtainium" is a real chemical element or a valid entry in the periodic table is **FALSE**.

No element by this name has ever been discovered, synthesised, proposed, or recognised by IUPAC or any scientific body. The word is a mid-twentieth-century engineering joke repurposed by Hollywood screenwriters, which subsequently migrated through popular culture into a space where some people take it as genuine. The phonetic similarity to real temporary element names like "ununoctium" (now oganesson) may contribute to confusion but does not confer legitimacy.

### 6.2 What This Case Reveals

The unobtainium claim is, in isolation, trivial. Nobody is harmed by briefly believing that a fictional element is real. But the *mechanism* by which the belief propagates is anything but trivial. It illustrates several principles of broader significance:

**Naming conventions carry epistemic authority.** The "-ium" suffix is not neutral. It triggers categorical assumptions about elemental reality that operate below conscious awareness. This has implications far beyond unobtainium — it suggests that *any* sufficiently well-constructed neologism, if it conforms to the naming patterns of a legitimate domain, can borrow that domain's credibility. The suffix does the work of persuasion that evidence would otherwise need to do.

**Fiction-to-fact migration is a feature, not a bug, of media ecosystems.** The path from *Avatar*'s worldbuilding to internet claims about the periodic table was not engineered by any bad actor. It emerged naturally from the way information loses context as it moves across platforms and formats. The original source was clearly fictional; the downstream instances were not clearly anything.

**Real science makes fiction more plausible.** The genuine advances in superconductor research and extreme materials science do not validate the unobtainium claim, but they create an environment in which the claim feels less absurd than it might otherwise. When real headlines announce near-room-temperature superconductivity, the distance between "we almost have this" and "we already have this, and it's called unobtainium" shrinks in the public imagination, even though the actual scientific gap remains enormous.

**Verification is freely available but rarely sought.** Any search engine query for "unobtainium periodic table" immediately returns debunking results. IUPAC's official element list is publicly accessible. The information needed to reject the claim is trivially easy to obtain. But the claim persists because most people do not verify claims that *feel* plausible — and the "-ium" suffix, the cinematic context, and the real-science adjacency all conspire to make this particular claim feel plausible enough not to check.

### 6.3 Closing Note

Unobtainium was always meant to name the gap between ambition and reality. That gap is real, and materials scientists work within it every day — pushing melting points higher, coaxing superconductivity to warmer temperatures, synthesising atoms that exist for fractions of a second. The periodic table continues to grow, and future elements will be given names that sound no less exotic than oganesson or nihonium. But unobtainium will not be among them. Its home is in the language of aspiration, not the language of nature — and understanding the difference is the entire point of scientific literacy.
