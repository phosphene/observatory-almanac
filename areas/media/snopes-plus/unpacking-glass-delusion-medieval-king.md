# A Medieval King Believed He Was Made of Glass and Could Shatter

## The Claim

Among the stranger facts that circulate as historical anecdotes on the internet is the claim that a medieval king — typically identified as Charles VI of France — genuinely believed his body was made of glass and lived in constant fear of accidentally shattering. The story is usually presented with a mixture of pity and dark wonder: here was a monarch ruling one of the most powerful kingdoms in Europe while sincerely convinced he was as fragile as a wine goblet. He reportedly had iron rods sewn into his clothing for reinforcement, forbade servants and courtiers from touching him, and refused to allow physical contact for fear of breaking.

The claim appears in popular history articles, in listicles about "strange facts," and in introductory discussions of psychiatric history. It circulates as a curiosity — a reminder that even the powerful are not immune to the peculiarities of the human mind — and as an illustration of how dramatically different historical conceptions of mental illness were from modern diagnostic frameworks. Sometimes it travels with additional details: that the condition had a name, "the glass delusion"; that it appeared in other historical figures as well; that it has vanished almost entirely from modern psychiatric literature; and that no one is entirely sure why.

## What's Actually True

The claim is historically documented, though it must be situated carefully within the context of medieval chronicles, which operated under different evidentiary standards than modern medical records.

Charles VI of France, known by both the epithets "Charles the Beloved" (Charles le Bien-Aimé) — used before his illness became manifest — and "Charles the Mad" (Charles le Fou) — used after — reigned from 1380 until his death in 1422. He began exhibiting severe psychiatric symptoms in 1392, when at the age of twenty-three he suffered what historians describe as a psychotic break while on a military campaign in Brittany. Accounts from contemporary chroniclers describe him attacking his own soldiers, killing four of them before being restrained, and falling into a catatonic-like state from which he took some time to recover.

From that point forward, Charles VI suffered recurring episodes of psychosis for the remaining thirty years of his life. The intervals of lucidity grew shorter over time. Contemporary chronicles describe him at various points failing to recognise his wife and children, believing himself to be Saint George, denying that he was the king of France, and at other times — the episode for which he is most famous in retrospect — believing that his body was made entirely of glass and that he would shatter on contact. To prevent this, he reportedly had iron or steel reinforcements inserted into his garments, refused to be touched, and moved with extreme caution. Some accounts describe him wearing a specially constructed padded cushion or harness beneath his robes.

The historical sources for this specific delusion are predominantly chronicles and court documents from the early fifteenth century. The most detailed accounts were compiled by the Monk of Saint-Denis (Michel Pintoin), whose *Chronique du Religieux de Saint-Denis* provides the most sustained contemporary account of Charles VI's reign and illness. Froissart's *Chronicles* and later synthesising works by historians also describe the king's condition in some detail, though with varying specificity about the glass delusion specifically.

It is worth noting the evidentiary limitations. Medieval chronicle writers were not physicians maintaining case notes — they were court observers with literary and sometimes political agendas, interpreting what they witnessed through frameworks very different from modern psychiatry. The "glass delusion" attribution to Charles VI appears more prominently in later historical accounts and may have been somewhat embellished or consolidated from a range of symptoms into a memorable anecdote. What the sources agree on is serious, recurrent psychosis; the precise phenomenology of any single delusion is harder to verify with modern-standard confidence.

What is much better documented, however, is that the glass delusion as a psychiatric phenomenon was widely recognised in late medieval and early modern Europe — not as a curiosity unique to one king, but as a named category of mental disturbance affecting educated Europeans across the 15th, 16th, and 17th centuries. This is where the claim becomes genuinely fascinating.

The Dutch polymath Caspar Barlaeus (1584–1648) suffered from it. A noble described in the casebooks of Alfonso Ponce de Santa Cruz — physician to Philip II of Spain — believed he was a glass vessel. Nicole du Plessis, a relative of Cardinal Richelieu, believed her buttocks were made of glass and refused to sit without cushioning for fear of shattering. Robert Burton discusses glass delusion cases in *The Anatomy of Melancholy* (1621) as examples of the distortions of melancholic imagination. Constantijn Huygens wrote his 1622 poem "Costly Folly" (*Dure Deftigheid*) partly about Barlaeus's glass delusion. Most remarkably, Miguel de Cervantes made a glass scholar the central figure of his 1613 novella *El licenciado Vidriera* (The Glass Graduate), suggesting the condition was sufficiently familiar to sixteenth-century readers to anchor a full literary narrative.

The condition was not merely anecdotal gossip — it was a recognised diagnostic category within humoral medicine, typically classified as a form of melancholy. The sufferer's conviction that they were made of glass was understood as an extreme manifestation of the imaginative faculty overpowering reason, a failure of the mind's ability to correctly perceive the nature of the body it inhabited. Treatments described in period medical texts include psychological interventions designed to "cure" the patient of the delusion through theatrical shock or persuasion — persuading the sufferer that they had survived an impact and not shattered — rather than the physical restraints of later psychiatric practice.

Modern psychiatric historians, notably Edward Shorter and the contributors to the *History of Psychiatry* journal, have studied why the glass delusion appears so concentrated in this period and then virtually disappears from clinical literature by the 19th century. One influential hypothesis is that delusions are partly culturally scaffolded: the specific shape a psychotic delusion takes depends on the symbolic materials available in the patient's cultural environment. Glass in late medieval and early modern Europe was a relatively rare, precious, and symbolically complex material — associated with fragility, transparency, and a kind of supernatural clarity. As glass became a mass-produced commodity in the 19th century, its symbolic weight diminished, and with it the cultural infrastructure that had been generating glass delusions. The underlying psychotic psychopathology presumably continued to occur, but it now expressed itself through different materials and metaphors.

## Why People Believe This

The story travels easily because it combines genuine historical strangeness with emotional accessibility. Hearing that a king — a person of supreme authority, presumably surrounded by the most sophisticated culture of his era — harboured a belief that strikes us as magical and fragile is somehow both deeply human and deeply alien. It creates intellectual vertigo: the simultaneous recognition that this was a real person with real power and real suffering, and the puzzle of why anyone would believe such a thing.

The anecdote also meshes with a prevailing popular notion that the medieval period was a time of extreme credulity and magical thinking, when the boundaries between rational and irrational were not clearly drawn. Some versions of the story use it to illustrate this point explicitly: "people believed all sorts of things back then." This framing is somewhat misleading — Charles VI's courtiers and physicians did not share his delusion; they recognised it as illness — but it makes the story more assimilable to contemporary assumptions about historical backwardness.

The detail of the iron rods sewn into clothing is particularly sticky. It is concrete, visual, and easily imagined. A king dressing himself every day in iron-reinforced robes to avoid being shattered — this is the kind of specific material detail that makes a historical anecdote feel real and that lodges in memory. Whether the detail is precisely accurate, embellished, or symbolically consolidated from a range of protective measures Charles actually took is hard to establish, but for narrative purposes it does the work of making the abstract claim (he believed he was glass) tangible and credible.

There is also genuine pathos in the story. Charles VI was not merely a curiosity — he was a person who suffered profoundly for three decades, whose illness created succession crises, whose wife and children navigated life with a monarch who sometimes did not recognise them, and who watched pieces of his kingdom break apart during some of the worst years of the Hundred Years' War partly because his incapacity left a power vacuum. The glass delusion is the memorable fragment of a much larger human tragedy.

## Verdict

**True, with the usual caveats of medieval historical documentation.** The claim rests on genuine contemporary chronicle sources that describe severe psychosis in Charles VI, including episodes consistent with the glass delusion. The glass delusion was a recognised psychiatric phenomenon in late medieval and early modern Europe, documented across multiple sources and subjects. Charles VI's case is the most famous but not the only documented instance. Where the claim should be qualified: the specificity of any single delusion in historical chronicles is difficult to verify with the evidentiary standards of modern psychiatry, and some details have likely been consolidated or embellished in transmission. The phenomenon as a category is real and well-documented; the precise phenomenology of Charles's specific episodes is somewhat less certain. Assign this as *true with historical source limitations acknowledged*.

## The Wider Picture

The history of the glass delusion opens into some of the most important questions in the history of psychiatry: is mental illness universal and neurologically determined, with only its cultural expression varying across time, or are certain mental illnesses genuinely culture-specific and time-bound, arising only when particular symbolic and social conditions exist?

This debate has been formalised in the concept of "culture-bound syndromes" — patterns of distress or abnormal behaviour that appear exclusively or predominantly in specific cultural contexts. The American Psychiatric Association's DSM-5 uses the term "cultural concepts of distress" to describe syndromes whose expression is shaped by cultural factors. The glass delusion is one of the cleaner historical examples: a clearly psychotic phenomenon (the person is experiencing a profound departure from reality about the nature of their own body) that nonetheless requires specific cultural materials — precious, fragile, symbolically loaded glass — to take its particular form.

The psychiatrist Andy Lameijn and historian of psychiatry Edward Shorter have argued that the disappearance of the glass delusion from clinical records is not evidence that psychosis declined or that humans became differently constituted, but that industrialisation changed the symbolic landscape that delusions draw from. Twentieth-century psychotics were more likely to believe they were controlled by radio waves or electronic surveillance than that they were made of precious materials. The medium of the delusion reflects the symbolic power hierarchies of its era. This has implications for how we interpret the psychiatric literature of any period — including our own.

Charles VI's case also invites reflection on the relationship between mental illness and political power. The history of his reign from 1392 to 1422 illustrates what happens when a monarch's capacity to function varies unpredictably: periods of competent rule interrupted by episodes of complete incapacity, with different factions at court manoeuvring to fill the vacuum during the bad episodes. His brother Louis of Orléans and his uncles, including the Duke of Burgundy, competed for regency powers during Charles's incapacities. The civil war between the Armagnacs and Burgundians, which fatally weakened France during the Hundred Years' War and arguably enabled Henry V of England's victories at Agincourt and the subsequent Treaty of Troyes, was in part a product of the governance vacuum created by the king's mental illness. Mental illness at the summit of power has consequences that ripple outward across entire societies.

The literary aftermath is also worth noting. The glass delusion inspired not just Cervantes' novella but a long tradition of commentary on the fragility of the self and the relationship between glass's optical properties and transparent, truthful perception. Glass in medieval and early modern symbolism was associated with the Virgin Mary (light passing through glass without breaking it as a symbol of the Incarnation), with the clarity of divine vision, and with mirrors as instruments of self-knowledge. A person who believed themselves to be made of glass was not merely expressing fear of fragility — in the symbolic economy of the period, they were perhaps expressing something about the nature of selfhood as transparent, penetrable, infinitely delicate. The psychiatric historians who see the glass delusion as culturally scaffolded find this symbolic richness significant.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not addressed this claim directly. The claim is well-supported enough and obscure enough that it typically circulates as a positive curiosity — "strange but true history" — rather than as a contested assertion requiring debunking. The fact-checking challenge is less "is this true?" than "what exactly is true, and what context is necessary to understand it properly?"

The principal danger in the popular circulation of this story is not that it is false but that it tends to be shorn of context in ways that trivialise both the history and the psychiatric science. A king who "believed he was made of glass" sounds like a quirk, a party fact, a medieval oddity. The fuller picture — decades of serious psychotic illness, a recognised European-wide phenomenon with a substantial medical and literary literature, questions about cultural scaffolding, a political and dynastic catastrophe — is considerably more substantial.

When mental health and history journalists engage with this material responsibly, they typically situate the glass delusion within the broader history of humoral medicine, describe the range of documented cases rather than focusing only on Charles VI, and note the scholarly debate about cultural scaffolding of delusions. The best accounts avoid both the "medieval people were simply credulous" framing that dehistoricises psychiatric experience and the "Charles VI was uniquely bizarre" framing that decontextualises his illness. The epistemological value of the claim is not merely the strangeness of the anecdote but what it reveals about the relationship between culture, symbol, and the shape of psychotic experience — a relationship that has not become less relevant simply because glass has become cheap.
