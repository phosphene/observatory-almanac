## The Claim

The claim circulating on social media — and debated in comment threads, home-economics forums, and personal-finance communities since at least 2010 — is that unplugging home appliances and electronics when they are not actively in use saves electricity. Versions of the claim vary in amplitude. Some assert that the savings are trivial and not worth the inconvenience. Others claim that standby power "steals" as much as ten percent of a household's electricity budget. The underlying factual question is whether plugged-in-but-idle devices continue to draw current, and if so, how much.

A September 2025 Facebook discussion in the group *Middle Aged and Boomers United* collected hundreds of conflicting answers. The same debate had appeared on Reddit's r/Frugal community as far back as 2010 and on X (formerly Twitter) in recurring cycles since. The debate's persistence suggests that the answer is genuinely unclear to most people — which is itself revealing, since the basic physics is well-established and has been documented by governmental and research bodies for decades.

Snopes rated this claim **Mostly True** in an October 2025 fact-check by William Kramer. This entry examines what the evidence actually shows, why the nuances matter, and what the research literature says about the magnitude and distribution of standby power consumption across device types.

## What's Actually True

The core of the claim is correct. The vast majority of modern household electrical devices consume power when they are plugged in but not performing their primary function. This power draw occurs in what engineers and energy researchers call *standby mode* — a state in which the device is not actively working but is ready to resume activity near-instantaneously. Standby modes serve several purposes: they allow devices to respond to remote controls, maintain internal clocks, preserve settings in memory, download updates, maintain network connections, and provide indicator lighting. All of these functions require electrical power even when the device is notionally "off."

The phenomenon is sufficiently well-documented that the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) established the Standby Power Center at the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL), one of the premier research institutions on residential energy efficiency in the United States. The LBNL's standby power website, hosted at standby.lbl.gov, collects and publishes measurement data on standby consumption for hundreds of device categories. The laboratory's research consistently shows that nearly all new electrical products have measurable standby loads (Meier, 2001, *LBNL Report 47177*).

A foundational dataset for this topic is a large-scale survey of appliances conducted in 2005 by Energy Efficient Strategies (EES), an Australian energy policy and planning consultancy, and published by the Australian government's Energy Rating programme. The survey — formally titled *Intrusive Survey of Energy Consumption of Household Standby Power in Australian Homes* (EES, 2005) — measured standby consumption across hundreds of device categories in a sample of Australian homes. It distinguished between devices in "active standby" (maintaining some operational function), "passive standby" (powered but performing minimal function), and fully "off" states. The survey found that the overwhelming majority of devices drew power in at least passive standby mode even when switched off at their front panel controls.

Alan Meier, a senior scientist at LBNL who has published extensively on standby power across multiple decades, confirmed to Snopes fact-checker William Kramer in October 2025 that the EES survey remains one of the highest-quality measurements available, though he noted that some findings are now outdated. In particular, Meier noted that many device categories in 2025 draw less standby power than in 2005 due to mandatory efficiency regulations and improved circuit design, while others — particularly smart home devices that maintain persistent network connections — may draw more than their 2005-era equivalents.

The DOE's July 2023 article *5 Tips for Energy-Saving Renters* explicitly recommends connecting devices to power strips or smart plugs to cut standby consumption entirely when not in use. The DOE described vampire power as potentially accounting for "a significant portion" of a household's energy bill. An earlier DOE article from 2022 on the agency's Energy Saver subsite outlined specific appliance categories as "energy vampires" — hair dryers, coffee makers, and computers were cited as common household examples.

LBNL's standby power data, synthesised across studies, suggests that for a typical U.S. household, standby loads account for approximately five to ten percent of total residential electricity consumption, though this figure varies considerably with household appliance inventory and usage patterns (Meier et al., 2004, *Energy Policy*, vol. 32, pp. 197–207). The absolute cost savings from eliminating standby loads are therefore moderate but not negligible — on an average U.S. electricity bill, five to ten percent represents a non-trivial annual sum, particularly for households with high device counts.

The nuance that makes this claim "mostly" rather than "entirely" true concerns the subset of devices that do *not* draw power when switched off. The 2005 EES survey found that only 2.7 percent of lamps and 3 percent of ceiling fans measured consumed any power in the off state. These device categories do not benefit from unplugging when not in use, because their switched-off state is genuinely a zero-load state. Meier further noted to Snopes that this is becoming less true for lamps over time, as an increasing proportion incorporate smart-bulb or networked lighting features that reintroduce standby loads.

A separate practical consideration: certain large appliances should not be unplugged arbitrarily. Meier specifically warned that refrigerators — while not energy vampires in the standby sense — introduce safety and food preservation concerns if unplugged, and that the act of repeatedly plugging and unplugging any appliance with a worn power cord introduces meaningful electrical hazard risk.

## Why People Believe This (and Why Some Don't)

The claim's ambiguous reception — some dismissing it as trivial, some overstating its importance — reflects several intersecting sources of confusion.

**The invisibility of standby consumption.** Unlike active device operation, standby power consumption produces no obvious sensory evidence. A television in standby mode is dark and silent. A smartphone charger plugged into the wall without a phone attached makes no noise, produces no light, and generates minimal heat. The device appears, to all ordinary observation, to be doing nothing. This invisibility leads many people to assume that no electricity is being drawn. The assumption is understandable but incorrect: small electronic loads — the kind that maintain a clock circuit, a remote-receiver module, or a network antenna — require continuous current even when invisible.

**Scale confusion.** The debate about whether unplugging "matters" is partly a debate about scale. For a single device in standby, the power draw is typically small — often less than one watt, rarely more than a few watts. Over a year, one watt of continuous standby consumption costs approximately one kilowatt-hour per year, which at median U.S. electricity rates amounts to roughly ten to fifteen U.S. cents. Per-device savings are therefore genuinely modest. The consumer advocacy implication is that unplugging a phone charger from the wall saves so little per year that it is not worth the inconvenience.

However, this per-device framing misses the aggregate picture. A typical modern household may contain dozens or even hundreds of individually plugged-in devices: televisions, cable boxes, gaming consoles, computers, monitors, sound systems, microwaves, coffee makers, toasters, phone chargers, tablet chargers, smart speakers, wireless routers, set-top boxes, cable modems, desktop printers, electric toothbrush chargers, and many more. If each device draws even a modest standby load, the aggregate across the household is substantial. Early LBNL estimates for U.S. households averaged around 50 watts of continuous standby draw across the entire home (Meier, 2001) — which, sustained year-round, amounts to approximately 438 kilowatt-hours per year, or a meaningful fraction of a household's total consumption.

**The "always on" smart home.** A complicating factor in 2025 that was less prominent in earlier research is the proliferation of smart home devices — smart thermostats, smart speakers, voice-activated assistants, networked doorbells, always-connected appliances — that are designed to maintain continuous network connections and cannot meaningfully be "unplugged" without losing their function. These devices deliberately maintain standby draws because their utility depends on constant connectivity. This category has expanded significantly since the EES survey was conducted, and it represents a qualitative shift: not just devices that happen to draw power at rest, but devices whose core value proposition requires continuous power.

**The practical inconvenience argument.** A legitimate counterargument to routine unplugging is the inconvenience and wear cost. Repeatedly inserting and removing plugs stresses both the socket and the plug, and worn connections can introduce arcing, overheating, and fire hazard. Power strips mitigate this by placing the switching point at the strip rather than at the socket — a design specifically recommended by LBNL and DOE guidance. Smart plugs, which allow remote switching via smartphone app or voice command, offer a further refinement: they eliminate both the physical wear of repeated plugging and the inconvenience of locating and manually operating each power strip in a home.

**The "some devices don't draw" complication.** The fact that lamps and ceiling fans typically draw no power in their switched-off state creates a legitimate qualification to the general claim — but it is a qualification, not a refutation. The exception for lamps and fans is narrow. The overwhelming majority of devices with electronic components draw standby current.

## Verdict

The claim is **Mostly True**. Unplugging appliances and electronics when not in use does reduce electricity consumption, because nearly all modern devices draw power in standby or off modes. The phenomenon is well-documented in decades of research by the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, the U.S. Department of Energy, and international energy efficiency bodies. The aggregate standby load across a full household is sufficient to represent a meaningful fraction of total electricity consumption — on the order of five to ten percent for typical U.S. homes.

The claim is not "entirely" true because a small category of devices — most lamps, most ceiling fans, and a few other simple appliance types — draw no power when switched off, and unplugging them provides no electrical savings. Additionally, some appliances (refrigerators, medical devices) introduce safety or functional concerns if unplugged. And the practical implementation of aggressive unplugging carries its own wear and hazard considerations if done at the socket level rather than via power strips.

The sensible practice, as both LBNL and DOE guidance recommends, is to use smart power strips or smart plugs for clustered entertainment and office electronics where standby loads are highest — particularly televisions and associated equipment, which tend to be among the largest standby consumers — and to apply selective rather than universal unplugging across other device categories. The savings per device are small; the savings across a fully enumerated household device inventory are real.

## The Wider Picture

The standby power question sits within a broader set of concerns about residential energy efficiency that have moved substantially up the policy and consumer agenda over the past two decades.

**The regulatory response.** The magnitude of standby consumption at a national scale attracted the attention of energy regulators globally from the late 1990s. The International Energy Agency (IEA) estimated in its 2001 report *Things That Go Blip in the Night: Standby Power and How to Limit It* that standby power accounted for approximately one percent of global CO₂ emissions — a figure that prompted coordinated international regulatory action (IEA, 2001). The European Union introduced standby consumption limits through its Standby Regulation (EU No. 1275/2008), which set maximum standby power requirements for household and office equipment. The U.S. Energy Star programme — administered jointly by the Environmental Protection Agency and DOE — introduced standby power criteria as part of its product certification requirements.

These regulations have demonstrably reduced per-product standby consumption over time. Meier's 2025 observation to Snopes that current devices generally draw less standby power than their 2005 equivalents reflects the cumulative effect of these equipment efficiency standards. The trend is positive, though partially offset by the growing total count of electrical devices in households and the increasing standby demands of networked smart devices.

**The carbon arithmetic.** The environmental case for eliminating standby loads is straightforward where the generation mix includes meaningful carbon-intensive sources. If a kilowatt-hour of grid electricity generates approximately 0.4 kilograms of CO₂ equivalent (the average U.S. grid intensity as estimated by the EPA for 2022–2024), then 438 kilowatt-hours of annual standby consumption per U.S. household translates to approximately 175 kilograms of CO₂ equivalent per year. Across 130 million U.S. households, this is approximately 22 million metric tonnes of CO₂ equivalent per year from residential standby power alone — a non-trivial share of residential sector emissions. The calculation is approximate and sensitive to assumptions about average standby load and grid carbon intensity, but the order of magnitude is consistent with IEA and LBNL estimates.

**Behavioural economics and the effort-savings gap.** Research in behavioural energy economics has examined why the practical uptake of energy-saving behaviours — including unplugging appliances — persistently lags behind the potential savings they would deliver. The literature identifies a range of barriers: inconsistency between stated environmental values and actual behaviour (Steg and Vlek, 2009, *Journal of Environmental Psychology*, vol. 29, pp. 309–317), the effort cost of new habits, the invisibility of savings (you never directly observe the electricity you didn't use), and the small per-action feedback loops that make any individual act feel low-stakes.

Smart home technology addresses several of these barriers by reducing the effort cost (a voice command or scheduled automation requires no physical intervention) and by providing feedback through energy monitoring apps that report consumption in real terms. The question of whether smart home technology on net increases or decreases household energy consumption is genuinely open — networked devices carry their own standby loads, and the energy cost of the infrastructure they depend upon (cloud servers, always-on network equipment) should arguably be included in any full accounting.

**The practical prioritisation question.** For a household attempting to reduce electricity consumption, standby power reduction is real but not necessarily the highest-leverage intervention available. Heating, ventilation, and cooling (HVAC) typically accounts for 40–50 percent of U.S. residential electricity consumption — far exceeding standby loads in magnitude. Water heating, major appliances such as refrigerators and washing machines, and lighting also dominate total consumption. Unplugging chargers and turning off power strips to eliminate standby power is genuinely effective at the margin, but it should be understood as a component of a broader efficiency strategy, not the primary lever.

Energy literacy — understanding which device categories really contribute to household consumption and by how much — is both limited and unevenly distributed among consumers. Research by Attari et al. (2010, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, vol. 107, pp. 16054–16059) found that survey respondents systematically underestimated the energy consumption of high-consumption appliances (dryers, water heaters) and overestimated the savings from low-consumption behaviours (turning off lights, unplugging chargers). This systematic misperception has been replicated in subsequent studies and suggests that well-intentioned energy consumers may be allocating attention to lower-impact actions while neglecting higher-impact ones.

**The smart grid future.** Looking further forward, the challenge of standby power in the home intersects with emerging smart grid architectures that allow demand-side management at the device level — where utilities or home energy management systems can negotiate with smart appliances to defer non-essential operation to off-peak periods, and where real-time pricing signals can incentivise household-level demand response. In this future, the question "should this appliance be drawing power right now?" is answered dynamically rather than through fixed consumer habits, potentially capturing the efficiency benefits of load control without relying on consumer behaviour change.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check by William Kramer offers a reasonable assessment of the evidence and arrives at the correct "Mostly True" verdict. The piece draws on the DOE's published guidelines, the EES 2005 survey, and direct expert input from Alan Meier at LBNL — a credible and authoritative source on standby power.

**What the fact-check gets right.** The fact-check correctly identifies that the general claim is true and documents it with official governmental and research sources. The qualification — that lamps and ceiling fans typically draw no power when off — is accurate and appropriately scoped. Meier's caveat that the EES survey data is now dated in some respects, and that smart devices are changing the picture, is a sophisticated and accurate nuance.

**What could be richer.** The fact-check could go further in contextualising the magnitude question — helping readers understand not just whether standby power is real but approximately how much it costs in typical household terms. This gap is common in brief fact-checks: the binary verdict question is answered, but the order-of-magnitude calibration that would help readers make practical decisions is often missing.

**The "mostly true" rating mechanics.** The "Mostly True" verdict in this case reflects an accurate understanding of the claim's structure: true as a general rule, with a limited but real set of exceptions. This is a well-calibrated verdict for the evidence. The alternative — "True" without qualification — would mislead readers into believing the rule has no exceptions, while "Mixture" would imply a more significant balance of truth and falsehood than the evidence supports.

Understanding standby power is genuinely useful knowledge for households seeking to reduce electricity consumption and energy costs. The fact that the savings are real, even if modest per-device, is worth knowing. So is the understanding of which devices are and are not energy vampires, and why the distinction exists. The deeper picture — of how standby consumption reflects design choices, regulatory frameworks, and the architectural implications of always-connected devices — adds further context for readers who want to understand not just what to do but why the situation exists in the first place.
