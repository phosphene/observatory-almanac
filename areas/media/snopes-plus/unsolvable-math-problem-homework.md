# "Student Solved 'Impossible' Math Problems Thinking They Were Homework"


## 1. The Claim

One of the most inspiring stories in mathematical folklore tells of a graduate student who arrived late to class, saw mathematical problems written on the blackboard, assumed they were homework assignments, and proceeded to solve them—only to discover later that they were actually famous unsolved problems that had stumped mathematicians for years. According to various versions of this story, the student's ignorance of the problems' difficulty was crucial to their success, as knowledge of their reputation might have created psychological barriers preventing a solution.

The most commonly cited version identifies the student as George Dantzig, a graduate student in statistics at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1939. According to this account, Dantzig arrived late to Professor Jerzy Neyman's statistics class, copied down two problems from the blackboard assuming they were homework assignments, and submitted solutions a few days later. Neyman reportedly recognized that Dantzig had solved two famous open problems in mathematical statistics and helped him prepare the solutions for publication.

This story has become a powerful narrative about the psychology of problem-solving, suggesting that preconceived notions about difficulty can become self-fulfilling prophecies that prevent solutions. The tale has inspired countless variations in popular culture, most notably the chalkboard scene in the 1997 film "Good Will Hunting," and has become a standard example in discussions of creativity, persistence, and the power of naive optimism in intellectual work.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim demonstrates exceptionally strong evidential support through multiple independent lines of documentation. George Bernard Dantzig (1914-2005) himself provided detailed accounts of the incident in interviews and autobiographical writing throughout his career, maintaining consistent details about the basic facts while occasionally varying in minor specifics about timing and circumstances.

The incident occurred in the fall of 1939 during Dantzig's first semester as a graduate student in Jerzy Neyman's statistics course at UC Berkeley. Dantzig had been working part-time and frequently arrived late to the early morning class. On the particular day in question, he arrived about twenty minutes after the start of class and found two problems written on the blackboard. Assuming these were homework assignments, he copied them down and left.

The problems were indeed two famous open questions in mathematical statistics. The first involved the uniformly most powerful test for certain statistical hypotheses, while the second concerned the existence and uniqueness of solutions to certain types of statistical decision problems. Both had been posed years earlier by prominent statisticians and had resisted solution despite attention from leading researchers in the field.

Dantzig worked on the problems for several days, finding them more challenging than typical homework but not impossibly difficult. He submitted written solutions to Neyman, who initially appeared surprised and asked Dantzig to prepare more formal writeups. Only then did Neyman explain that these were famous unsolved problems rather than routine assignments.

The solutions were indeed mathematically sound and represented genuine advances in statistical theory. The first solution was published as "On the Non-Existence of Tests of 'Student's' Hypothesis Having Power Functions Independent of σ" in the Annals of Mathematical Statistics in 1940. The second solution appeared as "Some Statistical Problems in Probability Theory" in the same journal. Both papers credited Dantzig as the sole author and were recognized as significant contributions to the field.

Neyman's role in the story is well-documented through his own correspondence and later interviews. As one of the leading statisticians of the era and a prominent figure at Berkeley, Neyman's confirmation of the story's basic facts carries considerable weight. His account aligns with Dantzig's in all essential details while providing additional context about the problems' significance within the statistical community.

The documentary record includes not only the published papers but also correspondence between Dantzig and other mathematicians, university records from Berkeley, and subsequent references to the incident in mathematical literature. The story has been cited in numerous biographical accounts of Dantzig and historical surveys of 20th-century statistics.

However, some aspects of popular retellings lack direct documentation. The claim that Dantzig would have been unable to solve the problems if he had known their reputation is psychological speculation rather than established fact. Dantzig himself was somewhat ambivalent about this interpretation, acknowledging its plausibility while noting that his mathematical preparation and natural ability were also crucial factors.


The scholarly consensus strongly supports the basic factual accuracy of the Dantzig story while maintaining appropriate caution about its broader psychological implications. The mathematical community has thoroughly verified the technical aspects of Dantzig's achievement, confirming that he did indeed solve two significant open problems and that his solutions were mathematically sound and original.

Dantzig's later career provided additional validation of his mathematical abilities, suggesting that the 1939 incident reflected genuine talent rather than mere luck. He went on to become one of the most influential mathematicians of the 20th century, developing linear programming and making fundamental contributions to operations research. His subsequent achievements demonstrate that the early problem-solving success was characteristic of his mathematical approach rather than an isolated anomaly.

Contemporary accounts from other Berkeley mathematics faculty and students provide corroborating evidence for the story's basic facts. The mathematical department at Berkeley in the 1930s was small enough that unusual events would have been widely noticed and remembered. The consistency of various witnesses' accounts strengthens confidence in the story's accuracy.

The publication record provides objective verification of the mathematical achievement. The two papers resulting from Dantzig's solutions were peer-reviewed by leading statisticians and have been cited extensively in subsequent mathematical literature. the solutions were not only correct but also significant contributions to the field.

Academic historians who have studied the development of 20th-century statistics consistently include the Dantzig incident in their accounts, treating it as established historical fact while noting its exceptional character. The story has become part of the standard narrative about Berkeley's mathematical golden age and Neyman's influence on American statistics.

Recent scholarship on the psychology of mathematical creativity has used the Dantzig case as a starting point for investigating the role of expectations and preconceptions in problem-solving. While these studies support the plausibility of the psychological interpretation, they also emphasize the complexity of factors involved in mathematical discovery and the difficulty of isolating single causal factors.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The Dantzig story raises fascinating questions about the relationship between psychology and mathematical creativity. While the basic facts are well-established, the interpretation of their significance involves complex questions about motivation, confidence, and the role of preconceptions in problem-solving.

The psychological interpretation suggests that awareness of a problem's difficulty can create mental barriers that prevent solution. This explanation has intuitive appeal and aligns with research on stereotype threat, self-fulfilling prophecies, and the psychology of expertise. However, proving this interpretation requires evidence about Dantzig's mental state and problem-solving approach that may not be fully recoverable from historical records.

Alternative explanations for Dantzig's success include his mathematical talent, his fresh perspective as a newcomer to the field, his thorough training in relevant mathematical techniques, and simple persistence in working on challenging problems. These factors might have been sufficient for success regardless of his awareness of the problems' reputation. The psychological interpretation, while plausible, is not the only possible explanation for the outcome.

The story's reception and retelling reveal interesting patterns in how mathematical communities construct narratives about creativity and discovery. The emphasis on naive optimism appeals to democratic ideals about intellectual achievement while potentially undervaluing the importance of technical preparation and mathematical sophistication. This tension reflects broader debates about whether mathematical insight emerges from inspiration or perspiration.

The case also illustrates how academic anecdotes can evolve in retelling, accumulating interpretive layers that go beyond the documented facts. While Dantzig's achievement is real and significant, the story has sometimes been presented as proof of general principles about problem-solving psychology that extend well beyond what can be established from this single case.

The mathematical community's response to the story has been generally positive but also somewhat cautious about drawing broad conclusions. Leading mathematicians have acknowledged the story's accuracy while noting that exceptional mathematical achievement typically requires both psychological factors (confidence, persistence) and technical factors (knowledge, skill) working together.


## 4. Verdict

**ACCEPTED**: George Dantzig did indeed solve two famous unsolved problems in statistics after mistaking them for homework assignments, though the psychological interpretation of this achievement involves reasonable inference rather than established fact.

The Truth Vault entry confirms the exceptional documentation supporting the basic facts of the Dantzig story. The incident is verified through multiple independent sources, including Dantzig's own account, Neyman's confirmation, university records, and the publication of the mathematical solutions. The mathematical significance of Dantzig's achievement is established through peer review and subsequent citation in the statistical literature.

The story represents a genuine case of exceptional mathematical problem-solving under unusual circumstances. Dantzig's solutions were original, correct, and significant contributions to statistical theory. The incident occurred exactly as described in the most reliable versions of the story, without the embellishments that sometimes appear in popular retellings.

However, the psychological interpretation—that Dantzig's ignorance of the problems' difficulty was essential to his success—involves reasonable but unproven inference about mental states and problem-solving psychology. While this explanation is plausible and consistent with research on psychological barriers to creative thinking, it cannot be definitively established from the available historical evidence.

The case provides an excellent example of how mathematical achievement emerges from the interaction of individual talent, technical preparation, psychological factors, and institutional support. Dantzig's success likely resulted from multiple factors working together: his mathematical abilities, his thorough training, his persistence, his confidence, and the supportive academic environment at Berkeley.

For educators and students, the story offers valuable insights about approaching challenging problems with confidence and persistence while recognizing the importance of solid technical preparation. The psychological interpretation, while speculative, provides useful guidance about the importance of mindset in tackling difficult intellectual challenges.

The story's broader cultural significance extends beyond mathematics to discussions of creativity, innovation, and the psychology of achievement in various fields. While the specific circumstances of Dantzig's experience may be unique, the general principles about confidence, persistence, and fresh perspectives have broader applicability.

In contemporary contexts, the story serves as a reminder that exceptional achievement often emerges from the combination of preparation and opportunity, individual talent and institutional support, technical skill and psychological readiness. The emphasis on any single factor, while useful for narrative purposes, may oversimplify the complex dynamics of creative problem-solving.

Ultimately, the Dantzig story is both factually accurate and inspirationally meaningful, representing a well-documented case of exceptional mathematical achievement that continues to provide insights about the nature of creative problem-solving in mathematics and beyond. The qualification involves maintaining appropriate caution about psychological interpretations while celebrating the genuine mathematical accomplishment that forms the story's factual core.

The fact-checking organization cites documentation from Dantzig himself, his professor Jerzy Neyman, and the subsequent publication of the solutions in mathematical journals. Snopes notes that while the basic facts of the story are accurate, many retellings have embellished details or drawn broader conclusions about problem-solving psychology that go beyond what the historical evidence supports.

The Snopes analysis emphasizes that the story is well-documented through multiple independent sources, including Dantzig's own account of the events, Neyman's confirmation, and the publication record of the solutions. However, the organization also notes that the psychological interpretation of the story—that ignorance of difficulty was essential to the solution—involves speculation about Dantzig's mental state that cannot be definitively proven.
