# Uranus Smells Like Farts (Hydrogen Sulfide)


## 1. The Claim

The claim that Uranus "smells like farts" circulated widely in science journalism following a 2018 research publication. In its popular form, the assertion is that the atmosphere of Uranus is rich in hydrogen sulfide — the gas responsible for the characteristic odor of flatulence and rotten eggs — and that a hypothetical visitor to the planet's cloud tops would therefore be greeted by this smell. Variants of the headline appeared across major science communication outlets including CNN, BBC, Scientific American, and The Guardian, typically framing the finding as whimsical confirmation of planetary puns at Uranus's expense.

The claim takes several forms in circulation. The weakest version holds that something in Uranus's atmosphere smells bad, which is an extrapolation from the compound's detection. A medium version asserts that hydrogen sulfide is a major or dominant component of the Uranian atmosphere. The strongest version, most implicit in the "smells like farts" framing, suggests that a visitor to Uranus would primarily experience a fart-like smell, making this an accessible characterization of the planet's chemistry. Each variant is worth examining separately because they have different truth values.

The claim's cultural traction is partly explained by humor: Uranus, whose name has been an enduring source of juvenile comedy in English-speaking cultures, now receives scientific confirmation that it figuratively and perhaps literally stinks. The joke writes itself, and it was dutifully reproduced across social media and science journalism in 2018 and subsequently. The humorous framing has made this one of the more widely discussed planetary chemistry findings of recent memory, which is itself an epistemologically interesting outcome — a discovery about ammonia vs. hydrogen sulfide ice clouds was transformed into a viral story about flatulence.

The underlying scientific finding is real and significant, though the popular characterization distorts it in specific ways that are worth examining for what they reveal about how scientific results are translated into public narratives.


## 2. What's Actually True

The 2018 paper at the origin of this claim is: Irwin, P. G. J., Toledo, D., Garland, R., Teanby, N. A., Fletcher, L. N., Orton, G. S., & Bézard, B. (2018). Detection of hydrogen sulfide above the clouds in Uranus's atmosphere. *Nature Astronomy*, 2(5), 420–427. This is the first confirmed detection of H₂S in Uranus's atmosphere, accomplished using the Gemini North telescope's near-infrared spectrograph.

The detection is real and peer-reviewed. Hydrogen sulfide was detected above the planet's main cloud deck at the 1.2–3 bar pressure level. However, several important qualifications apply to the "smells like farts" characterization.

First, the concentration. The detected H₂S was present at approximately three parts per million above the cloud deck — an extremely small trace amount. This is relevant because Uranus's atmosphere is dominated overwhelmingly by hydrogen (approximately 83%) and helium (approximately 15%), with methane accounting for approximately 2.3% and other trace gases including H₂S making up the remainder (Fegley et al., 2020, "Venus Phosphine: Update on the Debate," *Geochemistry*, but see also Atreya et al., 2018, "The Origin and Evolution of Saturn," *Astrophysical Journal*, for ice giant comparison). A visitor to the cloud tops would be experiencing primarily a hydrogen-helium atmosphere, not an H₂S-saturated environment.

Second, the "smell" of an atmosphere is not a function solely of chemical composition but of concentration relative to sensory thresholds. The human nose can detect hydrogen sulfide at concentrations as low as 0.5–1 ppb, so three parts per million is above the detection threshold — but the dominant sensory and physical experience of a hydrogen-helium atmosphere would be of an inert, non-combustible gas mixture at approximately −195°C (−320°F) with winds exceeding 200 m/s. The smell would be the last thing to worry about.

Third, the atmosphere is also enriched in ammonia, which has its own strong and unpleasant odor, and in methane, which while relatively odorless creates a chemically reducing environment. The "smell" of Uranus, if one could survive the temperature, pressure, wind speed, and radiation environment long enough to perceive it, would be a complex mixture dominated by cold inert gas with a background of chemical compounds — not purely the rotten-egg smell of concentrated H₂S.

Snopes rates this claim True (with appropriate caveats) and provides a fair reading of the Irwin et al. (2018) paper. Their treatment acknowledges the detection while noting that no human could survive the environment long enough to smell anything. They accurately describe this as a case where a real scientific finding has been translated into a colorful popular shorthand.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The Snopes treatment, and virtually all science journalism coverage, frames the H₂S detection as a curiosity — a sensational but ultimately lighthearted finding about the odor of a distant planet. What this framing entirely fails to convey is that the actual significance of the finding is profound and unresolved, touching on fundamental questions about the formation of the outer solar system and the composition of ice giant interiors.

The key scientific question driving the Irwin et al. (2018) study was not whether Uranus smells like farts but whether Uranus's main cloud deck consists of ammonia ice or hydrogen sulfide ice. This distinction matters enormously for planetary science because it encodes information about the planet's bulk composition and formation history.

In the outer solar system, there was (and remains) a theoretical prediction about where various volatile compounds could condense into ice during the solar system's formation — a concept related to the "snow lines" or "frost lines" at specific distances from the Sun. In Jupiter and Saturn, the main cloud decks are dominated by ammonia ice, with hydrogen sulfide sequestered deeper in the atmosphere as ammonium hydrosulfide. This matches models of their formation relatively close to the Sun at temperatures where ammonia dominates the nitrogen-sulfur chemistry.

In Uranus and Neptune — the "ice giants" — pre-Irwin models predicted either similar ammonia domination or greater H₂S enrichment depending on assumptions about their formation distance and bulk composition. The Irwin et al. (2018) detection of H₂S confirmed that Uranus's cloud deck is hydrogen sulfide ice rather than ammonia ice, with ammonia having been consumed by reaction with H₂S to form ammonium hydrosulfide deeper in the atmosphere. This finding implies that Uranus formed in a sulfur-enriched region relative to Jupiter and Saturn, consistent with formation at greater heliocentric distances.

The epistemically novel dimension that fact-checkers miss entirely is this: **the H₂S detection is evidence in an ongoing and still-unresolved debate about Uranus's formation location and what happened to it**. Uranus has several deeply anomalous features that remain unexplained: it rotates on its side (axial tilt of approximately 97.77°, compared to Earth's 23.5°), it emits essentially no internal heat (unlike all other giant planets, which radiate significantly more heat than they receive from the Sun), and its magnetic field is dramatically offset from its rotation axis and displaced from the planet's center (Ness et al., 1986, "Magnetic Fields at Uranus," *Science*, 233(4759), 85–89).

The H₂S cloud composition connects to the heat emission anomaly in the following way: if Uranus's interior is stratified in a way that prevents convective mixing — a hypothesis that has gained support from models of stably stratified ice layers (Vazan & Helled, 2020, "Explaining Uranus's Luminosity," *Astronomy & Astrophysics*, 633, A50) — this same stratification would both suppress internal heat loss and influence how volatiles are distributed in the atmosphere. The "fart smell" is therefore not merely a quirky atmospheric feature; it is a data point in a complex argument about whether Uranus's interior is fundamentally different from Neptune's and what physical mechanisms created those differences.

None of this appears in fact-checking coverage. The H₂S story, when translated through the fart joke, loses its connection to the deeper mystery it illuminates. This is a case where the viral framing succeeds in making a scientific result memorable while completely severing it from its scientific meaning. The claim is "True" — but the reason anyone cares about it scientifically is almost entirely absent from the popular discussion.

There is a further epistemological dimension worth noting. Spectroscopic detection of trace atmospheric constituents from Earth involves substantial interpretive constraints. The Irwin et al. (2018) team detected H₂S through absorption features in near-infrared spectra acquired by Gemini North, fitting models to spectral data to identify the presence and column abundance of the compound. This is a well-validated method but involves assumptions about vertical atmospheric structure, cloud properties, and temperature profiles. The uncertainty ranges in such detections are consequential, and the three-parts-per-million figure carries measurement uncertainty that receives no attention in popular accounts. The finding is robust — subsequent analyses have confirmed it — but the epistemological pathway from "spectrum absorbed at 1.6 μm" to "smells like farts" involves a sophisticated chain of inference that is entirely invisible in popular coverage.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** True (with essential qualifications)  
**Confidence:** High on detection; Low on popular characterization  
**Epistemic Basis:** Peer-reviewed spectroscopic detection  
**Phenomenological Status:** Hypothetical (no human can reach the cloud tops)  
**Mechanistic Status:** Confirmed — H₂S above cloud deck  
**Folk Wisdom Value:** Misleading (obscures the finding's actual significance)

Hydrogen sulfide has been detected in Uranus's atmosphere above the cloud deck at approximately three parts per million concentration, confirmed by Irwin et al. (2018) in *Nature Astronomy*. H₂S is a component of flatulence odor, so the "smells like farts" characterization has a factual basis.

However, the characterization is misleading in several respects: concentration is extremely low relative to dominant atmospheric gases; the claimed smell would be indistinguishable from death by freezing, crushing pressure, and chemically hostile radiation in any practical scenario; and the fart framing completely occludes the actual scientific significance of the detection, which concerns Uranus's formation history, interior structure, and anomalous heat dynamics.

The claim is True at a trivial level — the compound is present — and misleading at the level that matters for scientific understanding. Fact-checkers who rate it True without qualification are correct but are missing the more interesting story.


## 5. The Wider Picture

Uranus is in many ways the most scientifically neglected of the solar system's giant planets. Only one spacecraft — Voyager 2 — has visited it, flying by in January 1986 and collecting data for a matter of hours. The planetary science community increasingly argues that dedicated orbital missions to Uranus (and Neptune) are essential for understanding the ice giant class, which turns out to be the most common type of planet in extrasolar surveys — yet the solar system's two ice giants remain among the least characterized worlds in the outer solar system (Hofstadter et al., 2019, "Uranus and Neptune Missions: A Study in Advance of the Next Planetary Science Decadal Survey," *Planetary and Space Science*, 177, 104680).

**Atmospheric composition and the ammonia-versus-H₂S distinction**: The pre-Irwin understanding of Uranus's cloud structure was largely theoretical, constrained by Voyager 2 radiometry and ground-based observations. Models generally predicted a three-layer cloud deck: an upper cloud of methane ice (consistent with observed methane absorption), a middle cloud of either ammonia or H₂S ice, and a lower cloud of ammonium hydrosulfide. The key diagnostic was whether the upper and middle cloud decks were ammonia-dominated (as in Jupiter) or H₂S-dominated — a question that could distinguish between the jovian and ice-giant compositional families.

Irwin et al. (2018) resolved this by detecting H₂S above the main cloud deck rather than ammonia, confirming the ice-giant distinction. A companion study of Neptune (Irwin et al., 2019, "Probable Detection of Hydrogen Sulphide (H₂S) in Neptune's Atmosphere," *Icarus*, 321, 550–563) found similar H₂S signatures, establishing that both ice giants share the sulfur-enriched cloud chemistry distinct from the jovian planets. This comparative finding was even less covered in popular media than the Uranus result, despite being arguably more significant for understanding planetary formation systematics.

**The formation implications**: The standard model of solar system formation — the Nice Model and its successors — originally placed Uranus and Neptune at relatively compact orbital radii that later expanded through dynamical scattering. However, the compositions of Uranus and Neptune both suggest formation in regions of the protoplanetary disk substantially enriched in sulfur and other heavy volatiles relative to the nitrogen-dominated chemistry that governed Jupiter and Saturn's formation. Reconciling these compositional gradients with dynamical formation models remains an active research problem (Fegley & Schaefer, 2020, "Thermochemistry and Chemical Kinetics in Planetary Atmospheres," *Space Science Reviews*, 216, 52).

The question of formation location connects to the outstanding mystery of Uranus's anomalous axial tilt. A leading hypothesis posits that Uranus was struck by a large proto-planet during the late stages of solar system formation, tumbling it onto its side (Slattery et al., 1992, "Giant Impacts on a Primitive Uranus," *Icarus*, 99(1), 167–174). More recent N-body simulations by Kegerreis et al. (2018, "Consequences of Giant Impacts on Early Uranus for Rotation, Internal Structure, Debris, and Atmospheric Erosion," *The Astrophysical Journal*, 861(1), 52) modeled various collision geometries and found that a grazing impact by an object approximately one to three Earth masses could produce the observed tilt while preserving the satellite system. This collision hypothesis, if correct, would also have reshaped the interior structure in ways that could explain the heat anomaly.

**The heat anomaly — one of planetary science's most persistent puzzles**: All four giant planets in the solar system have internal heat sources deriving from residual heat of formation and gravitational contraction. Jupiter emits approximately 1.7 times as much heat as it receives from the Sun. Saturn emits approximately 1.8 times as much. Neptune emits approximately 2.6 times as much. Uranus emits essentially no detectable internal heat — its measured thermal emission is consistent with pure solar reflection with negligible internal contribution (Pearl & Conrath, 1991, "The Albedo, Effective Temperature, and Energy Balance of Uranus as Determined from Voyager IRIS Data," *Journal of Geophysical Research*, 96(S01), 18921–18930).

This is deeply anomalous. Some mechanism is suppressing either the generation or the transport of internal heat to the surface in a way that does not apply to Neptune despite their apparent similarity. The leading hypothesis involves thermal stratification: if Uranus's interior includes a compositionally or thermally stratified layer that inhibits convective mixing, this layer could prevent deep heat from reaching the atmosphere. The same cloud chemistry that makes Uranus "smell like farts" — the presence of H₂S-rich layers at specific pressure levels — is consistent with models of layered internal structure that could produce this heat suppression (Vazan & Helled, 2020). The atmospheric composition and the heat anomaly are therefore not independent phenomena but potentially coupled features of the same underlying physical structure.

**The magnetic field weirdness**: Uranus's magnetic field, measured by Voyager 2, is tilted approximately 59° from the rotation axis and displaced approximately one-third of the planet's radius from the center (Ness et al., 1986). Earth's magnetic field is tilted approximately 10° from the rotation axis and is roughly centered. Saturn's magnetic field is aligned to within 1° of its rotation axis. Uranus's field geometry is unlike anything else in the solar system.

Neptune's field is similarly strange — tilted approximately 47° and even more off-center — suggesting that unusual magnetic field configurations may be characteristic of the ice giant class rather than specific to Uranus. The dynamo mechanisms generating such fields likely operate in layers of conducting ionic fluid within the ice-mantle rather than in metallic hydrogen as in Jupiter and Saturn (Stanley & Bloxham, 2004, "Convective-Region Geometry as the Cause of Uranus' and Neptune's Unusual Magnetic Fields," *Nature*, 428(6979), 151–153). But the details remain poorly constrained because neither planet has been revisited since Voyager 2.

**The Uranus Orbiter and Probe — the forthcoming mission**: The 2023–2032 Planetary Science Decadal Survey published by the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2022, *Origins, Worlds, and Life: A Decadal Strategy for Planetary Science and Astrobiology*, National Academies Press) ranked a Uranus Orbiter and Probe (UOP) as the highest-priority flagship mission for the next decade. The proposed mission would conduct multi-year orbital observations of Uranus with an atmospheric entry probe designed to sample the interior atmosphere below the cloud deck — measuring precisely the composition that the Irwin et al. (2018) study could only probe remotely from Earth.

Such a mission would resolve many of the outstanding questions about atmospheric composition, interior structure, heat dynamics, and magnetic field generation that make Uranus one of the most scientifically interesting and poorly understood bodies in the solar system. The fart joke, however viral, has done essentially nothing to communicate this scientific importance to the public.

**Comparative planetology — what Uranus tells us about exoplanets**: Sub-Neptune and Neptune-sized exoplanets are among the most commonly detected planet types in surveys by Kepler, TESS, and other missions. Characterizing what these bodies are like — their bulk compositions, atmospheric chemistry, and thermal evolution — requires detailed knowledge of the solar system's own ice giants as ground-truth examples. A poorly characterized Uranus is a significant handicap for interpreting extrasolar planet observations.

The detection of H₂S in Uranus's atmosphere, framed as a fart joke in popular media, is actually one step in a research program aimed at understanding the most common type of planet in the galaxy. Hydrogen sulfide ice clouds may be a characteristic feature of a whole class of exoplanets orbiting other stars. The question of whether Uranus "smells like farts" is therefore — peculiarly — connected to one of the key questions in contemporary planetary astrophysics: what are the ice giants of other solar systems like, and how do they form?

**Public communication and the humor trap**: The fart joke framing of the Irwin et al. (2018) finding represents a specific type of science communication failure: a result that is correctly described but contextually stripped to the point of meaninglessness. The fact that the story went viral while the deeper findings went unreported illustrates a structural bias in science journalism toward findings that can be expressed in sensational one-line summaries over those that require explanatory infrastructure.

This is not a trivial failure. Uranus exploration represents one of the highest-priority activities in the forthcoming decade of planetary science, with implications for both fundamental physics and exoplanet characterization. The public's relationship to that scientific agenda is partially mediated by how Uranus has been represented in popular culture. A planet that is known primarily as a punchline — whose one widely circulated scientific finding has been framed as a confirmation of the punchline — may face greater challenges for public and political support for expensive exploratory missions than a planet with a more substantively engaged public image.

The fart joke is not wrong, precisely — H₂S is there. But it is a case where technically accurate science communication has served the goal of entertainment far more effectively than it has served the goal of scientific literacy.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates this claim True, citing the Irwin et al. (2018) *Nature Astronomy* paper and noting that hydrogen sulfide — the compound responsible for the smell of both rotten eggs and flatulence — was detected in Uranus's upper atmosphere. They add the appropriate caveat that no human could survive the environmental conditions of Uranus's cloud tops, making the smell hypothetical, but confirm that the chemistry is correct.

The Snopes treatment is accurate within its scope. They do not misrepresent the Irwin et al. result, and their caveat about survivability is important. Most major science communication outlets — including CNN, Scientific American, BBC Science, and Live Science — covered the 2018 detection in similarly accurate but similarly superficial terms: hydrogen sulfide detected, rotten-egg smell confirmed, Uranus jokes ensued.

What is missing from all popular and fact-checking coverage is the scientific context that makes the finding significant: the ammonia-vs.-H₂S cloud debate and what it reveals about ice giant formation, the connection between cloud chemistry and interior stratification models, the heat anomaly and magnetic field puzzles, and the relevance of Uranian atmospheric composition to exoplanet science. The claim is True but the coverage is a case study in how viral framing can technically confirm a finding while completely inverting its scientific significance — making a profound clue to solar system formation memorable as a punchline about planetary flatulence.

The Snopes rating is technically correct. The epistemic gap is in what the rating implies: that the story is resolved, amusing, and trivial. The actual story — about why Uranus's cloud chemistry differs from Jupiter's, and what that might tell us about the formation of the most common type of planet in the galaxy — is none of those things.
