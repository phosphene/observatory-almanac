# A viral US map shows how climate change will transform different regions


## 1. The Claim

At irregular but recurring intervals, a map circulates on social media purporting to show how climate change will alter the United States region by region. The images vary in their specific claims: some show projected temperature anomalies across the country by 2050 or 2100; others display a "climate analogue" comparison, showing which present-day location's climate a given city will resemble in the future; still others map drought risk, wildfire probability, sea level inundation zones, or heat index projections. What unites them is their presentation as definitive or near-definitive, their high shareability due to colour saturation and geographic familiarity, and a caption layer — whether in the image itself or in the post that carries it — that tends toward alarming certainty.

The most durable and widely shared of these maps originated from a 2019 peer-reviewed study published in *Nature Communications* by ecologists Matthew Fitzpatrick and Robert Ferreira, who constructed a dataset of "contemporary climatic analogues" — for hundreds of North American cities, they calculated which present-day city best matched the projected future climate of each location. The visualisation derived from that paper showed, for example, that Washington D.C. would feel climatically like Paragould, Arkansas; that Minneapolis would come to resemble Kansas City; and that San Francisco's mild Mediterranean climate would shift toward something resembling present-day Los Angeles. The interactive map was covered extensively by the Washington Post, the New York Times, the Guardian, and dozens of other outlets when it was published, and since then has periodically re-entered circulation as a graphic with reduced or absent attribution.

Other versions of the viral format include maps derived from NOAA national climate assessments, IPCC regional projection data, and research outputs from institutions including the Union of Concerned Scientists, NASA's Goddard Institute for Space Studies, and the Climate Impact Lab. The claim's core element is consistent: here is a map, and it shows you where you live is going to change in specific, spatially graspable terms.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The underlying science is robust.** The scientific basis for regional climate change projections over the continental United States is among the best-documented areas in all of Earth science. The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (IPCC, 2021, *Climate Change 2021: The Physical Science Basis*, Cambridge University Press) synthesised findings from hundreds of modelling groups and observational studies, confirming with high confidence that average temperatures across the contiguous US will continue to rise under all emissions scenarios assessed, with stronger warming in the interior West, the northern plains, and the Arctic-adjacent regions of Alaska. Precipitation patterns are projected to shift, with the southwestern US facing intensifying drought and the upper Midwest and Northeast seeing increased precipitation variability.

The Fitzpatrick and Ferreira study (Fitzpatrick, M.C. & Ferreira, R.R., 2019, "Contemporary climatic analogs for 540 North American urban areas in the late 21st century," *Nature Communications*, 10, article 614) is methodologically sound and was peer-reviewed. The analogue approach — finding an existing location that matches a projected future climate — is a legitimate and useful communication strategy precisely because it translates abstract temperature anomalies into experiential terms that non-specialists can register. The finding that many northern cities will experience climates currently associated with locations several hundred kilometres to the south is consistent with the broader body of regional climate projection research and does not depend on extreme or contested assumptions.

Regional projections from the Fourth National Climate Assessment (Reidmiller, D.R. et al., eds., 2018, *Impacts, Risks, and Adaptation in the United States: Fourth National Climate Assessment, Volume II*, US Global Change Research Program) are similarly grounded. The report documented with high confidence that the frequency and intensity of extreme heat events across the US has increased since the mid-twentieth century and will continue to do so; that sea level rise poses increasing risk to coastal communities in the Southeast and mid-Atlantic; and that wildfire risk in the western US is directly connected to warming-driven changes in moisture availability and ignition conditions.

**Where the viral framing goes wrong.** The scientific outputs that these maps encode are honest; the way viral sharing treats them is frequently not. Several specific problems recur.

*Scenario conflation.* Climate projections are always scenario-conditional. They are model outputs that describe what is likely to happen if greenhouse gas emissions follow particular trajectories — the IPCC uses a range of Shared Socioeconomic Pathways from low-emission SSP1-1.9 to high-emission SSP5-8.5. Viral maps almost universally present a single projection without specifying which scenario it represents, often using high-end projections because they are visually dramatic. A map showing worst-case 2100 conditions and a map showing moderate-mediated 2050 conditions will look dramatically different, but when both circulate without labelling, they merge into a single uniform impression of future certainty. Hausfather and Peters (2020, "Emissions — the 'business as usual' story is misleading," *Nature*, 577, 618–620) have specifically criticised the tendency in science communication to default to high-end scenarios without making the conditionality explicit.

*False precision.* Regional climate projections carry genuine uncertainty ranges that are meaningful. A map that shows a specific hexadecimal colour for a county's projected 2080 average summer temperature implies a level of spatial and temporal precision that climate models do not currently deliver at the county level. The spatial resolution of general circulation models used in IPCC assessments is typically on the order of 100–300 kilometres per grid cell; downscaling methods can improve this but introduce their own sources of uncertainty. Presenting county-level or city-level maps as though they have that precision misleads viewers about what the science can and cannot say (Knutti, R., Sedláček, J., Sanderson, B.M., Lorenz, R., Fischer, E.M., & Eyring, V., 2017, "A climate model projection weighting scheme accounting for performance and interdependence," *Geophysical Research Letters*, 44, 1909–1918).

*Decontextualisation.* When maps circulate stripped of their original publication context, the specific assumptions embedded in the research design are lost along with the source attribution. The Fitzpatrick-Ferreira analogue map, for example, was constructed using a high-emissions scenario (RCP 8.5) because the authors explicitly wanted to illustrate a potential future that policy action could avert — the map was intended as a motivation for mitigation, not a statement of inevitability. That framing was explicit in the original paper and in the initial round of media coverage, but it disappeared as the image circulated on its own. Multiple social media instances of the map in subsequent years presented it as a description of what will happen rather than what could happen under a specific scenario that has grown less likely as renewable energy has expanded faster than most 2019 models assumed (Hausfather, Z. & Peters, G.P., 2020, cited above).

*Cherry-picking maps.* The "viral map" genre is not a single artefact but a recurring format. Maps showing dramatic change circulate; maps showing less dramatic or regionally heterogeneous change do not. The selective survival of alarming visualisations in social media ecosystems creates a systematically skewed impression across the audience that repeatedly encounters them (Kahan, D.M., Peters, E., Wittlin, M., Slovic, P., Ouellette, L.L., Braman, D., & Mandel, G., 2012, "The polarizing impact of science literacy and numeracy on perceived climate change risks," *Nature Climate Change*, 2, 732–735).


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The authority transfer mechanism.** This is a case of what cognitive scientists call authority transfer — a finding from a legitimate scientific study is shared in a format that retains the authority of the original (peer-reviewed science, official data, expert institutions) while losing the qualifications. Viewers who would correctly resist an unfounded claim that their city will become like Texas nevertheless accept the same claim when it arrives attached to, or visually associated with, an academic paper. The peer-reviewed origin functions as a quality certificate that the popular version borrows without inheriting the epistemological structure that makes the original defensible.

This mechanism is particularly powerful for climate communication because climate science is both genuinely alarming and genuinely uncertain at regional scales. Audiences sympathetic to the scientific consensus on anthropogenic climate change have a motivated prior to believe that specific vivid projections are accurate, because they know that climate change is real and large-scale. The authentic concern functions as a conduit for claims that go beyond what the science supports.

**Geographic self-relevance.** Maps work on audiences in ways that textual or numerical claims do not. A map places the viewer inside the information — it is about their home, their city, their family's community. This spatial self-reference is a powerful attention-capture mechanism and makes the information more memorable and more emotionally resonant than an equivalent claim presented as a national statistical summary (Tversky, B., 2000, "Remembering spaces," in E. Tulving & F.I.M. Craik, eds., *The Oxford Handbook of Memory*, Oxford University Press). The vivid specificity of "your city will feel like [recognisable other city]" is one reason the Fitzpatrick-Ferreira analogue map spread so widely: it translated abstract science into a personally legible comparison.

**The simplification demand of viral formats.** Social media platforms favour content that can be assessed, evaluated, and shared in seconds. A map with colour gradients and a descriptive caption meets this criterion; a map with a long methodological note, a scenario specification, and probability ranges does not. The structural demands of the sharing ecosystem create systematic pressure toward oversimplification. This is not a failure of individual viewers' intelligence; it is a predictable outcome of the incentive structure built into platforms that measure engagement in terms of shares and reactions rather than comprehension (Vosoughi, S., Roy, D., & Aral, S., 2018, "The spread of true and false news online," *Science*, 359(6380), 1146–1151).

**Climate anxiety and narrative need.** Research on climate communication has found that people processing high levels of climate anxiety are more ready to engage with vivid specific scenarios than with probabilistic ranges, partly because narrative specificity — even unwanted specificity — offers a sense of cognitive grip on an otherwise overwhelming problem (Clayton, S., Manning, C.M., Krygsman, K., & Speiser, M., 2017, "Mental health and our changing climate: Impacts, implications, and guidance," American Psychological Association and ecoAmerica). A map that shows exactly what will change and where, even one that overstates its evidentiary basis, provides the kind of actionable specificity that abstract statements about global mean temperature increase cannot.


## 4. Verdict

**Accurate but misleadingly framed — the maps represent real science; the viral framing removes the qualifications that make the science honest.** The genre of "US climate change transformation map" is not invented or fabricated. The scientific literature on regional climate projections is extensive, peer-reviewed, and genuinely alarming in its implications. Maps like the Fitzpatrick-Ferreira climate analogue visualisation are legitimate science communication products derived from rigorous research designs.

However, the viral life of these maps habitually strips the scenario specification, probability language, uncertainty ranges, and temporal conditionality that distinguish responsible scientific communication. A map showing worst-case high-emissions 2100 conditions presented as a statement about what will happen, without the qualifier that it represents one scenario in a range of possible futures, is misleading regardless of the quality of the underlying research. The scientific consensus on climate change is strong enough that it does not require misrepresentation to constitute a powerful argument for action; maps that overstate their precision and certainty ultimately undermine credibility with parts of the audience they are trying to reach while providing misleading confidence to audiences that already believe the basic message.

The appropriate response to encountering one of these maps is not to dismiss it as fake — it almost certainly is not fake — but to ask: which scenario does this represent, for which model or model ensemble, at what spatial resolution, with what confidence interval, and was this map originally designed for the context in which it is now circulating?


## 5. The Wider Picture

The viral US climate change map is a subset of a broader phenomenon in science communication: the selective circulation of the most dramatic available visualisation of a complex probabilistic finding. This pattern appears with medical research (effect size amplification without baseline), economic projections (worst-case scenario normalisation), and demographic data (selective spatial aggregation to produce desired gradients), but it is especially acute with climate data because the subject combines high emotional stakes, genuine scientific concern, and visualisation outputs that are inherently dramatic at full-emissions scenarios.

The National Climate Assessment process represents the US government's attempt to synthesise regional climate science for a non-specialist audience. The contrast between the careful probability language in those reports and the certainty language in social media presentations of extracted maps from those reports illustrates the communication gap precisely. The Fourth National Climate Assessment (Reidmiller et al., 2018) uses explicit confidence levels, references to model ensemble spread, and explicit scenario labelling throughout — conventions that almost never survive translation into shareable map format.

Climate scientists have grappled with this problem explicitly. Research by Maibach, Myers, and Leiserowitz (2014, "Climate scientists need to set the record straight: There is a scientific consensus that human-caused climate change is happening," *Earth's Future*, 2, 295–298) and later work by Nisbet and colleagues has examined how the selective presentation of extreme projections can both motivate and alienate different parts of the public, finding that accuracy in communicating uncertainty does not weaken the case for action and may strengthen it with scientifically literate audiences. Work by the Climate Communication project at Yale and George Mason University has found that clear and honest probability framing builds rather than erodes long-term credibility with both accepting and sceptical audiences.

There is also a geopolitical complication specific to the US context. Climate change has been politically polarised in American public discourse in a way that has few parallels in other major democracies. In environments where the basic scientific fact of anthropogenic climate change is contested along partisan lines, oversimplified dramatic maps can be weaponised by critics who point out the overstated certainty as evidence that climate scientists are engaging in propaganda rather than science. Accurate, carefully scoped communication is therefore both an epistemic obligation and a strategic one.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Fact-checking organisations have engaged with specific viral instances of US climate maps with mixed results, largely because the genre poses structural challenges to standard true/false adjudication. The core issue — that the underlying science is legitimate but the framing oversimplifies it — resists the binary logic that most fact-check formats are built around.

Snopes has not addressed this category of claim as a standing topic. PolitiFact and the Washington Post Fact Checker have addressed specific politically charged climate claims — notably statements by politicians about climate projections — but the viral map genre itself, which is typically not attributable to a named political actor, falls outside their standard editorial workflow.

Climate-specific fact-checking organisations including Climate Feedback (affiliated with the French National Centre for Scientific Research) have reviewed viral climate claims and maps with more methodological precision, using panels of climate scientists to evaluate specific viral articles and images against the primary literature. Their approach — rating the scientific credibility of a specific claim against the evidence it cites or implies — is better suited to the genre than standard political fact-checking, but Climate Feedback's audience is substantially smaller than the social media ecosystems in which the maps circulate.

For media literacy educators, the viral climate map is an important teaching case precisely because it sits at the intersection of real science and real oversimplification. The lesson is not that the maps are wrong — the underlying science is largely not wrong — but that scientific claims carry their epistemological commitments as non-negotiable cargo. A projection is not a prediction. A scenario is not a forecast. A model output is not a measurement. These distinctions, which are routine in the scientific community and explicit in the primary literature, are systematically erased in the social media presentation of the findings, and restoring them is the core task of honest science communication.
