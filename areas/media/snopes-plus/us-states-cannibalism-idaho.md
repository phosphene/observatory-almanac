# Cannibalism is technically legal in Idaho and some other US states


## 1. The Claim

The claim that cannibalism is "technically legal" in Idaho — and, in more expansive versions, in the majority of American states — has circulated as a curiosity of American criminal law since at least the late 1990s and has been a recurring feature of "weird law facts" lists, law school hypotheticals, internet trivia, and credulous legal commentary ever since. The claim has achieved cultural saturation in the form of "fun facts" about American jurisprudence, appearing in viral Twitter threads, Reddit posts, law school discussion forums, and the margins of journalism about American legal exceptionalism.

The core reasoning offered in support of the claim is a statutory gap argument: the criminal codes of most American states lack a specific statute prohibiting the act of consuming human flesh, and the ancient common law crime of cannibalism — while historically attested — does not form part of the codified criminal law of most American jurisdictions. The logical move from "no specific statute" to "technically legal" is the key step in the claim's construction, and it is a step that produces an attention-grabbing proposition with enough superficial legal plausibility to circulate convincingly in popular discourse.

The claim sometimes carries an additional observation about Idaho in particular — that Idaho did have or did not have a specific anti-cannibalism statute at various points in its history — and uses Idaho as the paradigm case both because the state has sometimes been held up as an example of libertarian underregulation and because Idaho's 1990 cannibalism statute, discussed below, complicates the picture in an interesting way. The broader formulation of the claim — that the majority of U.S. states lack dedicated cannibalism statutes and therefore that cannibalism is technically legal in those states — is the version most relevant to evaluating the claim's logic.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The statutory gap is real but legally irrelevant.** It is true that most American state criminal codes do not contain a statute specifically titled "cannibalism" or "consumption of human remains" that creates a discrete, enumerated crime of consuming human flesh. A survey of state criminal codes as of 2024 finds dedicated cannibalism statutes in only a handful of states, Idaho notably among them. This statutory gap is a genuine feature of American criminal law, and it is the factual foundation for the claim.

The statutory gap is, however, not equivalent to a legal permission. The absence of a statute specifically prohibiting X does not mean that doing X is legal, because X may be constituted by acts that are separately prohibited. Cannibalism — understood as a human being consuming the flesh of another human being — cannot be accomplished without a predicate act or circumstance involving human death and dismemberment, and virtually every realistic predicate circumstance is independently criminal.

**The pyramid of predicate crimes.** Consider the realistic universe of circumstances in which a person could consume human flesh:

*Murder.* The most obvious pathway is killing another person and consuming their flesh. Murder is criminalised in all fifty states, the District of Columbia, and all U.S. territories. The absence of a cannibalism statute adds nothing to the penal consequences of killing a human being; the murderer faces homicide prosecution irrespective of what they did with the body afterward.

*Desecration of a corpse.* In the event that a person dies of natural or accidental causes and a third party subsequently consumes the flesh, the predicate act of dismembering or otherwise manipulating the corpse to obtain tissue for consumption falls under corpse desecration or abuse of corpse statutes. These statutes exist in virtually every American state. While they vary in their exact scope and penalties, they are sufficiently broad in most jurisdictions to cover the acts required to consume human flesh from a naturally deceased person without that person's prior consent.

*Unlawful interference with interment.* Most states additionally prohibit interference with body disposition, grave robbery, and related acts. Someone disinterring a body to consume it would face liability under these statutes independently of any cannibalism provision.

*Consent and its limits.* The most legally interesting edge case — and the one that forms the basis for the narrow legal argument that cannibalism could be "technically legal" — is the consent scenario: a living person gives explicit, informed, contemporaneous consent to another person to consume part of their body, and the act occurs without causing serious physical harm or death. This scenario is extremely narrow in two senses: first, it is nearly physically impossible to consume meaningful quantities of human flesh from a living donor without causing serious physical injury or death; and second, even in the most attenuated version (minor tissue, full consent), the legal question of whether valid consent can be given to conduct that would otherwise constitute aggravated assault varies by jurisdiction and may not necessarily result in a legal clearance.

The infamous Rotenburg/Meiwes case in Germany in 2001, in which Armin Meiwes killed and consumed Bernd Brandes with Brandes's documented prior consent, tested the legal limits of consent as a defence and produced a manslaughter conviction, later upgraded to murder on appeal (BGH — 2 StR 236/04, Bundesgerichtshof, 2005). While German law does not translate directly to American jurisdictions, the case illustrates that the consent defence encounters substantial legal obstacles even in systems with relatively robust consent doctrine.

**Idaho's 1990 anti-cannibalism statute.** A crucial complication for the specific Idaho version of the claim is that Idaho enacted a specific anti-cannibalism statute in 1990. Idaho Code § 18-5003, enacted as part of a legislative response to circumstances discussed in the legislative record of that period, explicitly prohibits cannibalism and defines it as "the act of a person who wittingly eats the flesh or blood of another human being." The statute provides for imprisonment of up to fourteen years, with an exception for acts done from necessity in a life-threatening emergency to survive.

The existence of this statute directly falsifies the version of the claim that presents Idaho as a state without a cannibalism prohibition. Versions of the claim that treat Idaho as the exemplar of cannibalism permissiveness through statutory omission are factually wrong: Idaho affirmatively criminalises cannibalism. The necessity exception, while legally and morally interesting, does not create a general permission; it creates a narrow affirmative defence applicable only in extreme survival circumstances analogous to those addressed in the famous nineteenth-century cannibalism cases including *R v Dudley and Stephens* (14 QBD 273, 1884) and the related American cases.

**The states without specific cannibalism statutes.** It remains true that most American states do not have specific cannibalism statutes. The states that have enacted specific provisions include Idaho (as above), and a small number of others have provisions addressing mutilation of human remains that could be applied to cannibalism acts. But the absence of a specific statute in the remaining states does not create a legal vacuum, for the reasons set out above: the predicate acts are universally criminalised, and prosecution under homicide, aggravated assault, corpse desecration, and related provisions is available.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The "technically legal" framing and its rhetorical power.** The phrase "technically legal" does a lot of work in this claim. It signals to the audience that the speaker is making a narrowly precise legal observation — drawing on technical statutory analysis rather than popular moral intuition — while the substance of the observation is that a thing most people regard as maximally transgressive is, at least in some formal sense, not legally prohibited. This combination of technical register and transgressive content is maximally attention-grabbing: it makes the claim feel like insider legal knowledge rather than tabloid sensationalism.

The "technically legal" formulation appears repeatedly in claims about American law that follow the same structure: X is not specifically prohibited by a statute, therefore X is "technically legal." This structure is legally naive but rhetorically effective. It captures the genuine insight that statutory law is not exhaustive — that not everything morally or socially prohibited is statutorily enumerated — but draws the wrong conclusion from this insight, by treating statutory enumeration as the only mechanism through which conduct can be criminalised or legally constrained.

**Epistemic novelty — the positive/normative gap in how people model law.** The most interesting epistemic phenomenon illustrated by the cannibalism legality claim is the widespread implicit model of law as a positive catalogue of prohibitions: if something is not listed as illegal, it is legal. This model is intuitive — it aligns with the reasonable expectation that people should be able to determine what is legal and illegal by consulting a code — but it reflects a misunderstanding of how criminal liability actually operates in common law systems.

American criminal law, derived from English common law, operates through multiple overlapping layers: constitutional provisions, federal statutes, state statutes, common law doctrines incorporated by reference into statutory schemes, and judicial interpretation that elaborates the scope of general provisions to cover specific conduct. The relationship between these layers means that conduct can be criminal even absent a specific statutory prohibition addressing it by name, because existing provisions cover the acts that constitute the conduct.

This is not a trivial technicality. The structure of criminal law — using general prohibitions covering broad categories of conduct (murder, assault, desecration) rather than exhaustive enumerations of every specifically prohibited act — is a deliberate design choice reflecting the difficulty of enumerating in advance every form that harmful conduct might take (Dressler, J., 2012, *Understanding Criminal Law*, 6th ed., LexisNexis). The opposite model — specific statutory enumeration of every prohibited act — would require a grotesquely complex code and would create perverse incentives to identify and exploit narrow gaps, which is exactly what the "technically legal" framing does rhetorically.

The cannibalism claim thus illustrates a broader feature of popular legal understanding: people tend to model law as a list of no's, when law is better modelled as a system of general principles with specific applications worked out through interpretation and prosecution. The difference matters especially in marginal cases and in claims that depend on the absence of specific statutory items.

There is a related epistemological point about how unusual cases function in legal discourse. Edge cases — the extreme, transgressive, or bizarre hypotheticals that populate law school classrooms and legal trivia — are valuable pedagogically because they test the limits of legal principles and force precise analysis of how statutory scope, common law application, and prosecutorial discretion interact. But when these edge case observations migrate from legal pedagogy to popular discourse, they lose their carefully qualified "in the strict sense of X, and assuming Y does not apply" framing and become unqualified claims about legality — "technically legal" — that import a practical permission that the underlying legal analysis never established.


## 4. Verdict

**True with crucial context that transforms the claim's meaning.** The narrow statutory observation underlying the claim — that most American states lack specific anti-cannibalism statutes, and that Idaho in particular had this feature (with the important exception that Idaho enacted a specific statute in 1990) — is accurate at the level of positive law survey. The conclusion typically drawn from this observation — that cannibalism is therefore "technically legal" in those states — is misleading to the point of being practically false.

Virtually every realistic act constituting cannibalism involves conduct independently criminalised by existing statutes. Murder, aggravated assault, corpse desecration, and abuse of human remains laws collectively ensure that there is no practical scenario in which a person consumes human flesh without committing one or more crimes in the process. The "technically legal" qualifier invokes a technical legal reading that is, in practice, inapplicable to any real-world conduct — which is precisely the rhetorical trick the phrase performs. It sounds like a precise legal observation; it is actually a hypothetical that has no application in the real world absent circumstances so unusual as to be effectively academic.

The Idaho-specific version of the claim is additionally confused because Idaho has, since 1990, specifically prohibited cannibalism by statute, undermining even the statutory observation that underlies the claim.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The cannibalism legality claim belongs to a family of claims about American legal exceptionalism that circulate as curiosities and that rely on the same statutory gap logic: X is not specifically prohibited, therefore X is technically legal. Similar claims have been made about various forms of incest between distant relations, about unusual property crimes, about obscure forms of self-harm, and about other transgressive acts whose absence from specific statutory enumeration in some jurisdictions is treated as a de facto legalisation.

These claims share a common function in popular culture: they perform a kind of irreverence toward law and moral consensus, signalling sophisticated awareness that law is incomplete and contingent while simultaneously using that incompleteness to make moral transgression seem less absolute than social consensus holds it to be. The resulting cultural work is not primarily factual but rhetorical: the claims say "the law is not as comprehensive as you think" and then imply, misleadingly, that this means transgressions have less legal reality than you thought.

From a comparative law perspective, the U.S. pattern of statutory comprehensiveness (or its absence) in state criminal codes is genuinely variable across jurisdictions. Studies of state criminal code structure find significant variation in the degree to which states rely on enumerated specific provisions versus broad general provisions with wide application (Robinson, P.H. & Dubber, M.D., 2007, *The American Model Penal Code: A Brief Overview*, New Criminal Law Review, 10(3), 319–341). This variation is the real, substantive legal story that the cannibalism claim touches on — but that story is about the architecture of criminal codes and prosecutorial discretion, not about whether some conduct is really permitted.

The Idaho anti-cannibalism statute of 1990 has its own context. The legislative record suggests the statute emerged partly from concerns arising from specific cases involving unconventional religious practices and partly from a broader legislative push in several states during the late 1980s and early 1990s to enumerate more explicitly certain categories of conduct that had previously been addressed only through general provisions. Similar legislative activity around corpse-related offenses occurred in multiple states during this period, often in response to specific prosecutorial difficulties in establishing liability for conduct that fell in the gaps between existing specific provisions.

The necessity exception in Idaho's statute — which provides a defence for cannibalism "under extreme life-threatening conditions as the only apparent means of survival" — has a direct historical antecedent in the English common law cases arising from maritime disasters and survival situations. *R v Dudley and Stephens* established that necessity is not a defence to murder even in extreme survival situations, producing one of the most debated doctrinal outcomes in common law history. The Idaho legislature's choice to include a necessity exception thus takes a position, at least implicitly, on one of the oldest unresolved questions in criminal law and moral philosophy — whether necessity can, in extremis, justify conduct otherwise absolutely prohibited.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes addressed a version of the cannibalism legality claim directly, publishing an entry that examined the claim that "cannibalism is legal in many U.S. states" and rated it as a misleading simplification for reasons that substantially overlap with the analysis above: the absence of specific cannibalism statutes does not mean that cannibalism lacks legal consequences, given the comprehensive criminalisation of the predicate acts through other provisions. However, this Snopes entry was not structured around a specific verdict label in the standard Snopes format and was treated more as an explanatory FAQ than a formal claim adjudication.

The absence of the claim from formal adjudication in the standard fact-check databases reflects the same difficulty that the Israel/ChatGPT case illustrates from a different angle: claims that are "technically accurate but misleading" create structural problems for binary verdict infrastructure. The statutory gap observation is accurate; the legal conclusion typically drawn from it is misleading; and there is no clean true/false rating that captures this distinction. The Truth Vault's "true with crucial context" category is designed precisely for claims of this type — where the factual foundation is real but the conclusion routinely drawn from it requires substantial qualification to be epistemically responsible.

A formal analysis of similar claims in popular legal discourse would likely find that "technically legal" is one of the most systematically misleading formulations in circulation, precisely because it sounds like a term of art while operating as a rhetorical gesture — performing legal precision while eliding the legal complexity that would undermine the claim's provocative conclusion.
