## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

On April 1, 2022 (April Fools' Day), the website VerveTimes published an article claiming that the U.S. Food and Drug Administration had decided to ban real meat in the United States, with the ban scheduled to take effect April 1, 2024. The article described the decision as "evidence that The Great Reset is rapidly approaching," invoked Bill Gates as an advocate for plant-based synthetic beef, and framed the purported ban as part of a broader globalist agenda. The article's framing explicitly linked the claim to the "Great Reset" conspiracy theory.

Snopes journalist Bethania Palma (2022) assigned the claim a verdict of **False**. The article originated as a declared April Fools' piece on a self-described satirical website; VerveTimes subsequently added a disclaimer clarifying that the "FDA ban on real meat is a fictional scenario." No such ban exists, has been proposed in legislation, or has been announced by any regulatory agency with jurisdiction over U.S. meat production. The FDA does not have unilateral authority to ban the sale of red meat in the United States; such authority rests primarily with the USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) under the Federal Meat Inspection Act, and meat consumption is not the kind of regulatory target that any major U.S. government body has proposed banning.

This Truth Vault entry examines the architecture of the VerveTimes article as a specimen of "fictional scenario as warning" propaganda; the "Great Reset" conspiracy theory and its documented link to antisemitic content frameworks; the political and economic context of meat consumption debates in U.S. policy; and the epistemics of satirical content that carries genuine ideological payload within a nominal joke frame.

## §2 The Claim, Its Source, and the Official Response

Palma (2022) traced the claim to VerveTimes, a website with a documented history of publishing content described by its own operators as "humorous or satirical in nature." The specific article framing explicitly invoked the "Great Reset" as context for the meat ban scenario, linking it to a real-world conspiracy theory that had attained significant circulation during the COVID-19 pandemic. When the article spread on social media and came to fact-checkers' attention, VerveTimes updated the piece to include the following disclaimer: "This is an April Fool's article and the FDA ban on real meat is a fictional scenario. A primary reason why we post April Fool's articles is to act as warnings. We want to wake people up to see what could happen if actions aren't taken to protect and preserve freedom."

This disclaimer represents an ideologically revealing inversion of typical satirical purpose. Traditional satire uses fiction to illuminate truth about real events or social conditions. The VerveTimes framing inverts this: it uses a fictional event (the meat ban) to create a feared future scenario, then declares the fictional event to be a "warning" about that scenario. The satirical wrapper provides legal and rhetorical plausible deniability ("it was just a joke") while the explicit ideological framing ("The Great Reset is rapidly approaching") communicates the genuine political message to the intended audience without the joke frame neutralizing it.

Palma (2022) correctly identified that no such FDA policy exists and cited the Anti-Defamation League's documentation of the "Great Reset" conspiracy theory as relevant context. The ADL has documented extensively that the "Great Reset" framework, derived from a 2020 World Economic Forum initiative of the same name, was transformed in right-wing online communities into a conspiracy theory alleging that global elites (often encoded in antisemitic dog-whistles as Jewish financial interests) were orchestrating the COVID-19 pandemic to implement totalitarian global governance (ADL, 2021, "The 'Great Reset' Conspiracy Flourishes Amid Continued Pandemic").

The Bill Gates reference in the article is also significant: Gates has become a recurring figure in pandemic-era conspiracy theories, including claims about COVID vaccine microchips, pandemic preplanning, and, relevant here, advocacy for eliminating conventional meat consumption. Gates has publicly advocated for affluent nations to transition to plant-based beef alternatives on climate grounds (Gates, B., 2021, *How to Avoid a Climate Disaster*, Knopf), but advocacy for a voluntary market transition is categorically distinct from government-mandated banning.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The "Warning Satire" as Paralogical Propaganda Form

The epistemically novel feature of this case is the specific propagandistic form employed by VerveTimes, which can be named *warning satire*: fictional content about a feared future scenario, published with nominal satirical framing, but which carries genuine ideological payload and is designed to circulate in ideologically sympathetic communities before or after the satirical frame is noticed or disclosed.

Warning satire differs from three adjacent forms that are better studied in the misinformation literature:

**Straightforward fabrication** (fake news) makes false factual claims with no disclosure of their fictional status and no satirical intent. Warning satire discloses its fictional status, at least eventually.

**Traditional satire** (The Onion, The Babylon Bee) uses exaggeration of existing real trends to create humor and social critique. The satirical object is something that already exists. Warning satire invents the satirical object (the meat ban) rather than exaggerating a real phenomenon.

**Astroturfing** creates false impressions of grassroots opinion. Warning satire does not primarily aim at creating false impressions about opinion—it aims at creating emotional anticipation of genuine fears.

Warning satire occupies a distinct ecological niche in the misinformation ecosystem: it provides communities with emotionally resonant content about feared scenarios, allows those communities to share and discuss the feared scenario as though it were real (because the satirical frame is often stripped or buried), and then retreats to "it was just a joke" when challenged. The "warning" framing explicitly encourages treating the fictional scenario as a likely future rather than as absurdist humor, which means the satirical frame is doing the opposite of what satire conventionally does—instead of creating critical distance, it creates emotional imminence.

Molina et al. (2021), in *Journalism Practice* (Taylor & Francis), documented that satirical misinformation shares many propagation characteristics with straightforward fabrication, including susceptibility to confirmation bias amplification and reduced cross-checking when the content aligns with pre-existing beliefs. The distinction that satire is "just a joke" does not prevent belief formation in audiences who encounter the headline or summary without the satirical disclosure.

The ideological potency of warning satire is maximized when it combines: (a) a genuinely feared policy outcome among the target audience; (b) a conspiratorial explanatory narrative that attributes the feared outcome to identifiable enemies; and (c) real elements that ground the fictional scenario in genuine debates (in this case, the real debates about plant-based meat alternatives, real advocates like Gates, and a real WEF initiative called the Great Reset). When all three elements are present, warning satire can operate as effective political messaging even when the satirical origin is known, because the message is "this could really happen" rather than "this is definitely happening."

Bail et al. (2018), in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* (National Academy of Sciences), found that exposure to ideologically incongruent social media content tends to reinforce rather than moderate political attitudes—a finding with direct implications for warning satire, which is typically consumed in ideologically homogeneous social environments where the fictional frame is treated as commentary rather than fiction.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Palma's (2022) investigation correctly identified the claim as satire, traced it to VerveTimes, explained the absence of any actual regulatory proposal, and provided relevant context about the Great Reset conspiracy theory. Several additional analytical threads merit attention:

**The April Fools timing as amplification mechanism**: The investigation identifies April Fools' Day as the publication date but does not specifically analyze how April Fools' Day publication serves claims of this type. There is a systematic pattern in which satirical content published on April 1 circulates without the satirical frame identified—the holiday provides both a reason to publish implausible-sounding content (plausible deniability) and a mechanism by which readers who encounter the content after April 1 lose the temporal context that would flag it as a holiday joke.

**The "could happen" future-tense structure**: The 2024 start date specified in the fabricated ban (April 1, 2024—notably also April Fools') creates a temporal window in which the claim is perpetually not-yet-falsified. Unlike a claim about a current or past event that can be definitively verified or falsified by checking evidence, a claim about a future policy action maintains emotional salience as a fear even after debunking, because audiences can always say "not yet."

**Regulatory authority mapping**: The investigation does not detail which U.S. agencies actually do have authority over meat safety and sales. A fuller treatment would note that U.S. meat production is regulated primarily by the USDA's FSIS under the Federal Meat Inspection Act of 1906 and the Meat Inspection Act of 1967, and that banning meat consumption or sale would require Congressional action—not regulatory action—of a politically implausible magnitude given the meat industry's substantial lobbying presence (spending roughly $13 million in federal lobbying annually in recent years, according to Center for Responsive Politics data).

**VerveTimes' broader content ecosystem**: The investigation does not characterize VerveTimes as a publication beyond noting its self-described satirical purpose. An examination of VerveTimes' content catalog would reveal a pattern of articles combining conspiracy theory frameworks with "satirical" fictional scenarios—a documentation that would contextualize this article as part of a consistent publishing strategy rather than a one-off joke.

## §5 Contextual Framing: The Policy Reality of Meat and U.S. Dietary Guidelines

The underlying policy landscape against which this claim circulates is worth examining, because a complete false premise does not mean there are no real policy debates about meat in the United States. Several genuine and ongoing debates provide the substrate that warning satire exploits:

**Climate and dietary guidelines**: The U.S. Dietary Guidelines Advisory Committee has, in various editions, acknowledged environmental sustainability as a relevant consideration in dietary guidance. In the 2020–2025 guidelines cycle, there were debates about whether to reduce recommended red meat consumption on environmental grounds; the final guidelines did not include explicit environmental sustainability language, but the debate was real (Lusk & Ellison, 2021, *American Journal of Agricultural Economics*, Wiley).

**Plant-based and lab-grown meat regulation**: The FDA and USDA have ongoing jurisdictional negotiations over how to regulate cell-cultivated (lab-grown) meat products, which began reaching commercial approval in 2023. The regulatory treatment of these novel products is genuinely complex and contested. This regulatory uncertainty is real, even if no proposed action involves banning conventional meat.

**State legislative responses**: In reaction to exactly the kind of warning satire represented by this VerveTimes article, multiple U.S. states have passed or proposed "meat protection" legislation explicitly prohibiting state and local governments from restricting conventional meat sales. Florida, Iowa, and other states have enacted such laws. These laws are responses to a phantom threat—no U.S. government body has proposed banning meat—but their passage creates a secondary misinformation layer: the existence of "meat protection laws" can itself be cited as evidence that a real threat exists, in a feedback loop where fabricated warnings produce real legislative responses that are then cited as confirming the fabricated warning.

Lusk (2019), in *American Journal of Agricultural Economics* (Wiley), found that consumer beliefs about food policy threats are highly susceptible to framing effects, with identical regulatory proposals generating very different opposition levels depending on whether they are framed as restrictions or as protections.

## §6 Verdict Assessment and Epistemic Calibration

Snopes' verdict of **False** is correct. No U.S. government body has proposed, legislated, or implemented any ban on real meat sales. The FDA does not have unilateral authority to implement such a ban. The VerveTimes article was explicitly labeled a satirical April Fools' piece by its own authors after circulation. The underlying regulatory, legislative, and political context offers no support for the claim's core assertion.

The broader epistemic significance of this case lies in the *warning satire* form: the deployment of a fictional feared scenario within a conspiracy theory framework, combined with a satirical frame that provides rhetorical escape while carrying genuine political messaging. This form is distinct from both straightforward fabrication and traditional satire, and fact-checking frameworks that focus primarily on factual accuracy without interrogating the propagandistic function of the satirical frame may underestimate the impact of such content.

The "Great Reset" framing links this specific false claim to a larger antisemitism-adjacent conspiracy theory infrastructure with documented real-world consequences, including violence connected to Great Reset rhetoric in several incidents documented by the ADL (2021). The meat ban claim is therefore not merely a harmless April Fools' joke but a functional component within an active misinformation ecosystem.

**EARC Gap: A (Analytical)** — The primary gap is analytical: the investigation correctly identifies the claim as satire and notes the Great Reset context but does not analyze the specific propagandistic architecture of the "warning satire" form, the role of April Fools' timing as a misinformation amplification mechanism, or the feedback loop between fabricated regulatory threats and real legislative responses against phantom bans.
