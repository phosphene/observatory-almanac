# Did USDA Predict Eggs Will Cost $12 Per Dozen in Fall 2022?

## §1 — Claim and Verdict Summary

In June 2022, a social media rumor began circulating that the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) had predicted egg prices would rise to twelve dollars per dozen by fall 2022. The claim spread through Twitter and related platforms in the context of widespread public attention to inflation, driven by post-pandemic supply chain disruptions, the Russian invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, and an avian influenza outbreak that was reducing the U.S. egg-laying hen population.

Snopes, in a fact-check published June 9, 2022 and authored by Dan Evon, rated the claim **False**. The USDA made no such prediction. A thorough search of the USDA's website, social media profiles, and official agricultural market reports produced no reference to any $12-per-dozen egg price forecast. In reality, the USDA's actual forecast — drawn from its Economic Research Service Food Price Outlook — projected egg prices could increase between 19.5% and 20.5% over 2022, which would bring the per-dozen price to approximately $2.50 to $3.00. The $12 figure alleged in the rumor was roughly four times larger than the agency's genuine forecast.

This case illustrates the distinctive misinformation pattern of fabricated government statistics deployed against a backdrop of genuine inflationary anxiety — where real concern about a real problem creates cognitive conditions that make exaggerated claims more persuasive.

## §2 — The Claim in Context: Real Inflation and the Egg Market in 2022

Understanding why this rumor found receptive audiences requires understanding the genuine inflation conditions in the U.S. egg market during this period.

Egg prices had genuinely risen substantially in the period preceding the June 2022 rumor. As reported by CBS News ("Why Egg Prices Are Soaring," *CBS Evening News*, 2022), the national average price for a dozen large white eggs had risen from approximately $1.60 per dozen in early 2021 to approximately $2.50 per dozen in spring 2022 — a roughly 56% increase. The CBS report attributed this increase to multiple interlocking pressures:

- **The Russia-Ukraine War**: Russia and Ukraine together are major global grain producers; the invasion that began in February 2022 disrupted wheat and corn markets. Since feed grain is a primary input cost in egg production, increased grain prices translated directly into increased egg production costs.
- **Drought conditions in the United States**: Domestic drought reduced U.S. grain yields, independently contributing to rising feed grain prices.
- **Avian Influenza Outbreak**: A severe outbreak of highly pathogenic avian influenza (H5N1) in 2021–2022 — described by USDA economists Matt MacLachlan and Shayle Shagam in a USDA radio segment (Gary Crawford, host, USDA Daily Radio Newsline, April 2022, https://www.usda.gov/media/radio/daily-newsline/2022-04-14/rising-egg-prices-getting-attention-consumers-and-economists) as "quite large" compared to the previous year — killed more than 20 million birds across 24 states. Livestock economist David Anderson noted in the CBS report that the 2022 outbreak was the worst avian flu event in seven years, directly reducing the egg-laying hen population and constraining supply.

In this context, egg prices were genuinely climbing, public awareness of egg-specific inflation was high, and there was a plausible narrative structure for claims about future price increases. Real conditions created what researchers call a "truth teller's advantage" for fabricated statistics — when a real underlying phenomenon (rising egg prices) is known to be occurring, false specific claims about its magnitude benefit from the background truthfulness of the general direction of change (Pennycook, Gordon, and David G. Rand, 2019, "The Implied Truth Effect: Attaching Warnings to a Subset of Fake News Stories Increases Perceived Accuracy of Stories Without Warnings," *Management Science*, vol. 65, no. 11, pp. 4944–4957).

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: Fabricated Government Statistics as an Inflation Anxiety Amplifier

The specific misinformation mechanism here is worth detailed examination: this is not a misinterpretation of real statistics but the invention of a fake authority citation. The rumor claims the USDA — a specific, named, authoritative federal institution — made a specific forecast. This is a more sophisticated and more harmful species of misinformation than a vague claim, because it borrows institutional credibility.

Fabricated government statistics in circulation exploit what epistemologists call the *authority heuristic* — the cognitive shortcut by which claims attributed to authoritative institutions are treated as more credible without independent verification (Cialdini, Robert B., 1984, *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion*, William Morrow). When a claim states "the USDA predicts X," target audiences who trust the USDA as an agricultural authority may not search for the primary source. When a claim states "prices will reach $12 per dozen" with USDA attribution, the institutional framing does additional verification work: it suggests the number has been calculated by professional economists using actual agricultural market data.

What makes the $12 figure particularly revealing is its magnitude. The USDA's genuine forecast was roughly $2.50–$3.00 per dozen — about 4× less than the $12 figure. This kind of dramatic overestimate is algorithmically optimized for engagement: a $3.00 per dozen egg price is a significant and newsworthy increase, but $12 per dozen is dramatically alarming in a way that a $3 estimate is not. The $12 figure plausibly (if incorrectly) suggests eggs would become an unaffordable luxury item for low-income households, rather than simply a somewhat more expensive staple — triggering a different (stronger) emotional response.

Research on the anatomy of politically viral misinformation consistently identifies emotional amplification as a core mechanism: claims that trigger fear, outrage, or disgust circulate more rapidly than factually accurate but less emotionally activating content (Brady, William J., Julian A. Wills, John T. Jost, Joshua A. Tucker, and Jay J. Van Bavel, 2017, "Emotion Shapes the Diffusion of Moralized Content in Social Networks," *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, vol. 114, no. 28, pp. 7313–7318). The $12 figure functions as an emotionally amplified version of the genuine forecast, calibrated to trigger fear responses rather than simply inform.

Dan Evon's framing in the Snopes headline — "We're not sure if this rumor originated with bad math or bad faith" — is itself an interesting epistemic admission. Bad math would mean someone genuinely misread or miscalculated from a real USDA data source. Bad faith would mean someone deliberately fabricated the figure. The investigation strongly suggests the latter, since no source document containing anything close to a $12 figure was found anywhere in USDA's actual output; but the honest uncertainty about origin is appropriate — tracing the genesis of a widely shared misinformation claim to a specific originating post is often impossible even when the claim is clearly false.

## §4 — Evidence Review: What the USDA Actually Said

The USDA's Economic Research Service (ERS) maintains a continuously updated *Food Price Outlook* data product that projects retail food price changes across multiple food categories. According to the ERS data current at the time of Snopes' investigation (USDA ERS, "Summary Findings," Food Price Outlook, https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/food-price-outlook/summary-findings/), eggs were projected to increase 19.5–20.5% in retail price over 2022.

This figure requires contextual grounding. Starting from a 2021 annual average of roughly $1.60 per dozen large white eggs, a 20% increase brings the forecast per-dozen price to approximately $1.92 at the low end of the range, to slightly above $2.00. However, since the $1.60 baseline was from early 2021 and by spring 2022 prices had already risen to approximately $2.50 per dozen, the ERS forecast effectively projected continued price levels of roughly $2.50–$3.00 over the remainder of the year. This is still a significant increase — food price economists quoted by CBS News characterized the observed egg price trajectory as extraordinary — but is qualitatively and quantitatively different from the $12 figure.

The USDA Agricultural Marketing Service also maintains detailed Egg Market News Reports (USDA AMS, https://www.ams.usda.gov/market-news/egg-market-news-reports), which provide granular data on egg prices at multiple market levels on a near-real-time basis. Snopes searched both this database and the USDA's social media accounts (Twitter at @USDA and its official Facebook page) and found no reference to any $12/dozen prediction.

USDA economists Matt MacLachlan and Shayle Shagam — the specific researchers who addressed egg prices in the April 2022 USDA radio segment — provided the most direct official characterization available: MacLachlan noted that the overall egg price increase from March 2021 to March 2022 was "quite large" by historical comparison, but at no point did either economist make predictions approaching the $12 figure alleged in the rumor.

## §5 — Broader Significance: Inflation Anxiety and the Misinformation Opportunity

The egg-price claim is one instance of a broader pattern that becomes particularly visible during inflationary periods: fabricated or dramatically exaggerated government statistics exploit genuine economic anxiety to generate viral engagement. The psychological mechanism is straightforward — if egg prices are already rising and causing real concern for household budgets, claims that prices will become even more dramatically unaffordable are both plausible-seeming (the direction is right) and emotionally compelling (the magnitude triggers stronger fear).

This pattern has historical precedents. During inflationary periods in the 1970s and 1980s, circulating predictions about energy and food prices routinely exceeded eventual outcomes. Research on economic anxiety and belief revision shows that people who are already anxious about a real economic phenomenon are less likely to engage in skeptical processing of related alarmist claims, a pattern consistent with existing research on motivated reasoning frameworks (Kunda, Ziva, 1990, "The Case for Motivated Reasoning," *Psychological Bulletin*, vol. 108, no. 3, pp. 480–498). When your eggs at the grocery store are visibly more expensive than last year, a claim that they will become dramatically more expensive still fits within the activated cognitive frame.

The misinformation is also structurally durable: even after fact-checks are published debunking the specific $12 figure, the broader narrative about egg price inflation remains accurate, and the emotional impression left by the $12 claim may persist even after cognitive correction. Research on the "continued influence effect" of misinformation — the tendency for corrected false beliefs to continue influencing reasoning even after conscious endorsement of the correction — suggests that highly arousing false claims are especially resistant to full correction (Johnson, Holly K., and C. Neil Macrae, 2012, "The Continued Influence Effect: Misinformation and How to Eliminate It," *PLoS ONE*, vol. 7, no. 9, e45894; and Johnson, Hollyn M., and Colleen M. Seifert, 1994, "Sources of the Continued Influence Effect: When Misinformation in Memory Affects Later Inferences," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, vol. 20, no. 6, pp. 1420–1436).

The EARC assignment for this entry is **R** (Refuted). The USDA made no prediction approaching $12 per dozen. The actual forecast was approximately $2.50–$3.00 per dozen, and the $12 figure has no traceable source in any genuine USDA data product.

## §6 — Conclusion and Epistemic Assessment

The claim that the USDA predicted egg prices would reach $12 per dozen by fall 2022 is **false**. Key findings:

1. Thorough searches of the USDA's website, ERS data products, AMS market reports, and official social media accounts found no reference to any $12/dozen egg price prediction.
2. The USDA's genuine forecast projected egg price increases of 19.5–20.5% for 2022, translating to a per-dozen price of approximately $2.50–$3.00.
3. The $12 figure alleged in the rumor was approximately four times larger than the agency's genuine forecast.
4. The rumor circulated during a period of genuine egg price inflation driven by the Russia-Ukraine war, domestic drought, and a severe avian influenza outbreak — real conditions that made exaggerated price claims more plausible-seeming.
5. The misinformation mechanism combines fabricated institutional attribution (false USDA source) with emotional amplification (alarming price figure) — a pattern characteristic of high-engagement inflation misinformation.

For the Truth Vault, this entry models the class of **fabricated government statistics** operating against a backdrop of genuine economic trends, and illustrates why genuine-underlying-trend conditions are not a defense against specific false claims — they are, in fact, precisely the conditions that make false specific claims most effective.
