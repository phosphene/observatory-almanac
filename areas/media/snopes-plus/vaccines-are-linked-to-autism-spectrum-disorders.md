# Vaccines are linked to autism spectrum disorders


## 1. The Claim

Few claims in modern public health misinformation have had more measurable consequences than the proposition that vaccines — particularly the MMR (measles, mumps, rubella) vaccine — cause or are linked to autism spectrum disorders. The claim emerged in its modern, scientifically couched form in 1998 and has persisted for nearly three decades, weathering an extensive evidentiary demolition, the formal retraction of its founding publication, and the professional destruction of its primary proponent. It continues today to circulate widely across social media platforms, wellness communities, vaccine-hesitant parenting networks, and anti-public-health media, contributing to measurably reduced vaccination rates and the resurgence of preventable diseases in populations that had previously eliminated them.

The specific claim varies in its formulation. The strongest version — championed by Andrew Wakefield, whose fraudulent research initiated the scare — holds that the MMR vaccine directly causes a regressive form of autism by triggering gastrointestinal pathology that in turn affects neurological development. A related but distinct version attributes autism risk to thimerosal, a mercury-containing preservative formerly used in some vaccines, arguing that mercury exposure at vaccine doses causes neurodevelopmental harm. A broader and vaguely stated version simply asserts that vaccines, as a class, are "linked to" autism without specifying mechanism — a claim structured to sound scientific and qualified while resisting specific refutation.

The context in which autism fears attach to vaccines is partly explicable by the timing of autism's typical clinical recognition. Autism spectrum disorder is most commonly identified between eighteen months and three years of age. This is also the period during which children receive several of their scheduled vaccinations, including MMR. The correlation in timing is real but reflects the developmental trajectory of autism's observable symptoms rather than any causal relationship with vaccination. Parents observing their child's autism diagnosis in the months following a vaccine dose have a subjectively compelling experience that feels like cause and effect, an experience that neither medical explanation nor statistical evidence easily overrides.

The claim has become a flagship of the broader anti-vaccination movement and is closely associated with a constellation of other vaccine-related beliefs including claims about toxic ingredient quantities, immune system overload, and government concealment of harm data. It is discussed in this entry on its own evidential merits rather than as a proxy for the broader vaccine debate.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The origin: a fraudulent study and its consequences.** The contemporary MMR-autism claim originates in a 1998 paper by Andrew Wakefield, John Walker-Smith, Simon Murch, and colleagues in *The Lancet*: Wakefield, A.J., Murch, S.H., Anthony, A., et al., 1998, "Ileal-lymphoid-nodular hyperplasia, non-specific colitis, and pervasive developmental disorder in children," *The Lancet*, 351, 637–641. The paper reported on twelve children with developmental disorders and gastrointestinal symptoms, suggesting a possible link between MMR vaccination and a novel syndrome. It was a small case series — not an epidemiological study — and even in 1998 it was accompanied by an editorial urging extreme caution about its interpretation.

The subsequent investigation of this paper constitutes one of the most thorough documentations of research fraud in modern biomedical history. Investigative journalist Brian Deer, working over several years, documented systematic manipulation of patient histories, undisclosed financial conflicts of interest (Wakefield had been paid by lawyers representing families seeking to sue vaccine manufacturers), and falsification of clinical and histopathological data. The General Medical Council in the United Kingdom conducted formal fitness-to-practice hearings that resulted in findings of serious professional misconduct against multiple authors. The *Lancet* fully retracted the paper in 2010. Wakefield was struck off the UK medical register. Deer, B., 2011, "How the case against the MMR vaccine was fixed," *British Medical Journal*, 342, c5347, provides the comprehensive documented account of the fraud.

**The epidemiological evidence: no association.** The scale of epidemiological research that followed the Wakefield publication is extraordinary. No scientific question in recent public health history has been examined with more resources, across more countries, and in more methodologically diverse ways. The findings are uniformly negative.

Taylor, B., Miller, E., Farrington, C.P., et al., 1999, "Autism and measles, mumps, and rubella vaccine: no epidemiological evidence for a causal association," *The Lancet*, 353, 2026–2029, examined records of 498 autism cases in the UK, finding no change in the pattern of autism diagnosis after MMR introduction and no clustering of cases around vaccination dates.

Madsen, K.M., Hviid, A., Vestergaard, M., et al., 2002, "A population-based study of measles, mumps, and rubella vaccination and autism," *New England Journal of Medicine*, 347, 1477–1482, is among the largest single studies on this question. It examined all 530,000 children born in Denmark between 1991 and 1998, comparing autism rates in vaccinated versus unvaccinated cohorts. The study found no increased risk of autism in vaccinated children and no dose-response relationship. The study's large sample size and national population coverage make it among the most statistically powerful investigations of the question.

Honda, H., Shimizu, Y., Rutter, M., 2005, "No effect of MMR withdrawal on the incidence of autism: a total population study," *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry*, 46, 572–579, provided a natural experiment by examining autism rates in Yokohama, Japan, following the withdrawal of the MMR vaccine in 1993. If MMR caused autism, removal of the vaccine should have reduced or stabilised autism diagnoses. Instead, autism diagnoses continued to rise after MMR withdrawal, providing evidence against a causal role.

Jain, A., Marshall, J., Buikema, A., et al., 2015, "Autism occurrence by MMR vaccine status among US children with older siblings with and without autism," *JAMA*, 313, 1534–1540, addressed the specific subpopulation that vaccine-autism advocates claimed was most at risk — children with autistic siblings who carry heightened genetic risk. Even in this high-risk group, vaccination status was not associated with autism risk.

Hviid, A., Hansen, J.V., Frisch, M., Melbye, M., 2019, "Measles, mumps, rubella vaccination and autism: a nationwide cohort study," *Annals of Internal Medicine*, 170, 513–520, followed 650,000 Danish children born between 1999 and 2010, again finding no association between MMR vaccination and autism.

**The thimerosal claim: also refuted.** The thimerosal variant of the vaccine-autism claim emerged partly as an attempt to maintain the broader association after the MMR-specific claims were weakened. Thimerosal contains ethylmercury, which prompted concerns about neurotoxicity by analogy with methylmercury, the form of mercury responsible for documented neurological damage in industrial poisoning cases like Minamata disease. However, ethylmercury and methylmercury have fundamentally different toxicokinetic profiles: ethylmercury is processed and excreted much more rapidly by the body and does not accumulate in neural tissue as methylmercury does.

Thimerosal was removed from routine childhood vaccines in the United States and Canada beginning in 1999 as a precautionary measure, providing a natural experiment similar to Japan's MMR withdrawal. If thimerosal caused autism, removal should have been followed by declining autism rates. Stehr-Green, P., Tull, P., Stellfeld, M., Mortenson, P.B., Simpson, D., 2003, "Autism and thimerosal-containing vaccines: lack of consistent evidence for an association," *American Journal of Preventive Medicine*, 25, 101–106, examined rates across multiple countries following thimerosal phase-out and found no reduction in autism diagnoses. Fombonne, E., Zakarian, R., Bennett, A., Meng, L., McLean-Heywood, D., 2006, "Pervasive developmental disorders in Montreal, Quebec, Canada: prevalence and links with immunizations," *Pediatrics*, 118, e139–e150, found continued increases in autism diagnosis rates after thimerosal removal in Quebec.

**Why autism diagnoses increased over the period.** A substantial proportion of the apparent increase in autism prevalence since the 1980s and 1990s is attributable to diagnostic expansion — broadening of diagnostic criteria in successive DSM editions, increased awareness among clinicians and parents, specialised diagnostic services, and advocacy-driven improvements in case-finding — rather than a true increase in the underlying condition. Lundström, S., Reichenberg, A., Anckarsäter, H., Lichtenstein, P., Gillberg, C., 2015, "Autism phenotype versus registered diagnosis in Swedish children: prevalence trends over 10 years in general population samples," *BMJ*, 350, h1961, examined Swedish population data and found that the rise in autism diagnoses was primarily explained by changes in diagnostic practice and increased help-seeking rather than biological incidence change.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The temporal coincidence problem and cognitive architecture.** The persistence of the vaccine-autism belief under conditions of overwhelming counter-evidence represents one of the most thoroughly studied cases of motivated reasoning in medical belief formation. The temporal coincidence between vaccination schedule and autism clinical recognition is real — in the sense that both typically occur in the second year of life — and is processed by cognitive systems that default to causal inference from temporal contiguity. This causal attribution from co-occurrence is not irrational as a heuristic under ordinary circumstances; most of the time, events that reliably precede other events are informative about causation. What makes it systematically misleading here is that the prior probability of autism in the developmental cohort is independent of vaccination status, a fact that is invisible to any individual parent observing their own child.

The epistemic structure of this situation is unusual: parents who experience their child receiving a vaccine and subsequently developing observable autistic symptoms have a subjectively authentic evidential experience that points in the wrong direction. No amount of population statistics easily overrides a directly witnessed sequence of events. Redelmeier, D.A., Tversky, A., 1996, "On the belief that arthritis pain is related to the weather," *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 93, 2895–2896, examined the structural features of this cognition under analogous belief conditions, finding that vivid personal experience of correlation consistently overrides statistical instruction.

**The Wakefield fraud as founding narrative.** The 1998 *Lancet* paper provided institutional scaffolding — the appearance of a peer-reviewed publication with named researchers, a named journal with genuine prestige, and a described mechanism — that legitimised parental concerns and provided them with a source to cite. The retraction removed the institutional scaffolding but not the belief, because the belief had by then been emotionally and reputationally invested in by a large community of parents and advocates who had organised social identities around it. Research in belief perseverance demonstrates that beliefs formed with institutional backing persist after the backing is removed, particularly when the beliefs are integrated into social group membership and personal narrative (Nyhan, B., Reifler, J., 2010, "When corrections fail: the persistence of political misperceptions," *Political Behavior*, 32, 303–330).

**Identity, community, and information environment.** The vaccine-autism claim has become a foundational belief of a distinctive social community — vaccine-hesitant and anti-vaccination parent groups — that provides emotional support, shared narrative, and community identity for parents navigating difficult autism diagnoses. The community provides an explanation (vaccines caused this), a villain (pharmaceutical industry and complicit regulators), and solidarity with other parents experiencing similar situations. The social functions served by this community are real and important; the claim that anchors it is false. This dynamic means that factual correction encounters not only individual cognition but community-level belief defence: people who leave the belief risk social exclusion from the community built around it, creating powerful structural incentives to resist correction.

**Distrust of pharmaceutical industry.** Much of the resilience of vaccine-autism belief is better understood as distrust of the pharmaceutical industry than as a sincere epistemic commitment to any particular causal hypothesis. The pharmaceutical industry has a documented history of suppressing unfavourable research, engaging in unethical promotional practices, and prioritising commercial interests over patient welfare in ways that have produced genuine public harm. This record creates a rational basis for scepticism of industry-sponsored research and industry-endorsed regulatory findings. The vaccine-autism case exploits this rational underlying scepticism: by framing the counter-evidence as industry-funded and the Wakefield research as courageous independent science, advocates reconfigure the evidentiary landscape as one of industry cover-up rather than scientific consensus. The irony — that Wakefield was actually paid by lawyers seeking to sue vaccine manufacturers, creating a financial conflict directly opposite to the "independent researcher vs. industry" framing — is not widely known.


## 4. Verdict

**False — the claim originates in documented fraud and is refuted by extensive independent evidence.** The claim that vaccines are linked to autism spectrum disorders is not supported by any credible body of scientific evidence. The founding study has been retracted as fraudulent. Multiple independent large-scale epidemiological investigations across multiple countries, involving millions of children and employing diverse methodological designs, have found no association between MMR vaccination, thimerosal-containing vaccines, or any other vaccine component and autism spectrum disorder. The claim is not a matter of ongoing scientific debate; it is a settled question on which the evidence is as clear as it is on any question in epidemiology.

The harm caused by perpetuation of this false belief is concrete and measurable. Vaccination rates in populations exposed to intense anti-vaccine messaging declined significantly following the 1998 publication and subsequent media amplification. Measles outbreaks in the UK, US, and multiple European countries in the 2000s and 2010s — in populations with previously high vaccination rates — were directly linked to reduced vaccine uptake in communities with high rates of vaccine-autism belief. Wakefield's fraud has contributed to preventable deaths.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The vaccine-autism claim is the most studied case of misinformation perpetuation in public health, and its study has generated substantial insights into the general conditions under which false health beliefs resist correction.

The case illustrates the asymmetry in how scientific consensus forms versus how false beliefs form. Scientific consensus on the lack of vaccine-autism link was established through rigorous, costly, multi-decade epidemiological work across multiple independent research groups. It required the assembly of population-scale datasets, multi-variable statistical analysis, and natural experiments. The original false belief required one small fraudulent case series, a press conference, and a media environment that treated scientific controversy as equivalent in epistemic authority to scientific consensus. The effort required to establish truth in this domain was orders of magnitude greater than the effort required to plant the original false belief, and truth still has not fully displaced falsity in public discourse.

The Institute of Medicine (now National Academy of Medicine, 2004) conducted a comprehensive review of the vaccine-autism evidence base that is among the most thorough assessments of any medical question in the modern era. Stratton, K., Gable, A., Shetty, P., McCormick, M. (eds.), 2004, *Immunization Safety Review: Vaccines and Autism*, National Academies Press. The review concluded that the evidence favoured rejection of the causal relationship between MMR and autism and between thimerosal-containing vaccines and autism. The review was explicitly designed to be independent of the pharmaceutical industry and the regulatory bodies that it examined.

The ongoing measles resurgences in wealthy nations with functional healthcare systems represent perhaps the most visible public health cost of a single misinformation event. The 2018–2019 measles outbreak in the United States involved more than 1,200 confirmed cases, the highest annual total since 1992, concentrated in communities with low vaccination rates. The WHO identified "vaccine hesitancy" — of which the autism claim is a principal driver — as one of the ten greatest threats to global health in 2019. The direct causal chain from one fraudulent paper to these public health outcomes has been documented across multiple independent analyses.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The vaccines-autism claim is among the most thoroughly fact-checked claims in the contemporary information environment. Snopes, PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, the BBC's Reality Check, AFP Fact Check, and virtually every major fact-checking organisation in the English-speaking world has addressed it in one or more of its forms, consistently rating it false.

The Snopes treatment focuses on the Wakefield fraud and the subsequent epidemiological record. PolitiFact and FactCheck.org have addressed the claim in response to specific public figures including politicians and media personalities who have given it credence. Additional fact-checking has occurred in response to documentary film promotion (*Vaxxed*, produced by Wakefield himself, which was withdrawn from the Tribeca Film Festival in 2016 following protests by the scientific community) and periodic viral social media circulation.

The challenge for fact-checkers in this domain is that repeated direct correction does not reliably reduce the prevalence of the belief and in some studies produces "backfire effects" in highly motivated believers — a phenomenon subsequently disputed in the methodological literature but whose underlying concern about motivated resistance to correction remains valid. Nyhan, B., Reifler, J., Richey, S., Freed, G.L., 2014, "Effective messages in vaccine promotion: a randomized trial," *Pediatrics*, 133, e835–e842, found that correcting vaccine-autism myths increased the proportion of parents reporting concern about vaccines among the most concerned subgroup — a finding that complicates straightforward factual correction strategies.

Contemporary fact-checking guidance for this claim emphasises: (1) leading with empathy for parents whose experiences feel like genuine evidence; (2) explaining the timing coincidence mechanism without dismissing parents' observations; (3) presenting the scale of the counter-evidence rather than relying on single studies; (4) addressing the Wakefield fraud directly as context; and (5) focusing on positive messaging about vaccine benefits rather than primarily on refutation. The media literacy meta-lesson is that factual accuracy is necessary but not sufficient to change health behaviour when beliefs are embedded in community identity, parental anxiety, and distrust of institutions.
