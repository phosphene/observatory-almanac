# Vaccines Cause Autism

## 1. The Claim

The claim, as it circulates most commonly today, goes something like this: childhood vaccines — particularly the MMR (measles, mumps, rubella) vaccine — trigger autism spectrum disorder in children who receive them. In its stronger forms, the claim is extended to thimerosal, a mercury-based preservative once used in some vaccines, as the specific mechanism. In its most extreme expression, parents are told that vaccination programs represent a deliberate cover-up of a public health catastrophe, with pharmaceutical companies and regulatory bodies suppressing the evidence.

This claim has been one of the most consequential medical myths of the past three decades. It contributed to declining vaccination rates in the United Kingdom, the United States, and elsewhere throughout the late 1990s and 2000s. Measles — a disease declared eliminated in the US in 2000 — returned in outbreaks directly linked to communities with low vaccination rates. The World Health Organization listed vaccine hesitancy as one of the ten threats to global health in 2019.

Understanding where this claim came from, why it was wrong, and why it remains so persistent is not merely a matter of correcting a false belief. It is a case study in how bad science, media amplification, and motivated reasoning can produce lasting public harm.

## 2. What's Actually True

### The Wakefield Study: What It Said and What It Was

The origin point of the modern vaccines-autism claim is a paper published in *The Lancet* in February 1998, authored by Andrew Wakefield and twelve colleagues. The study examined twelve children with gastrointestinal symptoms and developmental disorders, and suggested a possible link between the MMR vaccine, a novel bowel disease, and autism. The paper was careful in its formal language — Wakefield used phrases like "possible link" and "further investigation is needed" — but at a press conference coinciding with publication, Wakefield publicly recommended switching from the combined MMR vaccine to separate single vaccines, suggesting the combined formulation was unsafe.

The study was small (twelve children), observational, and without a control group. It was not, by any serious methodological standard, evidence of causation. The appropriate response from the broader scientific community was: note it as preliminary, attempt replication, and await larger studies. What happened instead was a media firestorm. The claim that MMR caused autism was broadcast across British tabloids and television news with a urgency that the underlying science in no way warranted.

### The Fraud

In 2004, investigative journalist Brian Deer began publishing findings that would ultimately expose Wakefield's study as not merely wrong but deliberately fraudulent. Deer found that Wakefield had:

- **Manipulated data**: The medical records of the twelve children in the study did not match the case descriptions published in *The Lancet*. Developmental symptoms that Wakefield claimed appeared after vaccination in fact preceded it in some children.
- **Concealed a financial conflict of interest**: Wakefield had been paid £435,643 (plus expenses) by lawyers preparing a lawsuit against MMR manufacturers. He had begun accepting this money before he enrolled the children in his study — and did not disclose it.
- **Conducted unethical research**: Wakefield had subjected the children to invasive procedures — colonoscopies, lumbar punctures, barium enemas — that were not clinically indicated and had not received proper ethical clearance. Several children were recruited through the anti-MMR campaign, not through standard medical referral.
- **Held a patent**: Wakefield had applied for a patent for a rival single-measles vaccine before his study was published. He had a direct commercial interest in discrediting the combined MMR formulation.

Ten of the thirteen co-authors retracted their names from the paper's interpretation section in 2004. *The Lancet* retracted the full paper in February 2010. The UK General Medical Council, after a lengthy investigation, found Wakefield guilty of serious professional misconduct. He was struck off the medical register — meaning he could no longer practice medicine in the United Kingdom — in May 2010.

The study was not merely flawed. It was fabricated.

### The Research That Followed

In the years between 1998 and 2010, and in the years since, researchers around the world conducted large-scale studies to test whether any version of the vaccines-autism hypothesis could be supported. The volume and consistency of this research is worth dwelling on.

A 2002 Danish cohort study followed 537,303 children born between 1991 and 1998, examining MMR vaccination and autism diagnosis. It found no difference in autism rates between vaccinated and unvaccinated children.

A 2005 Japanese study examined autism rates after Japan discontinued the combined MMR vaccine in 1993 — switching to monovalent vaccines — and found that autism diagnoses continued to rise after MMR withdrawal, contradicting the hypothesis that MMR was driving the increase.

A 2019 Danish cohort study — one of the largest yet conducted — followed 650,000 Danish children born between 1999 and 2010, with follow-up through 2013. It found no increased risk of autism in vaccinated children, no clustering of autism cases in the period following vaccination, and no association between vaccination and autism in children who were considered "at risk" by various indicators.

A 2014 meta-analysis published in *Vaccine* examined data from over 1.2 million children across five cohort studies and five case-control studies. It found no association between vaccination and autism, or between the MMR vaccine and autism specifically.

Research into thimerosal — the mercury-containing preservative proposed as an alternative mechanism — has been similarly unambiguous. Thimerosal was removed from childhood vaccines in the US between 1999 and 2001 (it was never present in the MMR vaccine, which does not use preservatives). If thimerosal were causing autism, removal would be expected to produce declining autism rates. Instead, autism diagnoses continued rising after thimerosal removal, as they had before. Large epidemiological studies in the US, Denmark, and Sweden comparing children who received thimerosal-containing vaccines with those who received thimerosal-free versions found no association with autism.

### Why Autism Appears Near the Time of Vaccination

The timing coincidence is real and requires explanation. Children receive the MMR vaccine at twelve to fifteen months of age. Autism spectrum disorder is typically diagnosed between eighteen months and three years of age — when developmental differences become apparent to parents and clinicians. These two windows overlap. A child who shows signs of autism around the time they receive their MMR vaccine will, by the nature of the statistical coincidence, appear to "develop autism after vaccination" even when the vaccine played no causal role.

This is not a cover-up. It is a statistical property of when in a child's development both events occur. Correlation in timing is not causation. The biological development that underlies autism begins well before any vaccine is administered.

## 3. Why People Believe This

### The Wakefield Effect

The vaccines-autism claim achieved initial credibility because it appeared in a peer-reviewed journal and was championed by a credentialed physician. Media coverage in the UK — particularly in the tabloid press and on television — amplified the claim without the skepticism that small, preliminary, uncontrolled studies warrant. By the time the fraud was exposed in 2010, the belief had embedded itself in public consciousness across multiple countries.

### Narrative Power of the Parent Testimony

Vaccine hesitancy around autism is sustained, in large part, by the testimony of parents who believe — sincerely and with great emotional conviction — that their child was normal before vaccination and changed after it. These testimonies are not fabricated. The parents are reporting what they observed. But observation is not causal analysis. A child who receives a vaccine at fifteen months and shows the first obvious signs of developmental divergence at eighteen months presents a compelling personal narrative of cause and effect that is statistically unremarkable given what we know about autism's emergence.

The human brain is wired to construct causal narratives from sequential events. "After this, therefore because of this" — the post hoc ergo propter hoc fallacy — is not a failure of intelligence. It is a default mode of reasoning that served our ancestors well in environments where sequential events often were causally related. In epidemiology, however, it is systematically misleading.

### Distrust of Institutions

The persistence of the claim is inseparable from declining trust in pharmaceutical companies, regulatory agencies, and medical establishments. When people distrust the institutions producing and evaluating vaccines, the existence of large-scale studies showing no link does not reassure — it confirms the suspicion that the evidence is being managed. Fraud by Wakefield strengthens this distrust paradoxically: if one researcher falsified data, perhaps the researchers refuting him are doing the same.

This epistemic situation is difficult. Institutional trust cannot be rebuilt simply by producing more evidence, because the production of evidence by the same distrusted institutions is itself taken as suspect.

### Social and Community Reinforcement

Once vaccine hesitancy becomes embedded in a social network or community, it is sustained by identity and belonging as much as by any factual belief. Questioning vaccines becomes a way of demonstrating that one is a critical thinker, a good parent, a member of a community that sees through official narratives. Changing one's mind requires not just updating a belief but, in some social contexts, defecting from a group.

## 4. Verdict

The claim that vaccines cause autism is false. It originated from a fraudulent study that was retracted in full, whose author was struck off the medical register for professional misconduct. The hypothesis has been tested in studies involving more than ten million children across multiple countries and multiple methodological approaches. No causal link has been found.

The timing coincidence — autism diagnosis and vaccination both occurring in the second year of life — is real and explains the intuitive plausibility of the claim. It does not constitute evidence for causation.

This claim falls in the EARC **E** (Epistemologically Loaded) category because its persistence is not primarily a matter of ignorance. People who hold this belief have usually encountered some version of the evidence. The claim persists because it is sustained by distrust of institutions, by powerful parental narratives, by community identity, and by the fundamental cognitive difficulty of distinguishing correlation from causation in individual cases.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### What Wakefield's Fraud Cost

The cost of the vaccines-autism myth is measurable. Measles vaccination rates in the UK fell from 92% in 1995 to 79% in 2003. Measles outbreaks followed. Thousands of children who were not vaccinated contracted diseases that vaccination would have prevented. Some were hospitalized. Measles can cause encephalitis, permanent deafness, and death.

In the United States, measles cases — which had declined to a few dozen per year following declared elimination in 2000 — surged in years marked by vaccine hesitancy. The 2019 outbreak, concentrated in communities with low vaccination rates, saw 1,282 confirmed cases — the highest count since 1992.

The claim also diverted enormous resources. Governments, health agencies, and researchers spent years investigating a hypothesis that had no credible scientific basis, resources that could have been directed toward understanding autism's actual etiology.

### Understanding Autism's Real Etiology

Autism spectrum disorder is not a single condition with a single cause. It is a spectrum of developmental differences characterized by variation in social communication, sensory processing, and repetitive behaviors. Its etiology is complex and still being understood.

Genetic factors play a substantial role. Twin studies show high heritability — estimates range from 64% to 91% depending on methodology. Hundreds of genetic variants have been associated with increased autism risk. Advanced parental age (both maternal and paternal) is a risk factor. Prenatal exposure to certain environmental factors — including some medications and inflammatory processes during pregnancy — has been investigated.

The biological processes underlying autism begin in utero, before any vaccine is administered. The claim that a vaccine administered at twelve to fifteen months triggers the condition requires a mechanism that has never been identified, tested, or replicated — because the condition's developmental roots lie earlier in life.

### The Epistemic Lesson

The vaccines-autism case is a canonical example of how scientific fraud, media amplification, and motivated reasoning interact. A fraudulent paper introduced a false hypothesis into public discourse. Media amplification without methodological scrutiny spread it. Retraction and refutation, arriving years later and lacking the drama of the original claim, could not fully retrieve the narrative. The belief persists in communities where institutional trust has been eroded, where personal testimony is weighted above epidemiological evidence, and where identity has become entwined with the belief itself.

No volume of evidence can resolve an epistemic crisis rooted in distrust. What can help is rebuilding institutional credibility through transparency — including transparency about failures like Wakefield — and creating conditions in which parents' concerns about their children's health are taken seriously within a framework that also values scientific rigor.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Fact-checkers universally rate the vaccines-cause-autism claim as false. Snopes, PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, and the AFP all evaluate it as debunked misinformation. The claim is rated False, Pants on Fire, or equivalent depending on the platform.

Standard fact-checker treatment includes: citing the Wakefield retraction, noting the GMC ruling, citing large-scale epidemiological studies, and explaining the timing coincidence. Most fact-checkers now encounter the claim in specific forms — a celebrity endorsing it, a politician citing it, a meme reformulating it — and address the specific claim while linking to the broader debunking record.

**What fact-checkers do well:** They are consistent, they cite primary sources, and they provide clear verdicts. They have created a large body of accessible reference material.

**Where they struggle:** The claim is epistemologically loaded — the people who hold it often reject the institutions whose evidence fact-checkers cite. A standard fact-check saying "the MMR vaccine does not cause autism, per the CDC and WHO" is precisely the kind of institutional citation that fails to move a reader who distrusts those institutions. This is not a failure of the fact-checker; it is the nature of the epistemic problem. The belief is not sustained by ignorance of the debunking — most adherents have encountered the debunking — but by distrust of the sources doing the debunking.

The deeper challenge for fact-checkers is that corrections to a high-salience misinformation claim rarely reduce belief in the claim among those who already hold it, and may in some experimental conditions reinforce it through the backfire effect (though subsequent research has complicated the original backfire findings). The landscape for vaccine misinformation specifically is one where the falsity of the claim is well-established, but the epistemic conditions for correction are challenging.
