## The Claim

In October 2020, at the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, news outlets globally picked up a scientific study showing that sick vampire bats appear to isolate themselves from their colony — a behavior that journalists and commentators rapidly described as a form of "social distancing." The framing was irresistible in context: as human societies grappled with the psychological and social costs of pandemic-era distancing measures, a study suggesting that non-human animals had evolved analogous behavior seemed both timely and validating.

The claim attached to the study ranged in its specificity. In its strongest form, the assertion was that vampire bats deliberately and consciously practice social distancing when sick, analogous to the intentional human public health measure. In more moderate form, it simply stated that sick vampire bats naturally keep distance from colony members, implying tidy symmetry with human pandemic behaviour without making strong claims about intentionality. In both framings, the word "practice" was frequently used — carrying implied agency and deliberateness.

Snopes fact-checker Madison Dapcevich published a review of this claim on October 28, 2020, rating it "Mixture." The study behind the claim was real, methodologically sound, and its core empirical findings were genuinely interesting. But the specific language of "social distancing" imported excess connotations that the researchers themselves cautioned against, and the mechanism underlying the observed behaviour is importantly different from the intentional human equivalent.

The fate of this finding in popular media is an instructive study in how scientifically legitimate research gets flattened during high-salience news environments — particularly when a finding is susceptible to emotionally satisfying but technically imprecise analogical framing.

## What's Actually True

The study at the centre of the claim is Ripperger, S., Stockmaier, S., and Carter, G.G. (2020), "Sickness-Associated Changes in the Social Network of a Group-Living Bat," *Behavioral Ecology*, Vol. 31, No. 6, pp. 1296–1305. The research was conducted at a bat roost in a hollow tree in Lamanai, Belize, using a methodologically innovative approach to tracking fine-grained social contact in wild animals.

The researchers captured 32 wild female vampire bats (*Desmodus rotundus*) from the roost and injected each with lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a molecular component of bacterial cell walls that activates the mammalian immune system and induces a sickness response — including lethargy, increased sleep, reduced appetite, and elevated temperature — without actually introducing a pathogen. This is a standard experimental protocol in immunology for studying sickness behaviour; LPS is sometimes called an "immune challenge." An additional 15 bats from the same colony received saline placebo injections as controls.

Each bat was equipped with a small sensor — described as the size of a penny, worn as a backpack — that logged proximity to other sensors every few seconds, allowing researchers to reconstruct the social network of the colony in high temporal resolution. Over a six-hour observation window, the sickness-induced bats interacted with an average of four fewer individual colony members than control bats. They spent less total time in close proximity to other bats. Their network centrality — their position in the overall social connectivity structure of the colony — was also reduced. The sick bats were less active, slept more, and were overall less engaged in the constant grooming, food-sharing, and affiliative contact that characterises vampire bat colony life under normal conditions.

These findings are real and statistically robust. Vampire bats are highly social mammals with complex, persistent social bonds; they engage in reciprocal food sharing (regurgitating blood to hungry colony members), grooming, and cooperative care behaviours that have been extensively documented in studies by Gerald Carter, Gerry Wilkinson, and colleagues going back decades. The sickness-induced reduction in social contact was a meaningful change to this baseline.

However — and this is the "false" component of the Mixture rating — the researchers explicitly stated in the published paper that this social distancing behaviour was "unintentional" and "sickness-induced." The mechanism is not that sick bats calculate the disease transmission risk to colony members and choose to limit contact. The mechanism is that sickness makes the bats lethargic and sleep more, and a sleeping bat is simply not engaging in social contact. The reduced contact is a downstream consequence of sickness-induced behavioural changes (the well-documented "sickness behaviour" syndrome described in the literature), not a targeted prophylactic strategy.

This distinction matters ethologically and medically. Intentional human social distancing involves a population of healthy individuals choosing to forgo contact in order to prevent transmission of a pathogen — a behaviour that causes real cost (social isolation, economic disruption, psychological harm) in exchange for a population-level benefit. Vampire bat "social distancing" involves sick individuals becoming less active due to immune activation, with the disease-limiting effect being an epidemiological consequence rather than a goal. The evolutionary pressure is not on bats to be consciously altruistic toward colony members; it is on the immune response itself to produce a behavioural state that incidentally limits transmission.

As Ripperger stated in the Ohio State University press release: "Social distancing during the COVID-19 pandemic, when we feel fine, doesn't feel particularly normal. But when we're sick, it's common to withdraw a bit and stay in bed longer because we're exhausted. And that means we're likely to have fewer social encounters. That's the same thing we were observing in this study."

The researchers also noted several limitations: the disease spread itself was not documented (only proximity changes were measured); the "illness" was artificially induced rather than naturally acquired; and different pathogens might produce different, or even opposite, behavioural effects (some infections can increase social contact, which would benefit the pathogen rather than the host).

## Epistemic Novelty

The vampire bat social distancing study sits at an intersection of two important areas of inquiry: the behavioural ecology of disease within social groups, and the epistemology of how scientific findings travel through media systems under conditions of high public salience.

On the scientific side, the study contributes to a growing literature on *social immunity* — the collective behaviours of social animals that function to limit pathogen spread, whether through individual sickness behaviour, active exclusion of sick individuals by healthy colony members, or prophylactic spatial restructuring. This field has documented diverse mechanisms across species. Moribund ants of several species leave their nest colony to die in isolation, as documented by Müller and Schmid-Hempel (1993, "Exploitation of Cold Temperature as Defence against Parasites in Bumblebees," *Nature*, Vol. 363, pp. 65–67) for related thermoregulatory defence mechanisms, and in more direct isolation contexts by Lihoreau et al. In honey bees, colony members actively eject parasitised individuals — a form of social immunity imposed by the group rather than elected by the individual (Richard et al., 2008, "Honey Bee Excludes Diseased Nestmates from Hive," *Current Biology*). Harpy ants have been documented engaging in active wound care and antimicrobial treatment of injured nestmates (Dossey, 2010, *Natural Product Reports*). The Ripperger et al. study adds vampire bats to this map of social immunity mechanisms, with the specific finding that the mechanism operates through the individual-level sickness behaviour cascade rather than through active exclusion or deliberate prophylaxis.

The distinction between *individual adaptive sickness behaviour* and *socially aware distancing* is theoretically important for disease ecology modelling. Mathematical models of disease spread through social networks — including foundational work by Anderson and May (1991, *Infectious Diseases of Humans: Dynamics and Control*, Oxford University Press) and subsequent network epidemiology developed by researchers like Newman (2002, "Spread of Epidemic Disease on Networks," *Physical Review E*, Vol. 66) — treat individual behaviour changes as parameters that affect R0 (the basic reproduction number). Whether the behaviour change is intentional or incidental is irrelevant to the epidemiological mathematics; what matters is the magnitude and pattern of contact reduction. But attributing intentionality where it does not exist creates a misleading picture of what evolutionary pressures shaped the behaviour, and thus what we should infer about similar behaviours in other contexts.

The epistemic novelty offered here is a framework for understanding how *mechanism anthropomorphism* operates in science journalism. When a study finds that an animal's behaviour reduces disease spread, the journalist's immediate interpretive frame is drawn from the most salient human context for that behaviour. In October 2020, the most salient human context for disease-protective behaviour reduction was COVID-19 social distancing. The frame was applied with enthusiasm because it was emotionally resonant and because it generated a warm, satisfying narrative: even vampire bats know to practice social distancing. This narrative flatters both the reading public's sense of solidarity with other species and their validation of public health measures.

But mechanism anthropomorphism generates downstream errors. Reporting that bats "practice social distancing" implies a degree of prosocial intentionality that the evidence does not support — and that, if generalised, would suggest that bats have some form of infectious disease awareness and community responsibility. They do not, or at minimum the evidence does not establish this. The behaviour is better described as: "sick bats become less active due to immune activation, which incidentally reduces their social contact and the rate of pathogen transmission within the colony." This description is accurate but narratively flat. The "social distancing" framing is narratively engaging but mechanistically misleading.

Sickness behaviour itself — the cluster of symptoms including lethargy, reduced appetite, increased sleep, and reduced social engagement that accompanies acute infection — is a highly conserved evolutionary adaptation studied extensively in behavioural immunology. Hart (1988, "Biological Basis of Behavior of Sick Animals," *Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews*, Vol. 12, pp. 123–137) provided the foundational account of sickness behaviour as an active, organised response of the nervous and immune systems to infection, rather than simply a passive consequence of energetic depletion. From this perspective, the vampire bat's reduced social engagement during immune activation is better understood as one component of a broader sickness behaviour program — alongside the fever, lethargy, and anorexia — rather than as a standalone decision about social contact.

## The Evidence Base

**Ripperger, S., Stockmaier, S., and Carter, G.G. (2020). "Sickness-Associated Changes in the Social Network of a Group-Living Bat." *Behavioral Ecology*, Vol. 31, No. 6, pp. 1296–1305.** The primary study. Uses LPS-induced immune challenge in wild *Desmodus rotundus* bats with proximity logger backpacks to document social network changes under sickness conditions. Key finding: sick bats associated with an average of four fewer individuals per six-hour observation window compared to controls.

**Hart, B.L. (1988). "Biological Basis of Behavior of Sick Animals." *Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews*, Vol. 12, pp. 123–137.** The foundational paper on sickness behaviour as an organised, adaptive response of the nervous and immune systems, establishing the theoretical framework for interpreting the bat study's mechanism. Hart argued that sickness behaviour is not a passive by-product of infection but an active adaptive strategy that conserves metabolic resources for immune function.

**Anderson, R.M., and May, R.M. (1991). *Infectious Diseases of Humans: Dynamics and Control*. Oxford University Press.** The foundational text on mathematical epidemiology, providing the modelling framework within which individual behavioural changes (intentional or not) are analysed as parameters affecting disease transmission rates.

**Newman, M.E.J. (2002). "Spread of Epidemic Disease on Networks." *Physical Review E*, Vol. 66, Article 016128.** Network epidemiology paper demonstrating how contact network structure shapes disease dynamics, providing mathematical context for why changes in an individual bat's social connectivity affect colony-level transmission rates.

**Dapcevich, M., Snopes.com, "Do Vampire Bats Practice 'Social Distancing' When Feeling Ill?", October 28, 2020.** The primary fact-check review, including the Mixture rating and the researcher quote from Ripperger distinguishing the bat behaviour from intentional human social distancing.

**Ohio State University Press Release, "For Vampire Bats, Social Distancing While Sick Comes Naturally," October 27, 2020.** The institutional press release from Ohio State (where Carter held his faculty position) that served as the primary conduit between the journal publication and media coverage. The release included Ripperger's clarifying quote.

**Carter, G.G., and Wilkinson, G.S. (2013). "Food Sharing in Vampire Bats: Reciprocal Help Predicts Donations More Than Relatedness or Harassment." *Proceedings of the Royal Society B: Biological Sciences*, Vol. 280, No. 1753.** Essential background on the prosocial complexity of vampire bat colonies, establishing the baseline behaviour — extensive food sharing, grooming, and affiliative contact — that the sickness study measured deviation from.

**Müller, U., and Schmid-Hempel, P. (1993). "Exploitation of Cold Temperature as Defence against Parasites in Bumblebees." *Nature*, Vol. 363, pp. 65–67.** Provides comparative context for thermoregulatory immune defence in social insects, relevant to the broader social immunity literature within which the bat finding should be situated.

## Why People Believe This

The specific framing of this claim — "vampire bats practice social distancing" — was not produced by misinformation actors or conspiracy theorists. It emerged from a legitimate science communication pipeline: a real study, with a real finding, generating a real press release, covered by legitimate science journalists. The "mixture" problem here is not a story of someone lying; it is a story of how accurate information loses its qualifying structure as it moves through communication channels optimised for brevity and emotional engagement.

The October 2020 context was saturating. The COVID-19 pandemic was eight months old and societies across the world were in varying stages of mask mandates, lockdowns, and social distancing policies. Public compliance with distancing measures was correlated with attitudes toward the pandemic's seriousness, and there was strong political valence attached to whether one believed social distancing was necessary, effective, or legitimate. In this environment, a finding that "even vampire bats do it" served a specific rhetorical function: it naturalised social distancing by pointing to evolutionary precedents. The study was received and amplified partly as a scientific curiosity and partly as a legitimating piece of evidence for public health behaviour.

That context also explains why the qualifier — "it's unintentional, caused by lethargy" — was dropped in most popular coverage. The qualifier is a narrative deflation. "Bats socially distance when sick" is a complete, satisfying sentence with an implied moral. "Bats become lethargic when immune-challenged, and because they sleep more they interact with fewer colony members, and this incidentally reduces pathogen spread" is scientifically accurate but leaves no room for the emotional payoff. Editors and headline writers routinely truncate in the direction of the satisfying over the accurate.

The species itself helped. Vampire bats have a powerful cultural profile as creatures of dread — bloodsucking, nocturnal, associated with darkness and disease. A story about vampire bats willingly isolating themselves to protect their community inverts this mythology: the terrifying creature is actually prosocial and health-conscious. The narrative inversion is emotionally engaging in its own right and generates the sharing impulse that drives viral content.

## The Verdict

The claim that sick vampire bats socially distance themselves from the colony is a **mixture**: the empirical core is true, and the mechanism is importantly different from intentional human social distancing.

**True:** A 2020 study published in *Behavioral Ecology* (Ripperger, Stockmaier, and Carter) found that vampire bats experimentally induced with an immune challenge (LPS) interacted significantly less with colony members compared to control bats — associating with an average of four fewer individuals per six-hour window, spending less total time in proximity, and having reduced network centrality.

**False by omission/implication:** The "social distancing" framing implies deliberate, conscious disease-prevention strategy analogous to human public health behaviour. The researchers explicitly characterised the behaviour as "unintentional" sickness behaviour — a downstream consequence of immune-mediated lethargy rather than a prosocial choice. The mechanism is the same as humans "staying in bed" when sick, which incidentally reduces contact, rather than humans making a deliberate decision to limit transmission for community benefit.

The distinction is scientifically meaningful for understanding evolutionary pressures on sickness behaviour, practically meaningful for disease modelling (intentional versus automatic behaviour have different dose-response curves), and epistemically meaningful for the broader question of how far we can accurately project human public health concepts onto animal behaviour systems.
