# Is a video of elephant throwing grass on its head real?


## 1. The Claim

A video that circulated extensively across social media platforms from November 2025 onward showed an elephant — already wearing some loose grass on its head — using its trunk to scoop up additional clumps of grass and toss them onto its own head, creating an increasingly elaborate and comically large grassy wig. The footage spread across X, Instagram, Facebook, Reddit, Threads, TikTok, and YouTube, accumulating millions of views. On November 24, 2025, X user @Yoda4ever posted the clip with the caption "Look, what I can do" alongside an elephant emoji. The video continued circulating into early 2026.

From the beginning, significant portions of the viewing audience questioned whether the video was real. The r/isthisAI subreddit hosted an extended discussion in which the post title stated: "Reddit seems divided on this one. Not quite sure myself but leaning towards AI." The division among viewers reflected a now-familiar dynamic: in a media landscape saturated with AI-generated animal footage, genuinely unusual or endearing animal behaviour is routinely suspected of fabrication. The deliberateness of the elephant's trunk movements — repeatedly, precisely gathering grass and placing it on its head — struck some viewers as too purposeful, too smooth, or too visually striking to be real.

The central question for fact-checkers was twofold: first, was the video authentic, and second, was the behaviour itself real (not performed, prompted, or trained for entertainment) and, if so, why would an elephant do this?


## 2. What's Actually True

**The video is authentic.** The footage was traced to its original source: a Facebook page maintained by a Thai elephant mahout (handler) located in Surin Province in northeastern Thailand. The page's name, written in Thai, incorporates the word "mahout" — a term defined by Merriam-Webster as "a keeper and driver of an elephant," used throughout South and Southeast Asia to describe people who work professionally with elephants, often in a lifelong apprenticeship with a specific animal.

The original Facebook video was published on November 18, 2025 and had accumulated over 3 million views by the time of the Snopes investigation. Community verification efforts on Reddit had identified the Facebook page before the formal Snopes fact-check: one X user posted the direct link to the account, and the Reddit thread's conclusion stated: "This comes from a Thai elephant influencer on Facebook. The video was posted on November 18th of this year. He is a 4-year-old male elephant in the Surin Province. They have other videos of the same elephant on their Facebook page."

The original video ran 18 seconds without cuts or transitions. Subsequent review found no major AI artefacts — no shifting, warping, or morphing of significant elements; no physically impossible movements; no inconsistency in the texture of the elephant's skin or the trunk wrinkles. The shadow that briefly appeared at the 7-second mark, which some viewers had cited as a potential AI anomaly, was identified as a shadow from the trunk's own curl. The video's resolution was relatively high without being unrealistically vibrant — a characteristic that tends to distinguish genuine camera footage from AI-generated content, which often applies an unnaturally smooth or oversaturated finish.

**The elephant: a four-year-old male from Surin Province.** The elephant in the video is a young male, approximately four years old, kept in Surin Province in northeastern Thailand. Surin Province has a deep and centuries-long relationship with elephant husbandry; the province hosts an annual Elephant Round-Up festival and is considered one of the centres of traditional mahout culture in Thailand. Surin elephants are typically managed in close human-elephant working relationships, with mahouts often bonding with specific animals over decades.

**The behaviour: sand-dusting and debris-tossing.** The activity depicted — an elephant gathering dry material with its trunk and depositing it on its own body — is a well-documented and functionally understood behaviour in Asian elephants, most commonly called "sand-dusting" or "dust-bathing." According to the Smithsonian's National Zoo and Conservation Biology Institute (2024 public communication), Asian elephants gather a "mixture of sand, dirt and hay" with their trunks and throw it onto their backs in order to "keep cool, prevent sunburn and ward off biting insects." This behaviour is described as learned: young elephants acquire it by observing older members of their herd, and individual elephants vary in how diligently they perform it.

The physiological context explains why dust-bathing is necessary. Asian elephants (*Elephas maximus*) have relatively sparse hair covering their bodies — short and coarse, described in the Smithsonian's account as resembling tyre treads — which provides minimal solar protection. Unlike many mammals, elephants have very limited sweating capacity; they possess sweat glands only between their toes, meaning their primary thermoregulatory mechanisms are behavioural (wallowing, bathing, shade-seeking) and physiological (ear vasodilation) rather than whole-body evaporative cooling. Throwing dry substrate — sand, dirt, grass, or hay — onto the skin serves as a physical barrier against solar radiation and provides an abrasive substrate that may dislodge ectoparasites and flying insects. The behaviour has thus been shaped by natural selection as a practical adaptation to the thermal and parasitological challenges of life in tropical and subtropical habitats.

**Expert confirmation.** Charae "Ray" Sangkaow, owner of the Following Giants elephant sanctuary and safe haven in Thailand, told Snopes by email that "this is normal elephant behavior," adding: "Normally it is mud or dirt for sun protection, or repelling insects, but we do see it with leaves and grasses too." This expert confirmation from a professional elephant conservationist in Thailand directly corroborates the Smithsonian's account and situates the specific grass-tossing behaviour as a variant of the broader sand-dusting category, substituting green grass for the more typical dry substrate.

**Other documented cases.** Reverse-image searches conducted during the investigation also surfaced multiple other videos of elephants with leaves, branches, and brush on their heads and backs — including a video on the Instagram account of elephant conservationist Lek Chailert (@lek_chailert), and a dedicated YouTube video of an elephant throwing brush on its head. This convergent evidence from multiple independent sources of animal welfare and conservation documentation confirms that debris-tossing is not idiosyncratic to the Surin elephant but is observed across the Asian elephant population in multiple countries.


## 3. Why People Believe This (or Disbelieve It)

**AI-scepticism as default mode in 2025–2026.** The AI-authenticity doubt that surrounded this video was even more pronounced than in some comparable cases because the behaviour itself is visually unusual. Unlike the rhino-muntjac encounter (whose AI-doubt rested primarily on the improbability of the species interaction), the elephant video's scepticism was compounded by the appearance of intentionality. The elephant's repeated, precise trunk movements — gathering grass, orienting it, depositing it on the head — looked like deliberate self-directed tool use, and tool-use framing activates stronger assumptions of cognitive complexity and potential visual implausibility.

BBC analysis of AI video detection markers published in November 2025 (Thomas Germain, BBC Future) noted that AI-generated clips tend to be very short — often just a few seconds — because maintaining physical consistency over time is computationally difficult for current generative models. The 18-second duration of the elephant video is a meaningful authenticity signal: an AI-generated clip of this length and apparent physical complexity would be unusual given the state of the art in late 2025. Reddit's community verifiers explicitly cited duration and trunk-wrinkle consistency as their key verification criteria.

**Epistemic novelty: the behaviour as a window into elephant cognition and ecology.** The sand-dusting behaviour depicted raises genuinely interesting questions about learned behaviour, ecological adaptation, and the cognitive capacities of Asian elephants that are worth drawing out explicitly. Research on elephant cognition has documented a wide range of complex behaviours: self-recognition in mirrors (Plotnik et al., 2006, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*), tool use (Hart et al., 2001, *Animal Cognition*), cooperative problem-solving (Plotnik et al., 2011, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*), and mourning behaviour toward deceased conspecifics (Moss, 1988, *Elephant Memories*). The sand-dusting behaviour is less cognitively demanding than some of these documented capacities, but it involves the same basic category of self-directed, environmentally responsive, socially transmitted learned action.

The Smithsonian's noting of individual variation in sand-dusting diligence is particularly interesting from a behavioural ecology perspective. The observation that "some elephants apply this sunscreen more diligently than others" suggests that there is meaningful individual-level variation in the performance of this adaptive behaviour — variation that may reflect individual temperament, learning history, ecological context, or combinations of these factors. The study of individual variation in adaptive behaviours is an active area of animal behaviour research (Bell et al., 2009, *Trends in Ecology & Evolution*), and elephant sand-dusting offers accessible observational data on this question.

The fact that young elephants acquire sand-dusting by watching older herd members is a form of social learning — transmission of adaptive behaviour across generations through observation rather than genetic inheritance. Social learning in elephants has been studied by Lee & Moss (1999, *Advances in the Study of Behavior*) and others, who documented how calves acquire foraging techniques, tool use, and social behaviours from their mothers and other herd members. The sand-dusting video, in this light, is not merely an entertaining anomaly but a visible instance of the same intergenerational knowledge transfer that underlies elephant population resilience.

**The "wig" narrative and its role in virality.** The video's virality was substantially driven by the apparent aesthetic outcome: the accumulating grass on the elephant's head looked increasingly like a wig, creating a visual gag with obvious anthropomorphic resonance. Humans are strongly disposed to project anthropomorphic narratives onto animal behaviour (Epley et al., 2007, *Psychological Review*), particularly when that behaviour appears to produce outcomes that have analogues in human practice — grooming, self-presentation, accessorising. The "elephant wearing a wig" reading was not accurate in any deep sense (the elephant was applying a thermoregulatory substrate, not performing an aesthetic self-presentation ritual), but it was irresistible as a narrative frame and was reflected in the captions attached to most viral distributions.

This anthropomorphic framing has a complicated relationship with the behaviour's factual content. On one hand, it contributed significantly to the video's spread, which ultimately resulted in widespread public awareness of a genuinely interesting adaptive behaviour. On the other hand, it substituted a human self-presentation narrative for the actual ecological and physiological explanation, potentially leaving audiences with a charmed but inaccurate understanding of why the elephant was doing what it was doing. Science communicators who encountered the video had a productive opportunity to use the anthropomorphic hook to deliver the actual content.

**Mahout culture and human-elephant relationships in Thailand.** The original source of the video — a professional mahout's personal Facebook page — provides a cultural context that the viral distribution almost entirely stripped away. Surin Province's mahout tradition represents a specific, centuries-old form of human-elephant relationship in which elephants are managed within family-based working partnerships rather than purely as zoo or sanctuary animals or as wild populations. The mahout relationship has been studied by researchers including Stanton et al. (2021, *PLOS ONE*) in the context of welfare assessments in Thai elephant tourism and working contexts. Understanding that the video came from within this tradition contextualises the elephant's comfort with human proximity and camera presence, and helps explain why the behaviour was captured so clearly — the mahout's familiarity with the animal enabled close observation.


## 4. Verdict

**True — the video is authentic and shows a real, well-documented behaviour.** The footage of a young Asian elephant using its trunk to throw grass onto its own head was real, unmanipulated, and originated from a Thai mahout's Facebook page in Surin Province, Thailand. The 18-second original video exhibited no AI-generation artefacts, was traced to a named and geolocated source, and was confirmed as depicting genuine behaviour by professional elephant welfare experts. The behaviour — a variant of sand-dusting/dust-bathing — is a well-documented, functionally understood adaptive behaviour in Asian elephants that serves thermoregulatory and insect-deterrent purposes.

The widespread AI-authenticity doubt this video generated was an understandable consequence of the current media environment but was resolved by rigorous source-tracing. The Snopes verdict of True is accurate and supported by multiple independent lines of verification.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The elephant grass-tossing video case has implications that extend across three distinct domains: media literacy in the AI era, animal behaviour science communication, and the ethics of virality in animal content.

**AI-authenticity verification as emergent media literacy practice.** This case, like the Zoo Wrocław rhino-muntjac case, illustrates how AI-authenticity verification has become a de facto literacy skill that significant portions of social media audiences are actively practising, sometimes with good methodology and sometimes with poor methodology. The Reddit r/isthisAI community's approach in the elephant case was ultimately sound: they applied multiple independent checks (duration, wrinkle consistency, shadow analysis, source search) and reached the correct conclusion (Real). But the verification process took days of distributed community effort, and large numbers of people interacted with the video during that period without any access to the verification result.

The BBC's own reporting on AI video detection (Germain, 2025) identified duration as the "number one sign" of AI generation, but this heuristic is explicitly provisional — as generative models improve, longer AI videos will become achievable, and duration will become a less reliable distinguishing criterion. This arms-race dynamic means that the specific techniques used to verify authenticity today will require updating as the technical capabilities of AI generation advance. Media literacy curricula need to treat AI-detection knowledge as inherently provisional and continuously updated, not as a fixed skill set.

**Sand-dusting as a gateway to elephant conservation awareness.** The true content of the video — the sand-dusting/dust-bathing behaviour — offers science communicators an opportunity to teach principles of animal ecology through a viral, emotionally engaging gateway. Parasitological load management, solar thermoregulation, socially transmitted learned behaviour, and individual variation in adaptive behaviour are all substantive scientific topics that this video can introduce to general audiences. The challenge is getting from the initial anthropomorphic "elephant wig" frame to the ecological one without losing the audience that the entertainment value initially captured.

Organisations like the Smithsonian National Zoo (whose 2024 Asian Elephant Awareness Month communication explicitly described sand-dusting) and Following Giants in Thailand are positioned to provide exactly this kind of contextualised communication, but their reach is typically smaller than the viral content itself. Bridging the gap between the audience that saw the viral video and the audience that receives the biological explanation requires proactive platform-level engagement or mainstream news coverage that incorporates the scientific context — the kind of reporting that fact-checking investigations like Snopes's can help produce.

**The Asian elephant's conservation status and why it matters.** The species depicted — *Elephas maximus*, the Asian elephant — is classified as Endangered on the IUCN Red List (Red List Assessment: Gopala et al., 2011, *IUCN*), with a population estimated at fewer than 50,000 individuals across disjunct range states in South and Southeast Asia. Habitat loss, fragmentation, human-wildlife conflict, and illegal poaching are the primary drivers of population decline. Captive and semi-captive elephant populations in Thailand, including those managed by mahouts in working relationships, represent a significant proportion of total Asian elephant numbers and have complex welfare considerations associated with their management modes (Elephant Conservation Network assessments; Leimgruber et al., 2008, *Biological Conservation*).

Viral content featuring elephants plays a role in the broader cultural visibility of the species, which in turn affects public support for conservation funding and policy. The framing of elephant behaviour in viral contexts — whether in terms of intelligence, playfulness, emotional complexity, or adaptive competence — shapes the cultural narrative through which conservation arguments are received. Understanding why the grass-tossing video resonated so deeply (the behaviour looks intentional and self-directed; the outcome is visually comic; the animal appears to survey its own work with apparent satisfaction) connects to broader questions about how animal personhood is constructed in popular culture and how that construction either supports or complicates conservation messaging.

**The Surin mahout tradition and welfare debates.** The origin of the video in a Surin mahout's Facebook page invites attention to the contested welfare literature on managed Asian elephants in Thailand. The mahout tradition is ancient and involves genuine bonds of care between specific humans and specific animals, but it also encompasses practices — including some forms of training historically used to make elephants compliant for human use — that contemporary welfare science views critically (Elephant Aid International reports; Schmidt-Burbach et al., 2015, *PLOS ONE*). The elephant in this video appears healthy and unbothered by camera proximity, but that single observation is insufficient basis for welfare assessment. Mentioning this context is appropriate both for accuracy and because the viral framing of mahout-origin elephant content frequently brackets welfare considerations entirely in favour of cuteness.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Jordan Liles's Snopes investigation, published January 5, 2026, provides a model for how viral AI-authenticity doubts should be investigated. The methodology combined three principal tools: reverse-image searching via Google Lens, direct source-tracing to the originating Facebook account, and expert consultation with a professional elephant welfare practitioner.

The reverse-image search approach was essential because the viral copies of the video had stripped the original source attribution. The search surfaced community discussion (including the r/isthisAI thread) where prior verification work had already been done, as well as the X user who had provided the direct link to the mahout's Facebook page. This illustrates an important principle: community verification efforts, even when incomplete, leave traceable marks that formal fact-checkers can exploit and build on. Ignoring community verification work and starting from scratch is less efficient than synthesising what community research has already established.

The expert consultation component — reaching out to Charae "Ray" Sangkaow of Following Giants for comment on whether the behaviour was real — added an independent authoritative voice on the biological question that separated from the source provenance question. This separation matters: source provenance establishes that this particular video is real; expert confirmation establishes that the behaviour it depicts is real and known. Both questions need answers, and they require different evidence.

For media literacy instruction the elephant case illustrates: (1) duration remains a useful but not definitive AI heuristic; (2) anatomical consistency across frames (wrinkles, textures, shadows) is a more nuanced check that requires biological knowledge to apply; (3) community verification on specialist subreddits often precedes and partially accomplishes what formal fact-checkers will later verify; (4) source geolocation (Surin Province, Thailand) provides cultural and ecological context that enriches the verification beyond simple true/false; and (5) the biological explanation of the depicted behaviour is itself a substantive component of the story, not merely a footnote to the authenticity determination.

The convergence between this case and the rhino-muntjac case (same publication period, same AI-doubt dynamics, same resolution methodology) suggests that AI-authenticity verification of genuinely unusual but real animal behaviour is becoming a recurring and structurally predictable category of fact-checking work. Developing standardised verification protocols specifically for this category — combining duration analysis, artefact checking, source-tracing, and expert biological consultation — would increase efficiency and consistency across fact-checking organisations engaging this type of claim.
