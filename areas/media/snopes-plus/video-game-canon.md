## §1 · Claim & Verdict Summary

**Core claim:** Entering the numeric code "153349" on a Canon calculator, then pressing the plus (+) and minus (−) keys simultaneously, unlocks a hidden game of Tetris playable on the calculator's display.

**Snopes verdict:** FALSE

**Truth Vault classification:** EARC-E (Empirically Refuted Claim). The claim is entirely falsified by direct physical replication: entering the code on any Canon calculator produces no game, no mode switch, and no alteration of calculator behavior. The code simply echoes back as the number 153349 on the display and nothing more. The videos documenting the supposed trick were produced through video editing, explicitly acknowledged by their creator as non-factual creative content.

This case belongs to a recurring internet folklore genre — the "device Easter egg" rumor — in which manipulated media creates the appearance that consumer hardware contains hidden modes or features not documented in official manuals. The genre has deep roots in technology culture and exploits genuine uncertainty about the complexity of embedded firmware in commercial devices.

## §2 · Origin & Spread

The original video was uploaded to YouTube by the user Brusspup on 23 October 2008, showing what appeared to be a Canon calculator displaying and running a game of Tetris after a numeric code was entered. The video accumulated millions of views over the years following its upload.

Brusspup's YouTube channel is explicitly dedicated to creative visual tricks, illusions, and editing experiments. As of the Snopes investigation, the channel homepage and video descriptions included clear, explicit disclaimers: "This video is for fun. Just practicing my editing skills." The Tetris calculator video was one of multiple videos in a series — others purportedly showed codes to play Defender and Pong on Canon calculators — each accompanied by the same disclaimer language.

Despite these disclaimers being present on the original source material, secondary coverage routinely stripped the disclaimer context. Two particularly notable examples of decontextualized amplification were:

1. An eHow article framed as a "How To" guide for playing Tetris on a Canon calculator, treating the video as evidence of a genuine calculator feature and providing step-by-step instructions.
2. An article on the Official Sharp Blog titled "7 Amazing Tricks a Calculator Can Do," which included the Canon Tetris trick as one of seven genuine calculator capabilities.

Both articles treated the video as factual without noting the creator's own disclaimer. This laundering of a self-described creative fiction through credible-seeming secondary outlets is a standard mechanism by which internet hoaxes gain unwarranted epistemic authority.

The rumor persisted in online communities through the mid-2010s, regularly surfacing on social media platforms when new audiences encountered the video without encountering the disclaimer, and was brought to Snopes' attention via reader inquiry in April 2015.

## §3 · Technical and Cultural Analysis

**The epistemic novelty here lies in the intersection of firmware complexity, platform credibility laundering, and the specific psychology of "Easter egg" belief cultivation.**

**Why the claim was initially plausible:**

Consumer electronics, including calculators, do sometimes contain genuine undocumented features or "Easter eggs" — hidden modes included by developers for their own amusement or testing convenience. The Texas Instruments TI-83, a ubiquitous graphing calculator used in secondary education, contains genuine hidden games and diagnostic modes accessible through specific key sequences. The Casio fx-9750 series includes a hidden racing minigame. Calculator Easter eggs are not a mythological category; they are a documented feature of the firmware development culture that has existed since at least the 1970s.

This background reality creates a genuine epistemic warrant for initial credulity when an apparently specific claim ("enter code X on model Y") is presented with apparent video evidence. Audiences reasoning from base rates of calculator Easter egg existence have no obvious reason to immediately dismiss a video demonstration as fabricated.

**What the video exploited:**

The Brusspup video exploited the post-production capabilities of basic video editing to insert frames of a Tetris game display onto apparent footage of a calculator screen. The edit does not require sophisticated special effects — a cut between footage of a real calculator and footage of an edited display (or a phone screen showing the game) would be sufficient. The visual resolution of consumer calculators' segmented displays makes it relatively easy to fake convincing display content, because the low-information density of the display means few pixels need to be altered.

**Platform credibility laundering:**

The epistemic novelty of this case at a structural level is the mechanism by which a self-labeled fiction video became, through secondary citation, a credible-seeming factual claim. This three-stage process — (1) original creator produces labeled fiction, (2) secondary publisher reproduces the content while discarding or ignoring the label, (3) tertiary audiences encounter the secondary publication and inherit its implicit factual framing — is a documented pathway in misinformation propagation (Vosoughi, Roy, & Aral, 2018, *Science*).

The eHow and Official Sharp Blog articles represent a particularly instructive variant: they did not merely fail to check the claim but transformed its genre. A video labeled "practicing my editing skills" on YouTube is in the genre of creative fiction. An eHow "How To" article is, by genre convention, a guide to performing a real action. When the claim migrated from one genre container to the other, the epistemic status claimed implicitly changed with it, entirely independently of any factual development.

**The calculator's firmware architecture:**

For the mechanically curious: Canon calculator firmware is written to serve a single defined purpose — arithmetic computation — with all user-facing interface behaviors specified in the device manual. Unlike programmable calculators (such as TI graphing models) or specialized scientific calculators with extended function libraries, standard Canon desktop calculators contain no general-purpose display-driving logic that could be repurposed to render a tile-based game. The display hardware itself — typically a 7-segment or 10-segment numeric LED/LCD — is not capable of representing the spatial information required to render a Tetris-style field of falling tetrominoes. Even if a firmware command existed to redirect display behavior, the hardware cannot represent the claimed visual output (Katz, 2011, *Embedded Systems Design*, Elsevier). This is an independent grounds for refutation: the claim is not just behaviorally unconfirmed but architecturally impossible on standard Canon hardware.

## §4 · Evidence of Falsity

The claim's falsity is established by direct empirical test: the code "153349" entered on a Canon calculator produces no game, no mode change, and no non-arithmetic behavior. Multiple users reported attempting the sequence on various Canon calculator models following exposure to the viral video, uniformly reporting that the sequence simply displayed the number 153349 on screen and nothing else.

The original content creator's explicit disclaimer — "This video is for fun. Just practicing my editing skills" — constitutes a first-person confession that the video documented a creative exercise, not a functional product capability. When the source of a claim explicitly and publicly states that the claim is fictional, the claim's evidentiary status is nil.

The broader pattern on Brusspup's channel — multiple videos of the same type (Defender, Pong, Tetris) with identical disclaimers — confirms that the "Canon calculator hidden game" series was a coherent creative project, not a documentary series about undiscovered firmware features.

No Canon technical documentation, service manual, firmware source code, or authorized dealer guidance has ever confirmed the existence of a "game mode" or undocumented entertainment function in any Canon calculator model. Canon has not, to any documented record, acknowledged or denied the specific claim, because the standard corporate response to demonstrably false product feature claims is silence.

## §5 · Why the Claim Persists

The Canon calculator Tetris claim persists in online culture for reasons that illuminate how internet folklore reproduces across platform generations:

**Social friction costs of verification are nonzero.** Instantly verifying the claim requires physical access to a Canon calculator. Unlike web-based factual claims that can be refuted with a 10-second search, this claim requires a hardware artifact that many people do not have immediately at hand. The friction of verification extends the window during which the claim circulates with unexamined credibility.

**The claim is harmless in its emotional stakes.** Unlike health misinformation or political hoaxes, the Canon Tetris claim poses no meaningful harm to the person who believes it. Beliefs with low stakes are less likely to trigger motivated verification behavior (Pennycook & Rand, 2019, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*). The calculator rumor occupies the "fun trivia" mental category, where truth-seeking motivation is relaxed.

**Platform migration erases context.** Each time the claim migrated to a new platform — from YouTube to eHow to Facebook shares to Twitter threads — the accompanying disclaimer context had another opportunity to be stripped. Digital information cascading across platforms consistently loses contextual metadata over time (Lazer et al., 2018, *Science*). A viewer encountering a screenshot of the calculator displaying Tetris in their feed, without any link to the original YouTube video, has been given pure false signal with zero counter-signal.

**"Easter egg" enthusiasm is genuine and culturally active.** The tech enthusiast culture that documents and celebrates genuine firmware Easter eggs is large and engaged. Websites dedicated to Easter egg collection have existed since the early web era. This community's genuine enthusiasm for Easter egg discovery creates a receptive audience already primed to welcome and share apparent new finds without the same skepticism applied to other factual claims.

**The video aesthetics are convincing.** Brusspup's production values are high enough that, absent any prior reason for suspicion, the video appears to document a real device behavior. Research on video authenticity assessment confirms that untrained viewers are poor at detecting post-production manipulations in both static and dynamic content when those manipulations are well-executed (Farid, 2009, *Scientific American*). The challenge of visual authentication of video evidence is a broad epistemic problem, not limited to this case.

## §6 · Conclusion and Epistemic Takeaway

The Canon calculator Tetris claim is FALSE. The code "153349" does not unlock any hidden game on Canon calculators. The video demonstrating the trick was explicitly labeled creative fiction by its creator, whose disclaimer accompanied the original upload. Secondary publications that reported the claim as fact did so through genre-laundering — transplanting self-confessed fiction into a factual genre container — and in doing so converted a labeled non-claim into apparent evidence.

The canonical epistemic lesson here is the **disclaimer-stripping problem**: online information cascades systematically remove source context, including explicit falsity labels embedded by original creators. A piece of content labeled "fiction" at origin becomes indistinguishable from "fact" after sufficient platform hops if audiences do not follow the chain back to the primary source. This problem is structurally similar to — but distinct from — deliberate hoax fabrication. In this case, no deception was attempted by the original creator; deception emerged through the dynamics of secondary and tertiary publication.

For technology Easter eggs specifically, the appropriate epistemic procedure is: (a) check whether the claimed code has been independently confirmed by multiple unrelated users with hardware in hand; (b) check whether the claimed behavior is architecturally possible given the device's hardware specification; (c) check the original source material for disclaimers or genre signals. This case fails all three tests.

**Research gap:** The "Easter egg hoax" as a distinct misinformation genre — false or fabricated claims about hidden features in consumer hardware or software — has not been formally taxonomized in the misinformation research literature. While Easter eggs as genuine phenomena are documented in software history studies (Levy, 1984, *Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution*), the propagation dynamics of fabricated Easter egg claims remain unexamined as a distinct category.
