# Violent Crime Is Increasing Globally and the World Is Becoming More Dangerous

## 1. The Claim

In surveys conducted across high-income democracies, majorities of respondents consistently report believing that crime is rising, that their communities are less safe than they were five or ten years ago, and that the world overall is becoming a more violent and dangerous place. The belief is so widespread that it functions as background common knowledge — a default assumption rarely challenged and frequently reinforced by political speech, news programming, and social media timelines.

The claim typically includes several distinct components: that violent crime (murder, assault, robbery, sexual violence) is increasing year on year; that the increase is global; that communities that were once safe are deteriorating; and that some recent change — immigration, drug use, social media, economic inequality, or political dysfunction — is responsible for the deterioration. These components are often asserted together as a coherent narrative of civilisational decline or social breakdown.

This claim is politically active: it has been central to electoral campaigns in the United States, United Kingdom, France, Germany, Brazil, and elsewhere, typically serving arguments for stronger policing, tougher sentencing, border restriction, or the rollback of criminal justice reforms. The claim's political utility gives it structural support beyond organic belief — advocates with policy agendas reinforce it regardless of the underlying data. Understanding the claim therefore requires evaluating both the empirical question (is violent crime actually rising?) and the political-communication question (why does the belief persist and who benefits from it?).

The short answer to the empirical question is that the claim is false for most of the world across most long-run time horizons. For most high-income countries over most of the past three decades, violent crime — and homicide in particular, which is the most reliably measured form of violent crime — has declined substantially. The world is, on most measurable dimensions, considerably less violent than it was fifty, one hundred, or five hundred years ago. The perception of rising danger is a systematic artifact of media consumption patterns and cognitive biases that cause people to overestimate risk from vivid, emotionally charged events.

## 2. What's Actually True

**Homicide as the Anchor Metric**: Homicide is the most reliable metric for comparative violence research because deaths are hard to conceal, incentives to report are relatively consistent, and recording practices are more standardised than for other violent crimes. Even homicide data has significant limitations — definitions vary across jurisdictions, undercounting occurs in conflict-affected areas, and historical estimates carry wide uncertainty bands — but it provides the best available long-run signal.

In the United States, the homicide rate peaked in 1980 at approximately 10.2 per 100,000 population and in 1991 at approximately 9.8 per 100,000. By 2014, it had fallen to approximately 4.5 per 100,000 — a decline of more than 50 percent over three decades (Federal Bureau of Investigation, Uniform Crime Reporting Program, multiple years). The COVID-19 period saw an anomalous spike, with the homicide rate rising to approximately 6.5 per 100,000 in 2020–2021, before declining again in 2022–2023. Even at the 2020–2021 spike, U.S. homicide rates remained well below the 1980s peaks.

In Western Europe, homicide rates are substantially lower than in the United States and have also declined. England and Wales recorded a homicide rate of approximately 1.1 per 100,000 in 1990; by 2015 it had fallen to approximately 0.89 per 100,000, near a historical low (Office for National Statistics, *Crime in England and Wales*, annual series). Germany, France, and Sweden show similar long-run declining trends across the post-war period. Nordic countries, often cited as among the safest in the world, have homicide rates of 1–2 per 100,000 that have remained stable or declined over decades.

**Long-Run Historical Context**: The long-run perspective dramatically amplifies the decline. Historical criminologist Manuel Eisner's analysis of homicide rates in Western Europe from the 13th century to the present — synthesising data from court records, coroners' inquests, and burial registers — found that homicide rates in medieval and early modern Europe were typically 20–40 times higher than modern rates (Eisner, M., 2003, "Long-term historical trends in violent crime," *Crime and Justice: A Review of Research*, 30, 83–142). Medieval English towns that appear in 14th-century records had homicide rates of 50–100 per 100,000 per year. Contemporary England's rate of under 1 per 100,000 represents a thirty- to seventy-fold reduction over the same territory in six centuries.

Steven Pinker's *The Better Angels of Our Nature* (2011, Viking Press) synthesised these and related findings across death in warfare, genocide, torture, and interpersonal violence, arguing for a long-run decline in human violence that he attributed to the expansion of the state, commerce, the printing press, and Enlightenment institutions. The book generated significant academic debate — the mechanisms Pinker proposes remain contested, and several critics have challenged specific datasets — but the empirical core finding of long-run homicide decline in Western Europe has not been successfully disputed (Eisner, M., 2014, "From swords to words: Does macro-level change in self-control predict long-term variation in levels of homicide?" *Crime and Justice*, 43, 65–134).

**Global Picture**: The global picture is more heterogeneous and caution is warranted in generalising. Latin America has exceptionally high homicide rates by world standards — Central America, Venezuela, Brazil, and Colombia have recorded rates of 30–80+ per 100,000 in recent decades, driven by organised crime and drug cartel violence that creates effectively war-like conditions in specific territories (UNODC, 2019, *Global Study on Homicide*, United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime, Vienna). Sub-Saharan Africa shows significant variation, with conflict-affected states recording very high rates and more stable states recording much lower rates. These regional realities are genuine and severe.

However, the UNODC's *Global Study on Homicide* data, covering over 190 countries, shows a global average homicide rate that has declined from approximately 7.7 per 100,000 in 2000 to approximately 6.1 per 100,000 in 2017 — a reduction of about 21 percent over 17 years (UNODC, 2019, *Global Study on Homicide*). High-income OECD countries as a group show steeper declines. The regions with the highest rates (Latin America, parts of Africa) are not regions where the "world is becoming more dangerous" narrative is primarily generated or consumed — which is itself a telling asymmetry.

**The Perception-Reality Gap**: The gap between perceived and actual crime trends is empirically documented and large. In the United Kingdom, annual surveys by the Crime Survey for England and Wales ask respondents both about their personal victimisation experiences (actual crime) and their beliefs about national crime trends. For most of the period 2002–2020, surveyed majorities believed national crime was rising even during years when recorded crime was falling precipitously (Office for National Statistics, *Crime Survey for England and Wales*, annual editions). The Gallup survey in the United States has tracked this perception-reality gap for decades: in most years between the early 1990s and the late 2010s, 60–80 percent of Americans told pollsters crime was getting worse nationally, even as FBI data showed sustained year-on-year declines (Gallup, *Crime*, annual tracking, 1989–present).

## 3. Why People Believe This

The perception-reality gap in crime is among the most studied divergences between public opinion and empirical reality in social science. Several well-characterised mechanisms explain it, operating at different levels.

**The Availability Heuristic and Vivid Instance Weighting**: Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman's foundational work on the availability heuristic (Tversky, A. & Kahneman, D., 1973, "Availability: A heuristic for judging frequency and probability," *Cognitive Psychology*, 5(2), 207–232) demonstrated that people estimate probabilities and frequencies by the ease with which examples come to mind rather than by base rates. Violent crime is, by the standards of news coverage, dramatically over-represented relative to its base rate. A single high-profile murder receives dozens of news cycles; the absence of crime on a given day generates no coverage at all. The result is a cognitive environment in which the availability of crime instances in memory far exceeds their actual frequency in the lived environment.

This mechanism is claim-specific because it operates differently for crime than for many other risks. Car accident deaths — which kill far more people annually than homicide in every high-income country — are not the subject of individual-incident news cycles. Heart disease, which kills 40–50 times more Americans per year than homicide, is structurally invisible on news programmes. The systematic over-coverage of violent crime relative to its actual risk contribution creates a specific and measurable distortion in risk perception.

**Negativity Bias and Differential Information Processing**: Humans process negative information more deeply, weight it more heavily in memory, and are more strongly motivated to seek it out than positive or neutral information — a phenomenon described across multiple cognitive frameworks (Baumeister, R.F., Bratslavsky, E., Finkenauer, C. & Vohs, K.D., 2001, "Bad is stronger than good," *Review of General Psychology*, 5(4), 323–370). In the crime context, this means that a crime story encountered in the news generates stronger encoding and longer retention than a "crime statistics fell this year" story, even controlling for story placement and word count.

The negativity bias also operates at the selective-exposure level: news producers know that fear-inducing content generates higher engagement metrics (time on page, shares, comment activity) than reassuring content. The commercial structure of digital news therefore creates systematic selection pressure toward crime stories, which in turn amplifies the availability heuristic — more crime stories → easier recall → higher perceived frequency → stronger belief that crime is rising.

**The Local-National Distinction and Social Comparison**: One of the most consistent findings in crime perception research is that people typically rate their own immediate neighbourhood as safer than "the country" or "society in general." In the Gallup surveys, respondents who believe national crime is rising simultaneously report that their own neighbourhood is safe (Gallup, *Crime*, annual tracking series, 1989–present). This local-national asymmetry reveals that the "rising crime" belief is driven primarily by media consumption rather than personal experience or community-level observation.

The mechanism is that personal experience provides a relatively accurate signal about local conditions (I can see that my street is safe), while national or global conditions are known only through media. Since media over-represents violent events, the mediated national image is more frightening than the experienced local reality. Most people live in areas where they are unlikely to experience violent crime, and their direct experience reflects this. But they infer from media consumption that somewhere else crime is rampant and rising.

**Politicisation and Availability Cascades**: Cass Sunstein and Timur Kuran's concept of the "availability cascade" (Kuran, T. & Sunstein, C.R., 1999, "Availability cascades and risk regulation," *Stanford Law Review*, 51, 683–768) describes how an initial availability bias can be amplified by social feedback into a self-sustaining false belief system: media attention → public concern → political attention → more media attention → further public concern. Crime beliefs have undergone multiple availability cascades, often deliberately seeded by political actors who benefit from elevated fear.

The political instrumentalisation of crime fear is documented in political science research. Campaigns and elected officials in multiple countries have strategically emphasised crime salience in election contexts, knowing that heightened crime perception favours law-and-order electoral messaging regardless of actual crime levels (Beckett, K., 1997, *Making Crime Pay: Law and Order in Contemporary American Politics*, Oxford University Press). The resulting dynamic creates a political-incentive structure that works against accurate crime communication: political actors who benefit from fear have every reason to amplify crime stories, while those who might benefit from accurate reporting of declining crime often find the accurate story electorally unrewarding.

## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** False (for most high-income countries over most recent time horizons); Regionally Heterogeneous (globally)
**Confidence:** High (high-income country data); Moderate (global data, given quality limitations)
**Epistemic Basis:** Multi-source empirical consensus; perception-reality gap extensively documented
**Phenomenological Status:** Real perception, false reality
**Mechanistic Status:** Fully characterised (availability heuristic, negativity bias, media structure, politicisation)
**Folk Wisdom Value:** Low

The claim that violent crime is increasing globally and the world is becoming more dangerous is false as a general proposition and inverted relative to best-available data for high-income countries over multi-decade time horizons. Homicide rates in the United States, Western Europe, and most OECD countries are near post-war or historical lows. The global average homicide rate has declined over the past two decades according to UNODC data. The very long run (centuries) shows a decline in interpersonal violence so large as to make contemporary rates almost inconceivably low by medieval standards.

Genuine regional exceptions exist. Latin America and parts of sub-Saharan Africa have very high homicide rates, and the violence there is severe and real. The claim that the world overall is becoming more dangerous does not describe these regions accurately either — their rates have fluctuated considerably over decades rather than monotonically rising — but they represent genuine safety failures that deserve serious policy attention.

The sustained popular belief in rising crime despite contrary evidence is not primarily a failure of information access. It is a predictable output of combining the availability heuristic, negativity bias in memory encoding, news media selection pressures for engaging (i.e., frightening) content, and the deliberate politicisation of crime fear by actors with electoral or commercial interests in elevated public anxiety. The perception is real; the underlying trend it claims to describe is not.

This is a case where the false belief carries significant policy costs. Overestimated crime risk supports punitive criminal justice policies that are expensive, demonstrably ineffective at reducing crime, and impose severe harms on incarcerated individuals and their communities. Several decades of criminological research documents that incarceration rates in the United States expanded dramatically during a period of falling crime, driven substantially by inflated public and political fear rather than evidence-based analysis (Western, B., 2006, *Punishment and Inequality in America*, Russell Sage Foundation). The crime-rising myth has thus contributed materially to the United States operating an incarceration system many times larger than comparable democracies, at enormous fiscal and human cost.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**The Criminological Consensus and Its Limits**: The finding of long-run declining homicide in high-income countries is among the more robust results in criminology. Its mechanisms, however, remain contested. Robert Sampson and colleagues have emphasised the role of community cohesion and collective efficacy in determining local crime rates (Sampson, R.J., Raudenbush, S.W. & Earls, F., 1997, "Neighborhoods and violent crime: A multilevel study of collective efficacy," *Science*, 277(5328), 918–924). Franklin Zimring and Gordon Hawkins (Zimring, F.E. & Hawkins, G., 1997, *Crime Is Not the Problem: Lethal Violence in America*, Oxford University Press) documented the exceptional U.S. homicide rate relative to other high-income countries and attributed it primarily to gun availability rather than higher rates of violent confrontation per se — Americans and Canadians have similar rates of assault, but Americans' assaults are far more likely to produce deaths because firearms are involved.

The 1990s U.S. crime decline generated a cottage industry of competing explanations: Steven Levitt and John Donohue controversially attributed much of it to the legalisation of abortion in the early 1970s and the resulting reduction in cohorts of unwanted children born into high-risk environments (Donohue, J.J. & Levitt, S.D., 2001, "The impact of legalized abortion on crime," *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 116(2), 379–420). Others emphasised the crack cocaine epidemic's ebbing, demographic shifts, policing innovations, and economic improvement. Most criminologists now accept that no single factor explains the full decline, and that crime rates respond to complex, interacting causal systems.

The COVID-19 anomaly — a homicide spike in 2020–2021 in the United States and several other countries — has been studied intensively. Proposed explanations include disruption of social institutions and community supervision structures; reductions in incarceration leading to an increased at-risk population in the community; economic stress and psychological distress; and changes in policing posture. The spike was real, concentrated in specific urban areas and demographic groups, and appears to have partially reversed. It does not support a narrative of general rising crime; it supports a narrative of specific conditions during an extraordinary social disruption generating a temporary increase that remained well below historical peaks.

**Cross-National Variation and What It Teaches**: The dramatic variation in homicide rates across comparable wealthy democracies provides natural experiments for causal analysis. The United States homicide rate of 5–7 per 100,000 is 4–7 times higher than most Western European countries. Japan, South Korea, and Singapore have rates of 0.2–0.5 per 100,000, an order of magnitude below the U.S. These differences persist across decades and across economic cycles, suggesting that structural factors (gun availability, inequality, social safety nets, incarceration practices) rather than transient events are responsible.

Income inequality correlates with homicide rates across countries in the World Bank's data (Fajnzylber, P., Lederman, D. & Loayza, N., 2002, "Inequality and violent crime," *Journal of Law and Economics*, 45(1), 1–39). Countries with higher Gini coefficients — more unequal income distributions — systematically show higher homicide rates, a relationship that holds within countries over time as well as between countries cross-sectionally. This finding has significant policy implications: crime reduction may be achievable through redistribution and social investment as much as through policing, a conclusion that cuts against the political direction typically taken by those who amplify crime fear.

**The Media Environment and Structural Distortion**: The internet era has intensified the media dynamics that drive crime fear. Legacy broadcast news had its own availability-bias problems, but at least had finite air time constraining the total volume of crime content. Digital platforms, operating on engagement-maximising algorithms, have no such constraint. A single violent incident can generate days of continuous coverage, social media sharing, and comment engagement, implying very high availability weight in memory regardless of its statistical representativeness.

Experimental research by Dominic Johnson and colleagues has documented that elevated threat perception — regardless of its accuracy — activates specific cognitive modes characterised by reduced trust, increased in-group/out-group differentiation, and greater tolerance for authoritarian solutions (Johnson, D.D.P. & Fowler, J.H., 2011, "The evolution of overconfidence," *Nature*, 477, 317–320). Fear is not merely an accurate or inaccurate emotional response to risk; it is a cognitive state that reshapes political preferences toward security-seeking and punitive systems. This creates a feedback loop between crime fear narratives and political outcomes that is at least partially independent of actual crime levels.

**The Role of Race in U.S. Crime Perception**: In the United States specifically, research has documented that crime fear is racialised in ways that further distort accurate risk perception. Studies by Michelle Alexander and others have documented that the War on Drugs and mass incarceration were implemented in ways that systematically targeted Black communities despite similar rates of drug use and many comparable crimes across racial groups (Alexander, M., 2010, *The New Jim Crow: Mass Incarceration in the Age of Colorblindness*, New Press). Media coverage of crime in U.S. news programmes over-represents Black perpetrators relative to their actual proportion of crime, a finding documented by a substantial body of content analysis research (Dixon, T.L., 2017, "Good guys are still always white? Challenging race and crime stereotypes in local news," *Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly*, 94(1), 142–160).

This racialisation of crime imagery means that U.S. crime fear is not merely an abstract statistical misperception; it is intertwined with racial anxiety and stereotyping, operating as a mechanism through which racial fear shapes criminal justice policy under empirically false premises. Correcting the "crime is rising" claim in this context requires engaging not only with statistics but with the racial politics through which crime narratives are constructed and consumed.

**Long-Run Optimism and Its Grounds**: Pinker's *Better Angels* thesis remains controversial, particularly regarding the claim that Enlightenment institutions are the primary causal mechanism of the long-run decline in violence. Critics including John Gray (Gray, J., 2015, "Steven Pinker is wrong about violence and war," *The Guardian*, March 13) have argued that Pinker under-weights colonial violence and that the post-1945 peace among great powers may be an anomaly rather than a trend. Nassim Taleb has raised methodological objections about fat-tailed distributions and the inferential problems of using historical absence-of-war to estimate future war probability (Taleb, N.N. & Cirillo, P., 2016, "The decline of violent conflicts: What do the data really say?" *Nobel Foundation Symposium*, Stockholm).

These are genuine intellectual disputes about mechanisms and generalisability, not disputes about the core homicide data that shows long-run decline. The most parsimonious reading of the available evidence is that interpersonal violence has declined dramatically in measurable terms while remaining unpredictably catastrophic at the level of organised conflict. The claim that "the world is becoming more dangerous" bundles together interpersonal crime, geopolitical conflict, terrorism, and other risk categories in ways that resist clean evaluation — but no honest reading of the data supports the conclusion that the trajectory of human violence is upward.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes does not maintain a direct entry evaluating the general claim that violent crime is increasing. The platform has addressed specific sub-components: fact-checks on particular politicians' claims about crime statistics, evaluations of misleadingly presented data about Chicago or other high-profile cities, and corrections of specific viral memes citing fabricated crime figures. These are valuable but do not constitute a systematic treatment of the overall claim.

PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, and similar operations have addressed the "crime is rising" claim repeatedly in electoral contexts, typically rating specific political claims about crime trends as "False," "Mostly False," or "Half True" depending on the time frame and geography selected. These fact-checks correctly note that the claim contradicts long-run trends but sometimes acknowledge genuine short-run upticks without fully contextualising them within the longer-run decline.

The most significant gap in standard fact-checking treatment of crime claims is the failure to engage with the psychological and structural mechanisms producing the perception-reality gap. It is not enough to cite FBI statistics in response to a claim that "feels" true to people whose media diet systematically over-represents violent incidents; that approach addresses the propositional claim without explaining why the false belief is so durable and so resistant to statistical correction. A fuller treatment — which this entry attempts — requires addressing the availability heuristic, the media selection dynamics, and the political instrumentalisation of crime fear as the systems that maintain the false belief despite the existence of widely available contradictory data.

The political economy of fact-checking also creates structural challenges here. Crime fear claims are largely generated by political actors with strong institutional support, while the correcting information comes from academic criminologists and statistical agencies that lack comparable media platforms. The asymmetry of amplification means that false claims routinely reach larger audiences than corrections, a problem no individual fact-check fully resolves. Sustained public education — through school curricula that teach statistical literacy and media literacy, through well-designed crime reporting guidelines for journalists, and through political norms that penalise empirically false crime claims — is ultimately a more scalable corrective than reactive fact-checking.
