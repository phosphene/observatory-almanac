# The vitamin K shot given to newborns is unnecessary or dangerous

## 1. The Claim

In the summer of 2023, a wave of posts began circulating on social media platforms asserting, with varying degrees of agitation, that the routine vitamin K injection administered to newborn babies was something between medically unjustified and outright sinister. The more dramatic versions of the claim went further still: "Vitamin K does not exist," wrote one widely shared account. Others described the injection as "mandatory poisoning," a "Big Pharma invention," or an assault on infants that parents had a duty to refuse.

The claims arrived in a climactic burst following a tweet from anti-vaccine activist Steve Kirsch, who in June 2023 declared his belief that "the US would be better off if everyone refused every vaccine and the K shot." The phrase "the K shot" was doing significant work in that sentence, conflating two separate things — vaccines and vitamin K supplementation — into a single target. Underneath the most incendiary posts ran a stranger thread: that vitamin K, the substance itself, had no independent existence. It was allegedly not a vitamin at all. The letter K, several commentators insisted, means potassium. Therefore, the medical establishment was injecting babies with potassium — or had invented a fictional nutrient — purely for profit or population control.

These claims, however exaggerated or internally inconsistent, had real-world consequences. Paediatric clinicians reported a measurable uptick in parents requesting to decline the shot. This followed a documented trend that the *New York Review of Books* had flagged as early as 2019, when it noted that vitamin K refusal was growing steadily within the same communities that rejected childhood vaccines. The overlap was not incidental. The same misinformation ecosystem that had eroded vaccine confidence was now turning its attention to one of the oldest newborn interventions in modern medicine.

## 2. What's Actually True

Vitamin K is a genuine, well-characterised group of fat-soluble vitamins whose primary biological role is to support blood coagulation. It was first described in the scientific literature in 1927, with its coagulation function confirmed through experimental work in the 1930s. The name "K" does, in fact, derive from German: the word *Koagulation*, reflecting where the substance was first found to have a decisive physiological effect. The naming convention is a historical artefact, not a conspiracy.

There are two major naturally occurring forms. Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone) is found predominantly in leafy green vegetables — spinach, kale, broccoli, and similar foods. Vitamin K2 (menaquinone) is synthesised by gut bacteria and also found in fermented foods and animal products. Both forms are metabolised and used by the body to activate clotting factors; without adequate levels of vitamin K, the coagulation cascade fails. Wounds do not close. Internal bleeding is not contained.

In adults and older children, this is rarely a problem. A reasonably varied diet replenishes vitamin K continuously, gut bacteria provide supplemental synthesis, and the liver stores modest reserves. Newborns are in a categorically different situation.

All newborns — not a subset, not an at-risk cohort, but every single human infant — are born with low vitamin K levels. This is not a pathological finding. It is a normal feature of the transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life. Several factors converge to create this state: vitamin K crosses the placenta poorly; breast milk, while nutritionally rich, contains relatively little vitamin K compared to the amount needed to support early coagulation; and the neonatal gut has not yet been colonised by the bacteria that would later contribute to vitamin K synthesis. The result is that nearly all newborns are, for a window of time after birth, at elevated risk of abnormal bleeding — a condition called Vitamin K Deficiency Bleeding, or VKDB.

VKDB can be early (within 24 hours of birth), classical (days two through seven), or late (two weeks to six months of age). Early and classical VKDB are uncommon in infants who are fed adequately; late VKDB is the most dangerous, because it often presents without warning and can cause life-threatening intracranial haemorrhage — bleeding inside the skull. Late VKDB is the condition that the newborn injection is specifically designed to prevent.

The preventive power of the injection is not a matter of speculation. A landmark observational study published in the *British Medical Journal* in 1991, drawing on data from across the British Isles, found that infants who received no vitamin K at birth were **81 times more likely** to develop late VKDB than those who received the injection. That figure has been consistently replicated in subsequent research across different healthcare systems and populations. The American Academy of Pediatrics first recommended routine vitamin K prophylaxis for all newborns in 1961; the recommendation has been reaffirmed in every subsequent review of the evidence.

The injectable form typically administered in hospital settings is a synthetic phytonadione — that is, a synthetic version of vitamin K1 — dissolved in a carrier solution. The dose is 0.5 to 1 mg depending on the infant's weight. It does not contain potassium. It is not a vaccine, does not introduce any pathogen or adjuvant, and does not interact with the immune system in the way vaccines do; it is purely a nutritional supplement delivered at a moment when the infant cannot acquire adequate levels through any other mechanism.

As for the claim that vitamin K "does not exist": this appears to originate from a category error — specifically, the conflation of the vitamin with the chemical symbol for potassium. In the periodic table, potassium's symbol is K, derived from *Kalium*, the Latin-derived term used in Germanic languages. Some commentators appear to have encountered references to "Vitamin K" late in life and drawn an incorrect inference that the K referred to the element. It does not. Vitamin K and potassium are entirely distinct substances with separate biochemical identities, metabolic roles, and mechanisms of action. No credentialled pharmacist, chemist, or physician has ever filled a "vitamin K prescription" with potassium tablets.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The vitamin K refusal movement does not exist in isolation. It is best understood as a downstream consequence of a broader anti-institutional turn in health attitudes, accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic and the ambient distrust of pharmaceutical companies that has grown over several decades.

Several psychological and social mechanisms are at work. First, there is the logic of contamination avoidance: the idea that it is unnatural to inject a substance into a newborn within hours of birth, before the infant has had any chance to adapt to the outside world. The framing of "mandatory" intervention — even when the evidence for it is overwhelming — reliably triggers resistance in a population sensitised to corporate or governmental overreach in health decisions. The perception of coercion substitutes for engagement with the underlying science.

Second, the category error between vitamin K and potassium is not as arbitrary as it might seem. Most people have never had reason to examine the naming conventions of vitamins, and the human pattern-matching capacity, when looking for evidence of deception, will seize on apparent coincidences. The K-means-potassium claim feels like a revelation to people who encounter it — a hidden truth that mainstream medicine has not bothered to correct. This is how much online misinformation works: it exploits genuine ambiguity or complexity in the world, and converts it into apparent proof of deliberate concealment.

Third, the anti-vaccine milieu has been explicitly working to extend its scope beyond conventional vaccines. Vaccine-hesitant parents are a high-value audience for health misinformation producers, and the vitamin K shot is a logical adjacent target precisely because it shares the feature of being injected and administered at birth. The effort to conflate it with vaccination — as Kirsch's tweet explicitly did — is a rhetorical strategy, not a medical analysis.

Fourth, there is the genuine complexity of knowing who and what to trust after a period (the COVID-19 pandemic) in which public health messaging was sometimes inconsistent, sometimes changed with limited explanation, and sometimes deployed alongside policy interventions that many people experienced as coercive. When trust in institutions has been damaged, every subsequent institutional recommendation becomes harder to accept at face value, regardless of its actual evidential basis.

None of these factors make the underlying claim less false. But they do explain why it finds receptive audiences.

## 4. Verdict

**False.** Vitamin K exists. It is a fat-soluble vitamin with a well-documented biochemical function, a research history stretching back nearly a century, and a clear, evidence-based role in the prevention of life-threatening bleeding in newborn infants. The newborn injection is not unnecessary, not a vaccine, not a delivery mechanism for potassium, and not a product of Big Pharma invention. The claim that vitamin K "does not exist" rests on a straightforward category error. The broader claim that the injection is dangerous lacks any credible evidentiary foundation; the documented danger runs in the opposite direction — refusing the injection exposes newborns to an 81-fold increased risk of late Vitamin K Deficiency Bleeding, which can cause fatal intracranial haemorrhage.

Snopes rated the claim "Vitamin K does not exist" as **False** in June 2023.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The vitamin K refusal movement illuminates several dynamics that recur across health misinformation episodes. The most important is what we might call the **extension problem**: once a particular community has successfully disrupted parental confidence in one well-evidenced intervention (childhood vaccines), the logical and rhetorical infrastructure of that disruption can be redirected at others. The vitamin K shot is collateral damage from the vaccine wars, in the sense that it has become a target not because there is any serious scientific controversy about its value, but because the social infrastructure for doubting it already exists.

This creates a genuine clinical problem. Unlike vaccine hesitancy, where the harms are often diffuse (reduced herd immunity, slower outbreak response), vitamin K refusal produces direct, individual, and rapid consequences. A baby who does not receive prophylactic vitamin K and goes on to develop late VKDB may experience irreversible brain damage or die within weeks of birth. Paediatric emergency physicians have documented cases in the United States, United Kingdom, and Australia in which infants presented critically ill with intracranial bleeds attributable to VKDB, and in the majority of those cases parental refusal of the newborn injection was listed as the precipitating factor.

The second dimension worth noting is the **naming convention trap**. Medical and scientific nomenclature is dense, historically contingent, and full of apparent anomalies. Vitamins were named in rough alphabetical order as they were discovered; the letter K was simply the next available designation at the time vitamin K was identified. Potassium received its symbol from a completely separate tradition — the Latin and Germanic naming of elements by national scientific communities working independently of the vitamin researchers. These two K's coexist in the knowledge space without any meaningful connection, but the apparent coincidence is exploitable. This is a structural vulnerability of technical naming conventions that misinformation producers have learned to weaponise.

Third, the episode demonstrates the durability of claims that survive initial debunking. The vitamin K controversy in 2023 was not new; identical or closely related claims had circulated in 2019, 2020, and earlier. Snopes and AP had both previously addressed related misinformation about the injection's ingredients and safety. The 2023 wave represented not new misinformation but the recycling and amplification of existing misinformation through new channels, triggered by a high-profile figure's endorsement. This is a recurring pattern: debunking does not permanently retire a false claim; it establishes a record that must be maintained and promoted actively.

Finally, the demographic context: studies consistently show that vitamin K refusal is disproportionately concentrated among parents who are educated, white, high-income, and ideologically invested in "natural" childbirth and parenting philosophies. This is not a phenomenon of ignorance in the conventional sense. It is a phenomenon of motivated reasoning within communities that have the social capital and confidence to challenge institutional advice — and whose confidence sometimes outpaces their scientific understanding.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes addressed the specific "vitamin K does not exist" iteration of this claim in June 2023, rating it **False** in a piece by investigative journalist Alex Kasprak. The Snopes treatment centred on establishing the basic scientific facts about vitamin K — its discovery history, its biochemical role, its distinction from potassium — and documenting the specific tweets and social media posts that propelled the claim into viral circulation.

The Associated Press had previously addressed related claims, notably in a May 2023 fact-check of misinformation about benzyl alcohol (a preservative used in the injectable formulation), which posts had incorrectly characterised as dangerous. AP found the preservative concentration in the injection to be well within established safety limits.

The CDC's resource library on vitamin K deficiency bleeding serves as one of the most authoritative and accessible debunking anchors; its fact sheets are among the most-cited sources in any correction of this misinformation. The agency's direct acknowledgment that "ALL newborns have low levels of vitamin K" — a sentence that some anti-injection campaigners have tried to reframe as evidence of a manufactured deficiency — is actually a direct statement of normal newborn physiology.

One challenge fact-checkers face with this claim is the multiplicity of its variants. The core claim — that the injection is unnecessary or harmful — branches into at least four sub-claims: (a) vitamin K doesn't exist; (b) vitamin K is just potassium; (c) the injection contains dangerous ingredients; (d) newborns don't really need it because the "deficiency" is artificially defined. Each variant requires a different rebuttal architecture. Correcting (a) requires basic chemistry. Correcting (b) requires historical explanation of naming conventions. Correcting (c) requires toxicological analysis of specific ingredients. Correcting (d) requires engagement with the epidemiology of VKDB and the statistical evidence for the injection's protective effect.

The broader lesson for media literacy practitioners is that health misinformation about newborn care is particularly resistant to correction because it is inseparable from deeply held beliefs about bodily autonomy, natural parenting, and institutional trust. The claim does not need to survive scientific scrutiny to survive publicly; it only needs to seem plausible enough to a parent in a state of heightened vigilance and minimal sleep, navigating a hostile information environment at one of the most consequential moments of their life. Fact-checkers who address only the factual errors without engaging the emotional and social context of the claim tend to produce corrections that persuade those already inclined to be persuaded, and fail to reach those who need it most.
