# Folk Remedies for Warts — Burying Toads, Rubbing Pennies, and Other Persistent Cures

## 1. The Claim

Few domains of folk medicine have generated as rich and globally distributed a body of cures as the common wart. Across continents and centuries, communities have developed elaborate prescriptions for eliminating these small, rough skin lesions — and the striking consistency with which people report their methods "worked" has sustained each tradition against the accumulated skepticism of modern dermatology.

In North American folk tradition, the most famous remedies involve transfer or burial: rubbing a penny on the wart and then burying it at a crossroads so that whoever finds the coin acquires the wart; rubbing a piece of raw meat or a potato half on the wart before burying it at midnight; or, in the Ozark traditions documented by folklorist Vance Randolph in the 1940s, burying a toad at a crossroads under a full moon. European variants include selling the wart to someone willing to take it for a farthing, counting the warts and telling the number to a stranger without revealing why, spitting on them at dawn before speaking, or washing the affected area in water drawn from a churchyard well. South Asian and East Asian folk medicine has comparable traditions involving specific plant applications, ritualized disposal, and the timing of remedies according to lunar calendars.

In Mark Twain's *The Adventures of Tom Sawyer* (1876), Tom and Huckleberry Finn debate the merits of spunk-water (stagnant swamp water) versus dead cats for wart removal — using dead cats by swinging them over a grave at midnight. These are not Twain's inventions; they reflect the genuine folk pharmacopoeia of 19th-century rural Missouri. The remedies he catalogued were real cultural artifacts of a community that, like practically every human community, had developed empirically-tested (if poorly controlled) solutions to a common and stubborn minor ailment.

What all these remedies share is a claim to reliably eliminate warts — often within days or weeks — without the chemical intervention now employed by modern dermatology. The mechanisms implied vary: transfer of the wart to another object (the penny, the stranger, the toad), sympathetic magic (the wart rots as the buried potato rots), divine intercession, or undefined effects of ritual timing. In most versions, the practitioner's past success with a remedy is offered as primary evidence. Grandmothers who cured cousins with raw meat; fathers who buried pennies and never had warts again; communities in which one specific local healer's ritual was considered uniquely effective.

The remedies cluster remarkably consistently around a few structural motifs regardless of cultural context: (1) do something to the wart, (2) dispose of or transfer the thing you used, (3) do not look back. This structure is found in Appalachian wart-charming, in British cunning-folk tradition, in West African ritual systems, and in South American folk curing traditions, suggesting either deep historical diffusion of a shared complex or independent convergent invention driven by a common observable reality.


## 2. What's Actually True

Warts are caused exclusively by human papillomavirus (HPV), a double-stranded DNA virus with more than 200 identified genotypes infecting specific epithelial tissue types. Doorbar, Quint, Banks, Bravo, Stoler, Broker, and Stanley (2012), writing in *Vaccine*, provide a comprehensive mapping of HPV types to specific clinical presentations: types 1, 2, 3, 4, and 7 are primarily responsible for common cutaneous warts (verruca vulgaris), plantar warts (verruca plantaris), and flat warts (verruca plana), depending on the genotype and location of infection.

The virus infects keratinocytes — the primary cells of the outer epithelial layer — through micro-abrasions or breaks in the skin, establishing replication in the stratum basale before producing viral particles that migrate to the surface. The immune system eventually mounts a cell-mediated immune response that clears the infection; this process requires recognition of viral antigens presented by infected keratinocytes, followed by cytotoxic T-lymphocyte activity that eliminates the infected cell population. The critical point for evaluating folk remedies is this: the immune response is almost entirely self-completing and operates on its own schedule, typically over months to years.

Massing and Epstein (1963), in a landmark prospective study published in *Archives of Dermatology*, followed a cohort of children and adults with untreated common warts over a two-year observational period. They found that approximately 65% of warts resolved spontaneously within two years without any medical treatment. Subsequent studies refined this finding: the Cochrane systematic review on local treatments for cutaneous warts by Gibbs, Harvey, Sterling, and Stark (2002) reported that placebo arms in wart treatment trials typically observed remission rates of 30–40% within three months. This means that in any given three-month period, roughly a third of warts will resolve regardless of what the practitioner does — providing a constant stream of apparent successes for any remedy applied.

Effective medical treatments are more efficient than waiting but operate on genuinely different timescales than the folk remedies suggest. Salicylic acid preparations (15–26% concentrations) achieve wart clearance through keratolysis, chemically softening and removing infected tissue layers over repeated daily application. Cryotherapy using liquid nitrogen destroys infected keratinocytes via intracellular ice crystal formation and inflammatory response. Cockayne et al. (2011), in a randomized controlled trial published in the *BMJ*, found salicylic acid and cryotherapy broadly equivalent in efficacy for plantar warts, each producing complete cure rates around 14–17% at 12 weeks — substantially better than placebo, but both operating well below the ~65% spontaneous two-year resolution baseline documented by Massing and Epstein.

No toad, penny, raw potato, or midnight burial ritual alters the immune biology governing HPV clearance. Human HPV subtypes are species-specific — they do not infect toads or other non-human animals, cannot be physically transferred through metal contact, and are not influenced by sympathetic association between the wart and a buried object. The bumpy appearance of toad skin — a visual resemblance to verrucae that clearly anchored that particular folk remedy — arises from parotoid glands secreting defensive toxins, not from viral warts. The two conditions share a surface texture only.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The claim-specific mechanism sustaining folk wart remedies is what might be accurately called **spontaneous-resolution attribution capture**: because warts resolve on their own at high base rates across predictable time windows, any remedy applied during that window receives apparent causal credit for the resolution.

This is a more specific epistemic failure than generic confirmation bias. The natural history data of Massing and Epstein (1963) creates a situation where the base rate of "treatment followed by resolution within two years" is genuinely high — approximately 65% — even when the treatment has zero biological effect. A practitioner who tries a folk remedy at the emergence of a new wart has a roughly 35–40% chance of the wart resolving within three months, and a 65% chance within two years. Among any community of practitioners, this produces a steady supply of confirming testimonials. The failures — warts that persisted despite buried pennies or transferred toads — receive significantly less social transmission than successes, because they generate no noteworthy narrative. Only the successes become stories worth passing on.

This basic mechanism is compounded by a small but real body of research on psychological mediation of HPV clearance rates. Surman, Gottlieb, Hackett, and Silverberg (1973) published a controlled study in *Archives of General Psychiatry* demonstrating that hypnotic suggestion produced statistically significant wart regression relative to untreated control warts on the same patients — using a within-subject design in which suggested warts were targets and control warts on the same body served as comparators. The effect, while debated and never fully mechanistically explained, has been attributed to indirect immune pathway modulation, reduced scratching and picking behavior (which spreads HPV and delays clearance), or stress reduction effects on T-cell activity. The most conservative interpretation: ritualized attention to a wart may have minor indirect biological effects even when the stated mechanism is pure fiction.

Cultural transmission adds a final layer of epistemic distortion. Folk medicine traditions selectively preserve methods associated with positive outcomes through the operation of what sociologists of medicine call confirmatory narrative selection: the penny buried and the wart that went away becomes a family story repeated across generations; the penny buried and the wart that persisted becomes a forgotten failure. The intergenerational archive of folk cures is thus systematically enriched with confirming cases while failures are lost to informal non-transmission.


## 4. Verdict

**AUGMENTED**: Folk remedies for warts — burying toads, rubbing pennies, applying potatoes at midnight, transferring warts to strangers — are not effective through any identified biological mechanism. No controlled study has demonstrated efficacy above the high spontaneous remission rate that characterizes untreated warts.

The durable cultural persistence of these remedies across disconnected cultures and centuries is explained by the remarkable coincidence of ritual timing and natural resolution. Because roughly two-thirds of warts resolve within two years without treatment, and because people seek remedies when warts are bothersome (a period that typically overlaps with the immune response timeline), any remedy will generate a consistent supply of apparent successes. The global folk pharmacopoeia of wart cures is a monument to empirical inference applied without appropriate baseline controls.

The appropriate clinical response to a wart is one of three: watch and wait (valid for most warts in adults, who have typically lower spontaneous resolution rates than children), salicylic acid preparation (over-the-counter, effective, requiring consistency over weeks to months), or dermatological consultation for persistent or spreading lesions (cryotherapy, immunotherapy, or other medical options). The penny burial is not recommended by any dermatological guideline, though it carries no direct risk of harm — and the small unresolved literature on hypnotic suggestion opens a sliver of theoretical possibility that ritualized attention may modestly accelerate immune response through indirect pathways.


## 5. The Wider Picture

Wart folk remedies occupy a structurally distinctive position in the ecology of medical mythology: they are uniquely positioned to perpetuate themselves because they are applied to a condition with a high natural recovery rate. This makes them fundamentally different from folk remedies for conditions that do not spontaneously resolve. An ineffective remedy for type 2 diabetes or structural scoliosis generates far fewer persistent believers, because those conditions do not normalize on their own within a comparable timeframe. Warts do, reliably, and this biological peculiarity becomes the generative engine of folk cure transmission.

The convergent "transfer" motif across unrelated folk traditions — the idea that a wart can be ritually directed to another person, animal, or object — warrants particular attention. This structural similarity across European, North American, East Asian, and West African traditions suggests either deep historical diffusion or independent convergent invention from a common observable reality. The convergence point is HPV's genuine transmissibility: warts do, in an unritualized sense, transfer between people through skin contact. Communities observing family clustering of warts correctly identified a real property — contagiousness — and logically extended the observation to the inference that warts could be deliberately directed to a new host. The folk observation correctly named the transmissibility while attributing it to the wrong mechanism.

Medical anthropologists have documented the social and psychological functions served by folk remedies beyond pharmacological effects. Structured remedies provide a framework for action in the face of a persistent symptom that generates social discomfort; they give the patient agency over a condition that otherwise involves waiting; they involve community participation (the elder who prescribes the treatment, the stranger who "purchases" the wart, the family member who administers the ritual). These functions persist independently of efficacy and explain why folk medicine traditions survive alongside accessible modern dermatology rather than being displaced by it.

The persistence of wart folk remedies in the era of widely available salicylic acid preparations has lessons for public health communication. Belief in traditional remedies does not vanish when effective alternatives are available; it coexists with evidence-based options, often applied sequentially — the folk remedy first (familiar, free, culturally validated), then the chemist's preparation (when the folk remedy's timeline expires), then the clinic. Understanding this sequential behavior is necessary for designing health communication that meets patients where their actual decision-making begins rather than assuming that available evidence translates into behavioral change.

The natural history data on wart resolution also carries an implication often overlooked in clinical guideline discussions: watchful waiting is a valid clinical strategy for most common warts in otherwise healthy patients. Given the 65% two-year spontaneous resolution rate documented by Massing and Epstein (1963), and given that no treatment has demonstrated dramatically superior efficacy without side effects, dermatological guidelines now often recommend watchful waiting as the first-line approach — a formal clinical vindication of doing nothing that the folk remedies, paradoxically, have been performing all along.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not published a dedicated fact-check on folk remedies for wart removal, and the specific traditions discussed here — the Ozark toad burial, the penny at the crossroads, the Twain-era dead-cat cure — fall outside the typical scope of contemporary viral-media fact-checking. The absence is somewhat ironic, given that wart folk medicine represents some of the most geographically distributed and culturally entrenched medical misinformation in human experience.

The broader question of wart treatment efficacy is well-addressed by institutional medical information sources: the NHS, Mayo Clinic, and American Academy of Dermatology all provide clearly written guidance that salicylic acid and cryotherapy outperform unverified traditional remedies in controlled conditions, and that most common warts resolve spontaneously regardless of intervention. These resources address the clinical question (what works?) competently, but do not engage the cultural question (why do folk remedies persist so robustly despite accessible alternatives?).

The structural reason for the Snopes gap is that folk wart cures do not circulate primarily as viral social-media assertions subject to fact-checking; they circulate as intergenerational family knowledge embedded in practical tradition rather than as shareable claims with verifiable source attributions. Standard fact-checking methodology operates predominantly on explicable propositional claims that can be compared to evidence, and folk medicine persists in a different epistemic mode — transmitted through performed demonstration and personal testimony rather than through assertions of efficacy that can be independently tested. This makes folk cures invisible to the standard fact-check pipeline while remaining culturally active across generations.
