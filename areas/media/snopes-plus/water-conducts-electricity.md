# "Pure Water Conducts Electricity"


## 1. The Claim

Water conducts electricity. The claim is presented as common knowledge and as the basis for a safety precaution: avoid mixing electricity and water because water is an electrical conductor and contact with an energized source in a wet environment creates a path for current to flow through the human body.

The safety advice—keep electrical appliances away from water, do not use electronics near baths or pools, do not operate electrical equipment in rain—is correct and important. The physical mechanism underlying it is not. The implicit model behind "water conducts electricity" is that water itself is the conductor. This is wrong. Water—pure, deionized water—is an extremely poor electrical conductor. What conducts electricity in virtually every real-world water context is what is dissolved in the water: ions from salts, minerals, and biological compounds.

The claim appears in school physics and chemistry curricula, safety demonstrations, and everyday explanations. Because the safety advice is correct—it is genuinely dangerous to mix live electrical circuits with tap water, seawater, or any water you would actually encounter—the incorrect physical explanation propagates without correction. The practical truth is preserved; the physical mechanism is not.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The resistivity of pure water** is approximately 18.2 MΩ·cm (megaohms per centimeter) at 25°C under conditions of maximum purity (ultrapure or Type 1 water). This is the resistivity achieved by laboratory deionized water after multiple stages of purification—reverse osmosis, ion exchange, and UV sterilization. For comparison: copper has a resistivity of about 1.7 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·cm; seawater has a resistivity of roughly 20–25 Ω·cm; tap water ranges from approximately 1,000 to 50,000 Ω·cm depending on mineral content. Ultrapure water at 18.2 MΩ·cm is seven to eight orders of magnitude less conductive than seawater, and approximately as resistive as some grades of glass.

In electrical terms, ultrapure water is for most practical purposes an insulator. You could, in principle, submerge an energized circuit in sufficiently pure water and have minimal current flow. Semiconductor fabrication facilities use ultrapure water for this reason—to clean wafer surfaces without introducing electrical hazards or ionic contamination.

**Why does water conduct at all, even poorly?** Pure water undergoes slight autoionization: H₂O molecules dissociate into H⁺ (or, more precisely, H₃O⁺, the hydronium ion) and OH⁻ ions at a rate determined by the water's ionic product constant Kw = 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C. This means that in one liter of pure water, 10⁻⁷ moles of H⁺ and 10⁻⁷ moles of OH⁻ ions are present—an extremely small concentration. These ions can carry electrical charge under an applied field, which is why pure water is not a perfect insulator—but the charge carrier concentration is so low that the conductivity is negligible by practical standards.

**What actually conducts electricity in real water** is dissolved ions. When sodium chloride (table salt) dissolves in water, it dissociates into Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. When calcium carbonate (limestone) or magnesium sulfate dissolves in hard tap water, it contributes Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, CO₃²⁻, and SO₄²⁻ ions. Seawater contains approximately 35 grams of dissolved salts per liter, dominated by Na⁺ and Cl⁻, giving it high conductivity. Human blood plasma contains electrolytes at concentrations calibrated for biological function—roughly 154 mEq/L of total ionic content—giving it substantial conductivity.

The conductivity of any water sample is a direct measure of its dissolved ion content. Water quality professionals measure electrical conductivity in microsiemens per centimeter (μS/cm) as a proxy for total dissolved solids. Pure water sits near zero μS/cm; tap water ranges from roughly 50–500 μS/cm; seawater is around 50,000 μS/cm. The scale spans three orders of magnitude, entirely determined by dissolved content, not by "water" per se.

**The mechanism of electrocution in water** follows directly: when you drop a live electrical appliance into a bathtub, the current that flows through the water to reach you flows through dissolved ions—soap residue, skin oils, minerals in tap water, and particularly the electrolyte ions at the skin surface and in sweat. The water in the bathtub is a conducting medium not because of water but because of everything in it. In a hypothetical bathtub filled with perfectly pure water, an appliance dropped into it would not create the same immediate hazard—though the concept is purely theoretical, as contact between pure water and any real surface, including human skin, introduces ions within seconds.

**This is why the Dead Sea—the saltiest body of water on Earth—is more dangerous in a lightning storm than a freshwater lake** (from a conductivity standpoint), and why seawater swimmers are at greater electrocution risk from improperly grounded underwater pool lighting than swimmers in rainwater pools.

**This is also why distilled water is used in car batteries** as a non-conducting diluent: the sulfuric acid electrolyte that carries current is added separately, and using tap water would introduce mineral deposits and ionic interference.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The belief is a reasonable inference from consistent experience, and its practical consequences are aligned with the correct safety behavior.

**Consistent experiential correlation.** Every time a person has encountered electricity near water in their lived experience—kitchen accidents, flooded basements, warning signs at swimming pools—the water involved was tap water, seawater, sweat-covered skin, or rain. In every real-world case, the water was conductive because it was laden with dissolved ions. The inference "water conducts electricity" is built from experience that is accurate in all accessible cases. The clean conceptual distinction between pure water and water-with-dissolved-solids is not accessible to everyday observation.

**The safety message is correct.** The behavioral conclusion—keep electricity away from water—is the right safety precaution. Teachers, parents, and safety instructors transmitting this message have no reason to qualify it with a chemistry lecture about ionic dissociation. The practical rule is exactly right; the chemical mechanism is unnecessarily complex for the safety context in which the claim typically circulates. Accuracy about mechanism has no safety benefit here, and imprecision has no safety cost. This is precisely the condition under which scientific inaccuracies can persist indefinitely in cultural transmission.

**The simplification is defensible in most educational contexts.** For a basic electricity safety lesson, "water conducts electricity, don't mix them" is accurate enough for the purpose. The more precise version—"dissolved ions in water conduct electricity, and virtually all water you will ever encounter has enough dissolved ions to be dangerous"—conveys the same safety lesson. Chemistry curricula that need to make the distinction do so; general safety education that doesn't need to has no incentive to complicate the message.

**The metaphorical conflation of water and what's in it.** In common usage, "water" refers to the liquid encountered in daily life—tap water, bottled water, ocean water, rain. It does not refer to H₂O molecules in chemically pure isolation. This is a reasonable everyday usage. The scientific distinction between "water" (the molecular compound H₂O) and "water" (the liquid people interact with, which is a solution containing ions, dissolved gases, organic compounds, and microorganisms) is not made in everyday language. The claim is accurate within the everyday usage; it is inaccurate within the chemical usage. Most science communication fails to flag the register shift between these two uses of the same word.

**Educational misconception propagation.** Physics demonstrations that show that water conducts electricity—by connecting a circuit through a beaker of tap water—accurately demonstrate that tap water conducts. They do not demonstrate that H₂O conducts, but they are typically presented as though they do. Students who experience this demonstration correctly retain "water conducts electricity" without any reason to doubt the formulation.


## 4. Verdict

**Classification: Distorted but Grounded**

The claim is wrong at the level of physical mechanism but right at the level of practical safety advice. This is a clean case of distortion-from-truth: a simplified rule that is accurate in the domain where it is applied (safety advice about real-world water) is misunderstood as a claim about the physical properties of the compound H₂O.

**What is physically accurate:**
- Real-world water—tap water, seawater, groundwater, body fluids, rainwater—conducts electricity well enough to be dangerous in contact with live electrical circuits
- Dissolved ions are mobile charge carriers in solution and produce substantial electrical conductivity
- Human bodies contain sufficient electrolytes (blood, sweat, urine) that wet skin or immersion dramatically reduces the resistance of the path from a live circuit to ground
- For all practical safety purposes, "keep electricity away from water" is correct and complete

**What is physically inaccurate:**
- Pure (deionized) water is not an electrical conductor in any practical sense—resistivity ~18 MΩ·cm
- It is dissolved ions that conduct, not water molecules
- The electrical conductivity of a water sample is determined almost entirely by its dissolved solid content, not by the water itself
- Chemically pure water is used in applications *because* of its low conductivity

**The precise statement:** Dissolved ions in water conduct electricity. Water—the molecular compound H₂O—does not, to any practical degree. The safety advice ("electricity and water don't mix") is correct because essentially all water encountered in practice is a solution of conductive ions. But the mechanism matters for understanding why, and for understanding why semiconductor plants use ultrapure water, why distilled water is used in batteries, and why resistivity is a water purity metric.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The chemistry of electrical conductivity in aqueous solutions connects to several important areas of science and engineering.

**Electrochemistry** is founded on the behavior of ions in solution. Faraday's laws of electrolysis (1833) described quantitatively how dissolved ions carry current and are deposited at electrodes. The entire field of battery technology—from the Voltaic pile to lithium-ion—depends on understanding that the electrolyte (the ion-conducting medium between electrodes) carries current via ions, not via water. A lithium-ion battery uses a lithium salt dissolved in an organic solvent as its electrolyte; the organic solvent is deliberately chosen for its ionic dissociation properties, not for any intrinsic conductivity of the solvent itself.

**Water quality and environmental monitoring** uses electrical conductivity as a primary measurement. Conductivity probes measure total dissolved solids in drinking water (municipal targets are typically below 500 mg/L in the U.S.), in wastewater treatment effluent, and in natural water bodies. Elevated conductivity signals pollution, agricultural runoff (fertilizer ions), or brine intrusion. The measurement is precise because it is measuring what is dissolved, not the water.

**Semiconductor fabrication** uses ultrapure water (UPW) with resistivity above 18 MΩ·cm for wafer rinsing because ionic contamination at the parts-per-trillion level can disrupt transistor function. The industry spends substantial resources achieving and maintaining this purity—purification systems that remove everything that makes water conductive—because the resulting liquid is genuinely non-conductive at the scales that matter for microelectronics.

**Biological conductivity** is an extension of the same principle. The electrical activity of neurons, muscles, and the heart depends on ion gradients across cell membranes—sodium, potassium, calcium, and chloride ions moving through protein channels according to electrochemical gradients. An ECG or EEG works by detecting the electrical fields generated by these ion movements in biological tissue. The conductivity of biological fluids—blood, cerebrospinal fluid, sweat—is what makes these measurements possible, and it is entirely the product of dissolved electrolytes.

**The safety implication restated precisely:** Electrical danger in wet environments comes from the specific conductivity of the water you are in contact with. Tap water, seawater, and sweat are conductive because of their ion content. The hazard is real. But it is not because of a property intrinsic to the water molecule—it is because of what water almost always contains in real-world environments. This doesn't change any safety practice; it clarifies the mechanism.

**Lightning and water** provides a useful case for the distinction. Lightning strikes on bodies of water conduct laterally through the water surface because the surface layer is conductive (dissolved ions, suspended minerals). Swimmers and boaters are advised to exit the water during lightning because the lateral conduction from a nearby strike can reach them through the conductive aqueous medium. The mechanism is ionic conduction. In perfectly pure water—which no natural body of water approaches—the lateral conduction would be negligible. In the real ocean or lake, it is substantial.

**Desalination and conductivity measurement** further illustrates the point. The purpose of desalination is to remove dissolved salts from seawater to produce potable water. The process—whether reverse osmosis, distillation, or electrodialysis—does not transform the water molecule; it removes ions. The resulting product water is measurably less conductive than seawater, with conductivity falling as salt concentration falls. Engineers monitor the desalination product's electrical conductivity as a quality metric precisely because conductivity is a reliable proxy for ionic content. Zero conductivity would mean zero dissolved solids—the limiting case of ultrapure water, which is not a goal of desalination (a small residual mineral content is typically maintained for palatability and health) but which illustrates that the relationship between water and conductivity is mediated entirely by what the water contains.

**Electrolysis in industry** depends on precisely controlled ionic concentrations in aqueous solutions. The chlor-alkali process—which produces chlorine gas and sodium hydroxide from brine—runs a current through a concentrated salt solution. It is the sodium and chloride ions that carry current between electrodes; the water molecules are the solvent, not the conductor. Scaling up or down the ion concentration directly scales conductivity. This relationship is so predictable and consistent that industrial electrochemical processes are designed and controlled through ionic concentration management, not through any manipulation of the water molecule itself.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not addressed the pure water conductivity claim, which is perhaps expected—it circulates as received scientific knowledge in safety education rather than as a contested political or viral claim. The platform's focus on viral misinformation means that well-established scientific misconceptions embedded in educational curricula and safety messaging are outside its typical scope.

This represents a broader challenge for science fact-checking: the most deeply embedded scientific misconceptions are not the ones spreading on social media, but the ones encoded in generations of curricula, safety posters, and common-knowledge frameworks. "Water conducts electricity" has been transmitted through elementary school chemistry, high school physics demonstrations, and every electrical safety training program without anyone having a particular reason to challenge it. It is not misinformation in the sense of a claim someone made falsely and strategically; it is a simplification that stuck.

The gap between "accurate for safety purposes in all real-world contexts" and "accurate as a description of H₂O's physical properties" is exactly the kind of distinction that does not appear in standard fact-checking workflows. It is not a politically contested claim, not a health hazard in itself, and not associated with any particular bad actor. It is simply a misconception—widely shared, practically harmless, physically incorrect. The value of correcting it lies not in preventing any specific harm but in cultivating the kind of precise causal reasoning that distinguishes what a phenomenon is from what it happens to be associated with.
