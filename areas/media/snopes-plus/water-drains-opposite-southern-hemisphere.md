# Water drains in opposite directions in the two hemispheres (variant)


## 1. The Claim

This variant of the hemisphere-drain myth has a particular narrative shape: the speaker has traveled to or returned from a Southern Hemisphere country—Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Argentina, Brazil—and reports either having personally observed the "reversed" drain direction or having been told by locals that drains there swirl clockwise (the opposite of the counterclockwise swirling the traveler expects in the Northern Hemisphere). In the tourist-activation version, equatorial attractions in Kenya, Ecuador, and Uganda offer live demonstrations ostensibly proving the reversal at the equatorial crossing point.

In the Southern Hemisphere framing, the claim often appears in a hedged form: "I know it sounds like a myth, but I actually saw it when I was in Australia." This first-person testimony variant is specifically resistant to fact-checking because the claim now rests on personal experience rather than hearsay. The correction not only has to establish that the physics does not support the claim but also has to explain how a personally observed instance of clockwise drainage in the Southern Hemisphere could occur without Coriolis causation—which requires engaging with the concept of base rates and local determinism in a way that does not directly challenge the traveler's veracity.

The Southern Hemisphere variant also circulates in the specific form of the Australian-toilet-flush joke, popularized by an extended scene in the *Simpsons* television episode "Bart vs. Australia" (Season 6, Episode 16, 1995), in which Bart calls a boy in Australia to verify that toilets flush backwards in the Southern Hemisphere, and the boy confirms they do. This cultural insertion gave the myth a comedic framework that made it easier to transmit as a knowing "science is funny" anecdote rather than a straightforward factual claim, further insulating it from direct correction.


## 2. What's Actually True

The physics underlying this variant is identical to the Northern Hemisphere framing and is covered in detail in the companion entry `water-drains-opposite-hemisphere.md`. The core physics verdict: the Coriolis force at household drain scale is overwhelmed by local factors by a factor of approximately 300 or more (Rossby number ~300 under typical household conditions). This is true in both hemispheres, at all latitudes above approximately 5 degrees from the equator where the Coriolis parameter is non-negligible.

Several additional factual points are specific to the Southern Hemisphere framing and merit direct address:

**Australian Toilets Do Not Systematically Flush Clockwise**: Empirical investigation of toilet and drain behavior in Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa finds exactly what the physics predicts: there is no consistent directional preference. Both clockwise and counterclockwise bowl rotation occur, determined by the design of the toilet bowl (particularly the angle and geometry of the inlet jets), rather than by hemisphere. Australian toilet manufacturers have confirmed in consumer documentation and engineering specifications that flush rotation is a design parameter, not a geophysical one. In some Australian commercial toilet designs, jets are deliberately angled to produce a clockwise flush for drainage efficiency reasons; in others, counterclockwise rotation is produced by the same design logic. The hemisphere has no detectable input.

**The Shapiro Experiment and the Southern Hemisphere Replication**: As discussed in the companion entry, Ascher Shapiro's 1962 controlled experiment published in *Nature* demonstrated counterclockwise drainage in the Northern Hemisphere under extraordinary precautions (1.8-m tank, 24-hour settling time, sealed environment). A complementary experiment was conducted in 1965 by Lloyd Trefethen and colleagues at the University of New South Wales in Sydney, published in *Nature* the same year, demonstrating clockwise drainage under equivalent precautions in the Southern Hemisphere (Trefethen, L.M., Bilger, R.W., Faulkner, P.T., Shields, R.E., & Wipperman, F.L., 1965, *Nature*, 207, 1084–1085). These paired experiments provide the strongest possible evidence that Coriolis effects can influence drain direction in a small container—but also that detecting this influence requires conditions so extreme (24-hour settling, large tank, controlled environment) as to be incompatible with any everyday observation. The takeaway is not "so the claim is right" but rather "so the claim could only ever be verified in this very specific experimental protocol."

**The Equatorial Tourist Demonstrations**: Several operators offering "equatorial crossing" demonstrations in Kenya (near Nanyuki, claimed to be on the equator), Uganda, Ecuador (the Mitad del Mundo monument near Quito), and elsewhere have built commercial entertainments around supposedly demonstrating the Coriolis drain reversal at the equatorial line. Observers have documented the techniques involved: the demonstrator fills a bowl of water, walks a few meters to one side of the painted or marked "equatorial line," and allows water to drain, with clockwise rotation; repeats on the other side, with counterclockwise rotation. Variations on the technique include placing a floating object in the bowl (a leaf or small float) to make the rotation more visible.

The effect is produced by the demonstrator's motion and the corresponding asymmetric angular momentum imparted to the water. When the demonstrator walks northward toward the "northern hemisphere" side and pauses, the water in the bowl has received a net angular momentum from the carried motion; when the stopper is removed, that angular momentum produces counter-clockwise rotation in the Northern Hemisphere side. The rotation direction is controlled entirely by the direction of walking (hence the angular momentum of the carried water) and not by any atmospheric or geophysical effect. Tipping the bowl slightly while walking produces the same effect. This has been confirmed by observers who have performed the demonstration themselves, varying walk direction while in the same geographic location, and obtaining both clockwise and counterclockwise results at will (Fraser, A.B., Penn State Meteorology resource archive, 2000s).

**The Latitude of the Demonstrations Matters**: At the true equator, the Coriolis parameter f = 0 exactly (since sin 0° = 0). If the Coriolis effect were the operating mechanism at equatorial demonstrations, there would be no defined direction of rotation—the effect would be zero, not reversed. The dramatic "reversal at the line" performance thus has a logical inconsistency built in even on its own terms: if Coriolis physics were responsible, the equatorial marker itself would be a point of zero effect, not a transition point. The fact that the demonstrations show a clean reversal precisely at or very near the equator itself is evidence that the effect is being mechanically produced rather than naturally observed.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The Southern Hemisphere variant carries distinct psychological dynamics that make it particularly persistent.

**Personal Experience as Epistemic Override**: The most force behind this variant comes from the personal-observation form: "I saw it in Australia." This is not a hearsay claim susceptible to the usual objection that it was just something read online. It is an autobiographical memory, which human cognition treats as privileged epistemic data. The traveler did observe water draining clockwise in at least some cases in Australia—this is almost certainly true, because approximately half of all drains behave in either direction depending on local conditions, so any sample of observed drains in any hemisphere will include both directions. The traveler selectively encoded the instances that confirmed the expected pattern (clockwise in Southern Hemisphere) and either did not observe or did not encode counterclockwise instances, which would have been anomalous and therefore cognitively uncomfortable.

This is a specific instance of confirmation bias operating on autobiographical memory formation in real time: the traveler entered Australia already expecting clockwise drains and observed drain behavior through that expectation, processing confirming observations as evidence and either not noticing or cognitively explaining away disconfirming ones. The resulting false memory—"I saw it personally"—is not a deliberate lie but a genuine artifact of how expectations shape perception and memory encoding (Loftus, E.F., 1996, *Memory: Surprising New Insights into How We Remember and Why We Forget*, Da Capo Press).

**The Cultural Weight of Travel Authenticity**: Travel exposure carries epistemic authority in social contexts. A claim prefaced with "when I was in New Zealand, I noticed" is harder to challenge than "I heard that in New Zealand." The speaker has paid the experiential and financial cost of visiting the Southern Hemisphere, which creates an implicit social claim to accuracy that makes overt correction feel like a personal challenge rather than a factual discussion. This social dynamic makes drain-direction myths more durable when they carry the "I was there" framing than when they circulate purely as internet claims.

**Epistemic Novelty—The Visitor's Gaze Effect**: There is an underappreciated dimension of how travel changes epistemological priors that this myth illustrates well. Travelers to foreign continents arrive in a state of heightened epistemic openness—everything is new, everything is potentially different, and unusual observations are expected and welcomed rather than triggined skepticism. This heightened openness is generally valuable for learning and cultural absorption, but it also reduces the activation of normal skeptical filters. A claim that would trigger critical evaluation at home ("water drains in opposite directions?") is readily accepted in the foreign travel context because the traveler is actively seeking evidence of difference. The Southern Hemisphere variant of the drain myth specifically exploits the "everything is different here" cognitive posture of the traveler, offering a handy physical marker of hemispheric difference that fits the travel narrative perfectly.

This mechanism suggests that travel experiences are a less reliable source of factual physical claims than intuition suggests. The travel context systematically suppresses normal skepticism, particularly for claims that frame the destination as distinct from home. Science communication that addresses travel-context myth propagation specifically—rather than treating all belief formation as equivalent across contexts—would need to develop epistemically attuned interventions that acknowledge the genuine value of openness to different experience while distinguishing physical phenomena (which do not change between hemispheres at household scales) from genuinely culturally variable practices and perceptions.

**The *Simpsons* Cultural Scaffold**: The Bart vs. Australia episode is worth specific attention as a myth transmission vector. The episode presents the clockwise-Australia claim in a comedic framework that insulates it from challenge: it is presented as a stereotype Bart tests (implicitly, it is already a known claim) and confirmed by an Australian character. The episode's humor depends partly on the audience recognizing the "fact" as a real science claim about hemisphere effects, which means the viewer processes it as accurate scientific background rather than as fictional premise. This cultural encoding—comedy built on the premise being true—is a particularly effective myth-transmission mechanism because the fictional framing creates affect that encodes the claim in memory more strongly than informational exposure alone (Bradley, M.M., & Lang, P.J., 1999, *Psychological Review*, 106, 376–395, on emotional enhancement of memory encoding more broadly).


## 4. Verdict

**Rating: FALSE**

The claim that water drains in a specific, hemisphere-consistent direction due to the Coriolis effect is false regardless of hemispheric framing. In the Southern Hemisphere, both clockwise and counterclockwise drainage occur in household contexts, determined by local factors (basin geometry, residual water motion, fill asymmetry, temperature gradients) rather than by Earth's rotation. The Coriolis force is real and significant at meteorological and oceanographic scales, but its effect at household drain scale is overwhelmed by these local factors by a factor of hundreds to thousands.

Controlled laboratory experiments confirm that under extraordinary precautions (large tank, long settling time, sealed environment), Coriolis effects can be isolated and produce hemisphere-consistent drain direction. These experimental conditions do not describe ordinary household drain behavior. Tourist demonstrations purporting to show equatorial drain reversal achieve their results through demonstrator motion, not geophysical effects.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The cultural biogeography of this myth is instructive. The Northern Hemisphere version—"drains counterclockwise here, clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere"—is primarily a Northern Hemisphere myth, transmitted among people who have not visited the Southern Hemisphere and hold mental models of it as a mirror-world where things work in reverse. The Southern Hemisphere version—"I saw it in Australia"—is primarily an international traveler myth, activated by the specific cognitive and social dynamics of travel experience described in Section 3.

Australia holds a specific cultural role in the imaginations of North American and European audiences as a far, antipodean, and "upside-down" country, and this cultural framing predisposes visitors to expect and seek evidence of physical reversal. The same dynamic applies to a lesser extent to New Zealand, Argentina, and South Africa. The geographic concentration of the "I saw it" variant among returnees from Australia specifically reflects this cultural imaginary of Australia as the hemisphere-reversal country.

The Coriolis effect's actual Southern Hemisphere expressions are both real and impressive, but they are not visible in kitchen sinks. The major Southern Hemisphere cyclonic systems—the Roaring Forties, the Southern Ocean lows that drive circumglobal weather patterns, the Atlantic and Pacific subtropical highs with their clockwise circulation—are genuine expressions of the Coriolis effect at scales where it dominates. El Niño and La Niña climate phenomena involve large-scale ocean and atmosphere interactions in which Coriolis dynamics are central. The Antarctic Circumpolar Current, the world's largest ocean current and a critical component of global thermohaline circulation, partly owes its coherence to the Coriolis effect restraining north-south meanders and channeling westward momentum into the circumpolar band.

These large-scale phenomena are scientifically sophisticated and genuinely fascinating. Making them accessible and accurate in popular science communication is far more valuable than the drain-direction myth, which compresses the Coriolis effect to a domestic appliance scale where it is irrelevant and thereby trivializes one of the most important physical effects shaping Earth's climate.

The Southern Hemisphere in general suffers from underrepresentation in Northern Hemisphere science education and popular science, which may contribute to the readiness of Northern Hemisphere audiences to accept any dramatic physical claims about Southern Hemisphere physics as plausible by virtue of the hemisphere's general foreignness. Accurate science communication about Southern Hemisphere physical geography—the unique properties of Southern Hemisphere ocean circulation, the particularly intense effect of the ozone hole on Southern Hemisphere UV exposure, the different tilt of solar noon, the visibility of the Magellanic Clouds—would both correct specific myths and build the background knowledge that makes individual false claims less sticky.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Southern Hemisphere variant of the drain myth is addressed by Snopes as a separate article from the general Coriolis drain claim, recognizing that it circulates in distinct contexts and may be searched for separately. The verdict is consistently False, with the treatment of physics closely paralleling the Northern Hemisphere version.

One specific challenge for fact-checking the travel-experience form of this myth is that the fact-checker cannot directly address the traveler's autobiographical claim. Stating "you did not see what you think you saw in Australia" is a neurologically accurate description of what happened (confirmation bias acted on the experience) but is socially and rhetorically counterproductive when communicated directly, since it is perceived as accusatory. More effective approaches frame the correction positively: providing information about the actual factors that determine drain direction (basin geometry, residual motion), noting that those factors vary unpredictably and produce both directions in any location, and framing the observation as accurate but the causal interpretation as incorrect. This allows the traveler to retain their autobiographical memory as real while updating the explanatory framework that the memory is filed under.

The *Simpsons* cultural scaffolding for this myth also creates a specific correction challenge: comedy-encoded beliefs are held differently than propositional beliefs and may require a different de-encoding strategy. Approaches that engage the humor—acknowledging the episode's popularity and cleverness—while distinguishing fiction-comedy from reality are likely to be more effective than treating the television reference as straightforwardly false. The corrective framing "the *Simpsons* episode played the myth for comedy, not as a physics documentary" provides a socially graceful way to separate the entertaining cultural artifact from the factual claim it encodes.
