# The Libet Experiment Proves We Have No Free Will

## 1. The Claim

In popular neuroscience, psychology, and philosophy-for-general-audiences circles, one experimental finding has achieved the status of a clean empirical verdict: Benjamin Libet's 1983 readiness potential experiments prove that we have no free will. The argument has become a staple of TED talks, bestselling science books, and introductory philosophy courses. It goes like this: Libet attached EEG electrodes to participants' scalps, had them flex their wrists at times of their own choosing, and asked them to report the clock position when they first felt the urge to move. He discovered that the brain produced a detectable electrical signal—the Bereitschaftspotential, or readiness potential—approximately 550 milliseconds before the movement occurred, while participants did not report a conscious intention to move until about 200 milliseconds before the movement. The conclusion drawn: the brain "decided" to move roughly a third of a second before the person was consciously aware of deciding. Conscious intention arrives too late to cause anything. You are not the author of your actions. Free will is an illusion.

This framing appears in Sam Harris's 2012 book *Free Will*, in countless YouTube videos reaching millions of viewers, and has shaped public understanding of neuroscience's relationship to human agency. It is presented not as a contested interpretation of ambiguous data but as an established experimental result—something "science has shown."

The version that circulates most widely as a decisive refutation typically makes three linked claims: (1) the readiness potential is a neural signature of the brain preparing a decision before consciousness is involved; (2) this preparation precedes and therefore cannot be caused by conscious intention; and (3) therefore, conscious will is causally epiphenomenal—it does not actually produce behavior, but only appears to. From these three claims, the elimination of free will follows with apparent logical force.

This is the claim under examination. It is more specific, and more scientifically vulnerable, than the broader philosophical question of whether free will is compatible with determinism—that debate remains genuinely open. The specific claim under scrutiny here is that the Libet experiment, as a piece of empirical neuroscience, has *proven* the absence of free will.

## 2. What's Actually True

### The Original Libet Paradigm

Benjamin Libet's 1983 study, published in *Brain* with co-authors Curtis Gleason, Elwood Wright, and Dennis Pearl, was a genuinely innovative piece of neuroscience. The Bereitschaftspotential itself had been identified in the 1960s by Hans Helmut Kornhuber and Lüder Deecke, who showed that self-initiated movements were preceded by a slow negative EEG wave building over motor cortex. Libet's contribution was to measure participants' reported conscious intention and compare it to the timing of this preparatory wave.

His finding was real and reproducible: the readiness potential preceded conscious intention reports by roughly 350 milliseconds. This is the data. The interpretation is where the controversy begins.

Libet himself was more careful than his popularizers. He proposed that while unconscious processes initiate movement preparation, conscious awareness could still exert a "veto"—it could abort the movement in the final 150–200 milliseconds before execution. He explicitly argued that this veto capacity was sufficient to preserve a meaningful role for conscious will. "The role of conscious free will," he wrote, "would be, then, not to initiate a voluntary act, but rather to select and control volitional outcome: either to permit a preplanned act to proceed or to prevent it." Popular accounts consistently dropped this caveat, leaving only the part that fits the "no free will" narrative.

### The Schurger Reinterpretation

In 2012, Aaron Schurger, Jacobo Sitaram, and Stanislas Dehaene published a paper in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* that did not merely qualify the Libet interpretation but proposed an entirely different account of what the readiness potential is and what it means.

Their argument turned on a feature of the experimental design that had received insufficient attention. Libet's task is unusual: participants are asked to move "whenever they feel like it," with no external trigger, no performance target, and no cost to moving at any particular moment. This is not how most deliberate actions work. When you decide to buy a coffee, apply for a job, or swerve to avoid a pedestrian, there is something in the world that your decision is responsive to—an occasion for action, a reason, a stimulus. The Libet task removes all of this. It asks participants to generate a self-initiated movement from a state of pure waiting, with no external trigger.

Schurger and colleagues built a stochastic accumulator model to account for this. In their model, neural activity in motor circuits fluctuates randomly around a baseline. When a participant is instructed to move freely, movement is triggered when accumulated neural noise crosses a threshold—not because a specific decision has been made, but because spontaneous fluctuation has exceeded the level required to commit to action. The readiness potential, in this model, is the observable signature of that accumulation: a slow build-up in the average EEG trace that is visible because the experimenter is averaging over many trials, all of which are backward-selected from the moment of movement.

This is the critical point about the experimental method. Libet's analysis worked by time-locking the EEG average to the moment of movement and looking backward. This means the average always shows a build-up before movement—because that's how you computed the average. The question is what that build-up means. Schurger's model predicts that in a task with no external trigger and no reason to favor any particular moment, the build-up is noise accumulation, not decision formation.

Schurger et al. tested their model against the Libet data and found a good fit. They also showed that the model predicts that if you interrupt participants with a brief transcranial magnetic stimulus at a random time during the waiting period, they will report feeling an urge to move if and only if their neural activity happened to be near the threshold at that moment—a prediction later confirmed empirically. This is exactly what you would expect from a noise-threshold model and is not what you would expect if the readiness potential were a deliberate neural decision-in-progress.

The implication for the free will debate is significant. If the readiness potential in the Libet task reflects stochastic noise crossing a threshold rather than a pre-made unconscious decision, then the temporal precedence of the readiness potential over conscious intention reports tells us nothing about the causal role of consciousness in deliberate action. The Libet paradigm was examining a specific, unusual decision context—spontaneous self-initiated action with no occasion for it—and the neuroscience of that specific context has now been substantially reinterpreted.

### Subsequent Research and the Evolving Consensus

Schurger's reinterpretation has not been universally accepted; no single model in an active research area ever is. But it has substantially altered the field's understanding of the Libet findings.

Subsequent work by Schurger and others has refined the stochastic threshold model, tested it against newer neuroimaging data, and extended it to related paradigms. Critics have raised genuine questions about specific model parameters and about whether the model generalizes to more externally cued voluntary actions. But no study has reinstated the original Libet interpretation in its strong form—the claim that the readiness potential is a pre-conscious decision signal that causally precedes and generates the subsequent conscious intention.

Other lines of neuroscience research have also complicated the picture. Studies using higher-resolution recordings (ECoG rather than scalp EEG), real-time decoding algorithms, and naturalistic action paradigms have produced more complex and less clean-cut results than the original Libet setup. Some researchers have found that participant-specific preparatory signals can be used to predict movement before the reported intention in some contexts. Others have found that the timing relationship between preparatory signals and conscious reports is far more variable than the Libet averages suggested.

What is clear is that the science has moved on. The Libet experiment, in the form in which it circulates in popular accounts as a definitive proof, was a simplified representation of a contested finding even at the time—and is substantially more contested now. The claim "the Libet experiment proves we have no free will" cannot be defended as an accurate summary of the current neuroscientific evidence.

### The Philosophical Landscape

Even setting aside the Schurger reinterpretation, the leap from the Libet data to "no free will" requires bridging a philosophical gap that the experiments themselves cannot close.

The readiness potential timeline, if taken at face value, would establish that neural preparation precedes conscious intention reports in a specific, minimally motivated, uncued action task. It would not establish that:

- Determinism is true in general
- Conscious deliberation is causally inefficacious
- The kind of agency required for moral responsibility does not exist
- "Free will" in any philosophically meaningful sense has been refuted

Each of these conclusions requires additional premises that are deeply contested. The dominant tradition in professional philosophy of mind—compatibilism—holds that the entire Libet debate is largely beside the point when it comes to the kind of free will that matters for practical life, moral responsibility, and human agency.

Compatibilism, associated with philosophers including Daniel Dennett, Harry Frankfurt, Susan Wolf, John Martin Fischer, and P.F. Strawson, begins from a different question: what kind of freedom is worth wanting? The answer is not uncaused causation—not the ability to violate physical laws and generate actions from nowhere—but the ability to act in accordance with your own reasons, values, and deliberations; to respond to evidence; to revise behavior in light of new information; to be the kind of agent that can be reasoned with, trusted, held to account, and praised or blamed.

On this view, whether the brain initiates motor preparation 550 milliseconds before conscious awareness is neither here nor there for the question of whether you are a free agent. What matters is whether your action reflects your reasons, your character, your values—whether it flows from the kind of reasoning process that makes you an agent rather than a weather system. Compatibilism does not claim that consciousness magically intervenes in physics; it claims that the right level of description for agency is the level of reasons and deliberation, not the level of millisecond-scale neural preparation.

Hard determinism—associated in contemporary work with Robert Sapolsky's 2023 *Determined: A Science of Life Without Free Will*—does dispute compatibilism's adequacy. Sapolsky argues that even at the level of reasons and values, everything about you is the product of genetics, developmental environment, hormonal state, and prior experience—none of which you chose. You cannot be held responsible for your character if your character is the output of processes entirely outside your control.

This is a serious philosophical argument. But it is a philosophical argument, not a neuroscientific one. The Libet experiment does not support or refute compatibilism. It operates at a different level of analysis, addressing millisecond-scale neural preparation for a specific kind of wrist movement, and the conclusions drawn from it about consciousness and causality remain deeply contested even at that level.

## 3. Why People Believe This

### Scientific Authority as Rhetorical Shortcut

"Studies show" is one of the most powerful rhetorical moves in public discourse. When a philosophical conclusion—free will is an illusion—can be attached to a specific experiment conducted by a named scientist at a research university, it acquires an epistemic warrant it would not have as naked philosophy. The Libet experiment gave the hard determinist position the appearance of empirical grounding.

This move is not dishonest in intent; Harris, Sapolsky, and others who use it genuinely believe the neuroscience supports their conclusions. But it collapses a series of interpretive steps that are not at all settled into a single bold headline. The experiment becomes the proof. The contested philosophy behind it becomes invisible.

### The Simplicity of the Timeline

"Your brain decides before you do" is the kind of claim that survives the attention economy. It can be expressed in seven words, illustrated with a simple diagram, and verified (apparently) by pointing to a 40-year-old experiment. The compatibilist response requires explaining what kinds of agency matter for moral responsibility, why the Libet paradigm is not representative of deliberate action, how reasons can be causally efficacious in a deterministic universe, and why the Schurger reinterpretation is relevant. None of this fits a headline.

The asymmetry in communicability between the strong claim and the nuanced correction is a structural feature of this debate. Books like *Free Will* reach hundreds of thousands of readers. Papers in *PNAS* about stochastic threshold models reach a far narrower audience, even when their implications are more important.

### Popular Neuroscience's Explanatory Appetite

There is a persistent appetite in popular science for brain-based explanations that reduce complex human phenomena to their neural underpinnings. This "neurocentrism" tends to produce accounts in which the brain is the real agent and consciousness is merely a narrator, reporting on decisions already made. The Libet finding feeds this appetite perfectly. It says: consciousness is downstream, epiphenomenal, not in charge. The brain—understood as a physical system operating below conscious awareness—is pulling the levers.

This framing feels cutting-edge and counterintuitive. It also happens to fit a broader cultural narrative about the self as less autonomous than it appears—a narrative that resonates with the experience of being manipulated by algorithms, nudged by behavioral economics, and driven by evolutionary imperatives. "Your brain decides for you" connects easily to "your phone is deciding for you."

### The Missing Distinction Between Free Will Types

Much of the confusion in this debate comes from the fact that "free will" means different things to different people, and these meanings are not always distinguished in popular accounts. There are at least three different things the term can refer to:

1. **Libertarian free will**: The ability to make choices that are genuinely uncaused—not determined by prior physical states. An agent that could, given identical prior conditions, have chosen differently. This is what quantum mechanics proponents sometimes invoke (though the invocation is contested), and it is what most philosophers of mind regard as incoherent or at least scientifically ungrounded.

2. **Compatibilist free will**: The ability to act on one's own reasons, values, and deliberations without external coercion; to be responsive to reasons; to have the kind of reflective self-governance that distinguishes persons from simple causal mechanisms. This is what most professional philosophers mean by "free will."

3. **Folk free will**: The intuitive conviction that we are the authors of our choices, that we could have done otherwise in some meaningful sense, and that this is the basis for praise, blame, and moral responsibility. This is what most non-philosophers mean.

The Libet experiment, in its original interpretation, targets libertarian free will—by showing that neural processes precede conscious intention, it appears to show that consciousness is not an uncaused cause. It says nothing directly about compatibilist free will, since compatibilists never claimed that consciousness is an uncaused initiator of motor preparation. And its relationship to folk free will is mediated by contested philosophical interpretation.

When popular accounts say "the Libet experiment proves we have no free will," they typically mean to establish the strongest conclusion—that libertarian free will is gone and everything downstream (including compatibilist free will) collapses with it. This requires philosophical bridging that the experiment cannot provide.

## 4. Verdict

**The specific claim is refuted. The broader debate remains genuinely contested.**

The claim "the Libet experiment proves we have no free will" is not a defensible summary of the current state of science. It rests on an interpretation of the readiness potential that was already qualified by Libet himself and has been substantially challenged by Schurger's 2012 stochastic noise-threshold model. The paradigm examines a narrowly unusual decision context—spontaneous, uncued, single-degree-of-freedom movement—and the dominant interpretation of what the readiness potential means in that context has shifted significantly since the original study.

This does not mean free will is vindicated in all forms. Hard determinism is a serious philosophical position held by serious scholars. The broader debate about whether determinism is true, whether it eliminates morally relevant agency, and whether compatibilism is adequate is not settled and may never be settled. Robert Sapolsky's case in *Determined* is more than an argument from the Libet experiment; it is a systematic argument about the way biological, developmental, and environmental factors shape every disposition a person has, making talk of authentic self-origination incoherent.

What is not defensible is treating the Libet experiment as a settled empirical proof of the philosophical conclusion. The experiment is contested at the level of what the readiness potential means. The philosophical inference from any interpretation of the readiness potential to "no free will" requires contested bridging premises. And the entire debate bypasses the compatibilist tradition, which offers the most developed and widely-held account of agency among professional philosophers of mind.

The honest verdict: the Libet claim as a "proof" is refuted. The free will question as a whole is genuinely open.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### Stakes for Criminal Justice

The free will debate has real policy implications, and proponents of hard determinism have often motivated their case partly by appeal to those implications. If people genuinely cannot do otherwise given their neurobiology and environment, then the retributive rationale for criminal punishment—you deserved what you got because you chose wrongly—collapses. What remains is purely consequentialist: incarceration is justified only if it prevents future harm or enables rehabilitation.

Sapolsky, Eagleman, and others have argued that this shift is overdue and would produce a more humane and effective criminal justice system. They are not wrong that many retributive sentencing practices produce neither justice nor deterrence. But the argument does not require the hard determinist conclusion. Consequentialist critiques of punitive incarceration are available on compatibilist grounds as well—what matters is whether the punishment serves good ends, not whether the criminal was a free agent in the libertarian sense.

More troubling is the possibility that eliminating the folk notion of moral responsibility entirely could undermine the social practices of accountability that make trust, cooperation, and civil life possible. P.F. Strawson's 1962 paper "Freedom and Resentment" argued that our "reactive attitudes"—gratitude, resentment, indignation, love—are not optional add-ons to human social life that can be removed once we accept determinism. They are constitutive of what it means to relate to other people as persons. Abandoning them would not make us more rational; it would make us less human.

### Consciousness and the Hard Problem

One reason the free will debate is unlikely to be resolved by neuroscience alone is that it runs directly into the hard problem of consciousness—the question of why and how physical processes give rise to subjective experience. We have increasingly detailed accounts of the neural correlates of conscious states: which brain regions are active, how they interact, what signals predict reported awareness. What we do not have is a satisfying explanation of why these physical processes are accompanied by experience at all.

This matters for the Libet debate because the inference from "the readiness potential precedes conscious intention reports" to "conscious intention is causally irrelevant" requires assumptions about the causal architecture of consciousness that are not established. It assumes that the timing of a reported intention reflects the timing of the intention itself—that introspection is accurate and immediate. It assumes that the only way consciousness could be causally relevant is if it operated before the readiness potential. And it assumes that consciousness and neural activity are the kind of things whose causal relationships can be read off millisecond-scale EEG traces.

None of these assumptions is secure. Introspection is notoriously unreliable about timing, as Libet himself acknowledged. The causal relevance of consciousness might operate at timescales and levels of analysis that EEG simply cannot capture. And the relationship between subjective experience and neural correlates is precisely what remains unexplained by the hard problem.

### Schurger's Contribution

Aaron Schurger's work deserves specific recognition because it accomplished something that happens rarely in neuroscience: it proposed a mechanistic model that accounts for an existing dataset while yielding novel, testable predictions that were subsequently confirmed. The stochastic threshold model is not merely a philosophical objection to Libet's interpretation; it is a computational model with specific implications for what should happen if participants are interrupted at various points during the waiting period, for what the distribution of pre-movement neural activity should look like, and for how the timing of the readiness potential should vary as a function of task structure.

These predictions have been tested and have fared reasonably well. The model has been extended and refined by subsequent researchers. It has not been conclusively established, but neither has it been refuted. It represents the current state of the neuroscience more accurately than the original Libet interpretation does.

What Schurger showed is that the readiness potential in Libet-style paradigms is most parsimoniously explained as the observable signature of random neural fluctuations crossing a decision threshold in a task that removes all external reasons to act at any particular moment. If that is right, the readiness potential tells us about the stochastic dynamics of spontaneous action generation, not about whether consciousness has causal power over deliberate decision-making.

### Compatibilism's Enduring Case

The compatibilist tradition did not need Schurger's reinterpretation to survive the Libet challenge. Even accepting the original Libet finding—that neural preparation precedes conscious awareness by several hundred milliseconds in an uncued wrist-flexion task—compatibilists could point out that this result is entirely consistent with their view.

Compatibilists never claimed that the conscious experience of willing is the first event in a purely mental causal chain that then reaches down and physically initiates the neural activity. That is a Cartesian picture of mind-body interaction that most contemporary philosophers have abandoned. What compatibilists claim is that you are free when your actions flow from your reasons, values, and character without external compulsion—when you are the kind of agent that can be reasoned with and held to account. The timing of neural preparation relative to conscious reports in a single-degree-of-freedom uncued movement task is simply not what compatibilist freedom is about.

Dennett has put this point with characteristic force: the question "did your brain decide before you did?" is malformed, because the brain is you. The relevant question is whether the process that issued in the action was the kind of reasoning process that constitutes deliberate agency—whether it was responsive to reasons, integrated with your values, capable of revision in light of evidence. Neural preparation for spontaneous wrist flexion tells us very little about this.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

### Why This Claim Escapes Standard Fact-Checking

Snopes, PolitiFact, and similar organizations are well-equipped to verify empirical claims: did this event occur, did this person say this thing, does this statistic check out. They are less well-equipped to adjudicate claims that sit at the intersection of empirical science and contested philosophy—claims where the facts are disputed at the level of interpretation rather than observation, and where expert opinion is genuinely divided.

"The Libet experiment proves we have no free will" is exactly this kind of claim. It is not factually false in the way that "vaccines cause autism" is factually false—it cites a real experiment, by a real scientist, that produced real data. Its error lies in the interpretation: in what the readiness potential means, in whether the philosophical inference holds, and in whether the scientific picture presented is current. These are not questions that yield to a simple search for the primary source.

As a result, this claim has not been directly addressed by major fact-checking organizations. It circulates largely unchallenged in popular science because the correction requires scientific and philosophical depth that doesn't fit the fact-checking format.

### Science Journalism's Uneven Track Record

Science journalism has done better than institutional fact-checking, but unevenly. Outlets including *The Atlantic*, *Quanta Magazine*, *Aeon*, and *Nautilus* have published serious pieces engaging with the Schurger reinterpretation and the compatibilist tradition. Philosophers and neuroscientists including Eddy Nahmias, Alfred Mele, and Adina Roskies have written widely-accessible critiques of Libet overclaiming.

The problem is one of reach and timing. Sam Harris's *Free Will* was published in 2012, the same year as Schurger's *PNAS* paper. The book reached a popular audience in the millions. The paper reached specialists and, eventually, science journalists—but the gap in penetration between the confident popular claim and the scientific correction is vast and has not closed.

Robert Sapolsky's *Determined* (2023) substantially extended the hard determinist case, reaching another large popular audience. It engages more seriously with the compatibilist tradition than Harris did, but its treatment of compatibilism remains contested by professional philosophers who argue that Sapolsky mischaracterizes what compatibilists are actually claiming.

### What Adequate Coverage Would Require

A rigorous fact-check or science journalism treatment of this claim would need to accomplish four things:

First, accurately represent the Libet finding and its specific context—a minimally motivated, uncued, single-degree-of-freedom movement task—and distinguish it from the broader range of human deliberate action.

Second, present the Schurger reinterpretation as the current scientific picture, not as a fringe challenge to a settled consensus. The stochastic threshold model is a serious computational account that has been tested and refined; it is not a philosophical objection but a competing scientific model.

Third, distinguish between the specific empirical claim about the readiness potential and the broader philosophical claim about free will and determinism. The former is contested at the level of neuroscience; the latter is contested at the level of philosophy; and the two levels of contestation are not the same debate.

Fourth, take compatibilism seriously as a position held by the majority of professional philosophers of mind and ethics—not as a definitional trick or a failure of nerve, but as the most developed account of what agency and moral responsibility actually require.

The claim "the Libet experiment proves we have no free will" fails on multiple grounds: empirically, because the interpretation of the readiness potential is substantially contested; scientifically, because the paradigm is specifically designed to examine a narrow and unusual decision context; and philosophically, because the inference from neural timing data to the elimination of morally relevant agency requires contested bridging arguments that the experiment cannot supply. This is not the same as saying free will in every sense is vindicated. The deep questions remain open. But the specific claim—that a 1983 experiment settled the matter—does not survive scrutiny.
