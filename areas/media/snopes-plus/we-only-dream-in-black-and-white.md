# "People Used to Dream Only in Black and White Before Color TV"

## 1. The Claim

The claim holds that before the widespread adoption of color television, the majority of people dreamed in black and white—not as a matter of individual variation, but as a general human condition. The popular version typically identifies black-and-white television as the cause: because people's primary visual experience of moving images was monochromatic, their dreaming minds processed imagery in the same restricted palette. When color television became dominant in the 1960s and 1970s, people began dreaming in color, and the black-and-white era of human dreaming ended.

This narrative appears in popular psychology articles, trivia collections, and discussions about consciousness and media effects. It has the surface features of a well-documented empirical claim: it seems testable (you can survey people about their dreams), it has a plausible mechanism (media exposure shapes cognitive templates), and a clear historical timeline (black-and-white broadcast television was commercially dominant from roughly the late 1940s to the mid-1960s in the United States and later in much of Europe). The claim is often cited as a striking example of how technology reshapes human inner experience.

A related but stronger version of the claim suggests that all pre-modern humans—before any black-and-white medium—dreamed exclusively in black and white, as if color dreaming were a recent cultural achievement rather than a biological default. This version implies that black-and-white dreaming was the evolutionary baseline and color dreaming is a modern learned capacity. Both versions share the assertion that there was a discernible transition in dreaming phenomenology aligned with changes in visual media.

The claim circulates alongside genuine research findings, producing confusion about what the science actually shows. Survey studies from the 1950s do show high rates of reported black-and-white dreaming among populations with heavy black-and-white television exposure. These data are real. The question is whether they reflect actual monochromatic dreaming, failures of color recall in dream reporting, or media-shaped expectations that color dreaming was unusual or absent.

## 2. What's Actually True

The core neurological claim—that human visual cortex processing during REM sleep is monochromatic—is not supported by the evidence. The human visual system, including its operation during dreaming, appears to process color information as part of its typical function. Neuroimaging studies of REM sleep show activity in color-processing areas of the occipital cortex, specifically in regions including V4 and posterior inferior temporal cortex, which are implicated in chromatic processing during waking vision (Hobson et al., 2000, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*).

The most important empirical challenge to the black-and-white dreaming claim comes from cross-cultural and historical comparison. If black-and-white dreaming were a baseline human neurological condition, we would expect evidence of it from pre-photographic cultures with no exposure to monochromatic media. Anthropological dream reports collected from indigenous communities in the Americas, Africa, and Oceania—where survey instruments were applied before widespread introduction of Western media—consistently describe color in dream imagery (Tonay, 1993, *Dreaming: Journal of the Association for the Study of Dreams*). Pre-photographic European dream diaries and literary dream accounts from classical antiquity through the 18th century frequently mention color in dream imagery, with Aristotle noting in *De Somniis* (c. 350 BC) that colors are perceived in dreams as they are in waking life.

The pivotal modern research was conducted by Eva Murzyn at the University of Dundee (Murzyn, 2008, *Consciousness and Cognition*). Murzyn surveyed 76 participants over age 25 and found that those who reported heavy childhood exposure to black-and-white television were significantly more likely to report dreaming in black and white as adults—the first systematic test of the media-exposure hypothesis. Critically, participants who grew up watching color television almost never reported black-and-white dreaming. Murzyn concluded that black-and-white media exposure in formative years influences how people conceptualize and report dream experiences, but this effect reflects the medium reshaping how people interpret and remember their dream experiences, not the medium changing the neurological generation of dream imagery.

Direct polysomnographic observation supports the view that dreaming is typically experienced in color. Studies using immediate post-awakening reports during monitored REM episodes—which minimize both memory decay and post-hoc reconstruction—show substantially higher rates of color dream reporting than retrospective surveys (Herman, Roffwarg & Tauber, 1968, *Perceptual and Motor Skills*). When dreamers are woken during confirmed REM sleep and interviewed within seconds, color reports approach 80–85% of dream events. Retrospective surveys of dreams from the night before show much lower rates, suggesting that color information in dreams decays quickly from memory and is not the most salient feature dreamers encode on waking.

The historical survey data showing high black-and-white dreaming rates in the 1950s are therefore most parsimoniously explained by a combination of factors: color information in dreams is poorly retained in memory; reporting norms shaped by widespread black-and-white media made monochromatic dreaming seem normal; and some proportion of dreamers genuinely may have experienced more vivid or better-retained black-and-white imagery because that was their dominant template for narrative visual sequences. None of this requires positing that people were neurologically incapable of color dreaming.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The black-and-white dreaming myth demonstrates a genuinely fascinating phenomenon: the capacity of media environments to shape second-order cognition—not just what people experience, but how they conceptualize, recall, and report what they experience. The mechanism here is not that black-and-white television neurologically suppressed color processing in the visual cortex during sleep. It is something more subtle and epistemologically interesting.

**The early survey data are real and were taken at face value.** Studies from the 1950s did find high rates of black-and-white dream reporting. Calvin Hall's extensive dream diary research, conducted across the 1940s and 1950s and analyzed in Hall & Van de Castle (1966, *The Content Analysis of Dreams*, Appleton-Century-Crofts), found that approximately 71% of reported dreams lacked color. These numbers were widely cited and contributed to the provisional scientific consensus that human dreaming was primarily achromatic. What was not adequately appreciated at the time was the role of reporting modality in producing these numbers: retrospective self-report of dream color is highly sensitive to both memory decay and to what dreamers consider worth noting or mentioning about their dreams.

**Media environments shape the representational templates we use to think about experience.** This is the epistemic novelty of the color dreaming case. Research by Kosslyn (1994, *Image and Brain: The Resolution of the Imagery Debate*, MIT Press) and subsequent cognitive science work established that visual imagery—including imagery during sleep—involves the activation of cortical representations shaped by prior experience. A person whose primary exposure to narrative visual media has been monochromatic may develop stronger representational templates for achromatic image sequences. These templates may not replace color processing, but they may provide the vocabulary through which dreaming content is encoded into episodic memory on waking. The media environment shapes what you notice, label, and retain—not what your visual cortex generates.

This constitutes what might be called **second-order media influence**: rather than changing perceptual experience directly, media exposure changes the conceptual schemas through which experience is reported and remembered. Dream research has independently established that the content of dreams (characters, settings, activities) is strongly influenced by waking experience and emotional preoccupations (Domhoff, 2003, *The Scientific Study of Dreams*, American Psychological Association). The extension of this principle to the perceptual qualities of dreams—including color—is a theoretical contribution that the black-and-white dreaming data inadvertently generated.

**Reporting norms compound actual phenomenology.** When black-and-white dreaming was presented as normal or typical in popular science writing and psychology curricula, dreamers who experienced color in their dreams may have been less likely to record or report it as a notable feature. The absence of color mention in a dream diary does not mean the dream lacked color; it often means color was not salient enough to record. Conversely, in an environment where color dreaming is understood as normal, dreamers are more likely to notice and report it when it is present, and to note its absence as unusual when it is absent. The shift in baseline reporting expectations would produce exactly the historical pattern observed—high rates of reported achromatic dreams in the mid-20th century transitioning to high rates of reported color dreams as color television became normative—without any underlying change in neurological dream generation.

**The plausible mechanism made the claim credible.** Media effects on cognition are real and extensively documented. The relationship between visual media palette and dreaming cognition seemed like a reasonable extension of documented media influence effects. This plausibility served as a durable source of credence for the myth: it was not obviously implausible, it had survey data behind it, and it told a satisfying story about the relationship between technology and consciousness. It was exactly the kind of finding that science communicators find compelling and shareable.

## 4. Verdict

**MOSTLY FALSE, WITH AN EMPIRICALLY INTERESTING RESIDUE.**

Human beings do not naturally dream in black and white as a baseline neurological condition, and the transition to color television did not neurologically restore color dreaming to people who had been deprived of it. The human visual cortex processes chromatic information during REM sleep, color is reported in dreams at high rates when measurement is conducted close in time to waking, and pre-photographic cultures had no shortage of color in their dream reports.

What is true is that populations with heavy childhood exposure to black-and-white television report higher rates of achromatic dreaming as adults, and that mid-20th century populations showed high rates of such reporting in surveys. Murzyn (2008, *Consciousness and Cognition*) confirmed this exposure-to-reporting relationship. The mechanism, however, is almost certainly mediated by how people represent, recall, and report their dream experiences rather than by changes in visual cortex function during sleep.

**EARC mode C (Contextual)**: The claim cannot be dismissed as simply fabricated. It has a real empirical history, real survey data, and captures a genuine phenomenon—media-shaped dream recall and reporting. The correction requires specifying the mechanism more precisely: the medium may shape the conceptual templates for reporting dream experience without directly altering the neurological generation of perceptual content during sleep.

**Gap category: media-influenced phenomenology.** A novel category earned by this case: the myth documents a genuine psychological effect (media-shaped dream reporting) while misidentifying it as a neurological effect (media-caused black-and-white dreaming). The distinction matters because the former is about epistemology and cognition; the latter is about neurophysiology. Both are interesting, but only one is supported.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The color dreaming case sits at the intersection of consciousness research, media effects scholarship, and the philosophy of introspection. It raises a question that extends well beyond dreams: to what extent are self-reports of subjective experience reliable testimony about underlying phenomenological facts, and to what extent do they reflect culturally mediated interpretive schemas that people apply to their experiences?

This is not a trivial question. William James (1890, *The Principles of Psychology*, Holt) recognized that introspection is a trained rather than a spontaneous capacity, and that what people report about their mental states is shaped by the conceptual vocabulary available to them. Dream phenomenology is particularly vulnerable to this dynamic because dreaming is a transient state that must be reported after the fact, under conditions of rapid memory decay, by subjects who are applying waking conceptual categories to pre-waking perceptual content.

Empirical dream research has repeatedly discovered that the features of dreams people report most readily—narrative coherence, emotional tone, key actors—are not necessarily the features that were most perceptually prominent during the dream. Color appears to be a perceptual feature that is processed during dreaming but is not reliably encoded as a memorable property of dream narrative. Koulack & Goodenough (1976, *Psychological Bulletin*) documented the extreme sensitivity of dream recall to waking conditions, noting that dream memories begin deteriorating within seconds of waking and that the reported content of dreams is powerfully shaped by both contextual cues and internal attentional focus at the moment of waking.

The broader implication for media effects research is significant. The standard media effects framework asks how media influence behavior, attitudes, and cognition in terms of waking life. The color dreaming case suggests that media exposure can also shape how people conceptualize and report private experiences that occur independent of media—extending media influence into the architecture of self-report itself. This is a genuinely underappreciated mechanism that may operate in other domains: the way people describe their emotional lives, their pain, their sense of time, and their social perceptions may all be partly organized by the representational templates that dominant media environments provide.

The cultural history dimension is equally notable. American commercial television achieved massive penetration rapidly: by 1960, roughly 90% of American homes contained a television set, the overwhelming majority of which produced monochromatic images (Nielsen Media Research historical data). The speed of this transformation meant that within roughly a decade-and-a-half, the dominant template for experienced narrative visual imagery in American life shifted from color (direct embodied experience of the world) to an achromatic medium. No prior culture had experienced an analogous rapid mass transition to achromatic narrative imagery on this scale. The mid-20th century represents a genuinely unusual historical episode in the phenomenology of visual experience, even if the effects were on recall and reporting rather than on dreaming itself.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not published a dedicated fact-check on the black-and-white dreaming claim, though it has addressed related claims about the psychological effects of television on cognition. The claim has received more systematic treatment in academic consciousness research than in popular fact-checking sources.

Murzyn (2008, *Consciousness and Cognition*) remains the most-cited empirically grounded treatment of the specific media-exposure mechanism. Her research was widely covered by science journalism outlets and is frequently cited in discussions of the claim, though sometimes misread as confirming the original myth rather than explaining why the survey data appeared as they did.

The BBC's science coverage and a number of university research summaries have addressed the claim by distinguishing between what the historical surveys showed and what the underlying neurological evidence suggests, reaching the assessment that dreams are typically colorful but that black-and-white media exposure influences reporting.

The claim's resistance to comprehensive fact-checking likely reflects its apparent triviality—the practical stakes of whether people dreamed in color or not seem low—combined with genuine empirical complexity that resists the binary True/False format that works well for simpler factual claims. The correct answer involves mechanism specification: not "people definitely did dream in color" (though that is probably true) but "the survey data showing achromatic dreaming reflect reporting and recall effects rather than neurological generation of monochromatic imagery." That nuance is difficult to communicate in a standard fact-check format.
