# We Only Use 10% of Our Brain

## 1. The Claim

The claim is familiar enough that it requires almost no introduction: human beings use only ten percent of their brains. The other ninety percent sits dormant, an untapped reservoir of potential. If only we could access it — if only we could unlock that silent majority — we would be smarter, more capable, perhaps superhuman. The claim often comes packaged with an inspiring addendum: great geniuses like Einstein purportedly used more of their brain than ordinary people, explaining their extraordinary abilities.

This myth appears in self-help books, motivational speeches, pharmaceutical marketing, and popular science writing. It has been attributed to Albert Einstein, to William James, to neuroscientists who supposedly said it before modern imaging revealed the truth. It has inspired films — most notably *Lucy* (2014) and *Limitless* (2011), both premised on the idea that accessing more of the brain leads to superhuman cognitive abilities. It appears in textbooks, in teacher training materials, and in the informal wisdom passed down from parents and teachers to children with the authority of received common sense.

Few beliefs about the brain are this widespread, this wrong, and this consequential.

## 2. What's Actually True

Neuroimaging — particularly functional MRI (fMRI), positron emission tomography (PET), and electroencephalography (EEG) — has made it possible to observe brain activity directly in living humans performing a wide range of tasks. The findings are consistent and unambiguous: all regions of the brain are active at various points during normal functioning. There is no persistent dark region. There is no large-scale dormant territory waiting to be awakened.

The brain does not operate all regions simultaneously at full capacity at every moment — that would be physiologically impossible and undesirable (simultaneous activation of all neurons produces a seizure). Different tasks engage different brain circuits. Sleep, rest, and focused attention each produce different patterns of regional activation. But across the full range of activities that make up a human life — sleeping, waking, thinking, feeling, moving, perceiving, remembering, imagining — all brain regions show activity. No region is consistently unused.

This is not merely an observational fact; it is what we would expect from evolutionary and metabolic first principles. The human brain is extraordinarily expensive to operate. It constitutes roughly 2% of body mass but consumes approximately 20% of the body's total energy at rest. The metabolic cost of maintaining neural tissue is high — neurons require constant energy supply just to maintain their resting membrane potentials, their synaptic connections, and their cellular infrastructure. If 90% of the brain were truly unused, natural selection would have eliminated it long ago. Organisms that devoted a fifth of their caloric budget to non-functional tissue would be at a severe survival disadvantage. The very fact that the brain is as large and metabolically costly as it is argues powerfully against the ten percent claim.

Damage to brain tissue provides converging evidence. If 90% of the brain were dormant and non-functional, then damage to randomly selected brain regions would affect function only 10% of the time. This is radically inconsistent with what we observe in clinical neurology. Damage to virtually any brain region — through stroke, traumatic injury, tumor, or surgical resection — produces detectable functional effects. The effects vary by region and by the specific damage, but there is no large territory of the brain where damage is routinely consequence-free. Every part of the brain does something.

The 10% figure also conflicts with what we know about brain development. The postnatal period of human brain development involves extensive pruning — axons and synaptic connections that are not used are eliminated. The principle underlying this process is "use it or lose it": connections that carry information are strengthened; connections that do not carry information are pruned away. If 90% of the brain were truly unused from birth, the pruning process would eliminate it during development. This does not happen.

What about the claim that geniuses use more of their brain? This too lacks support. Neuroimaging studies of highly skilled individuals — expert chess players, mathematicians, musicians — generally show *more efficient* brain activation, not more extensive activation. Expert performance is associated with doing more with less, not with recruiting a larger proportion of neural real estate.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The 10% claim is a case where a myth's origins are genuinely murky, where multiple independent sources may have contributed to its emergence, and where the myth's content — its psychological and cultural appeal — explains its durability better than any single origin story.

**The misquoted William James.** One frequently cited source is William James, the American philosopher and psychologist, who in his 1907 essay "The Energies of Men" and related lectures argued that human beings fail to live up to their potential — that we habitually operate below the maximum of our capabilities. James's point was motivational and psychological, not neuroanatomical. He was not making a claim about the percentage of brain tissue used. But his language was vivid and was quoted often, and somewhere in the transmission process his philosophical observation about unrealized potential was translated into a neurological claim. The specific 10% figure is not in James's writing.

**Dale Carnegie and self-help culture.** The 10% claim appears in Dale Carnegie's *How to Win Friends and Influence People* (1936), one of the bestselling books of the twentieth century. Carnegie wrote: "Professor William James of Harvard used to say that the average man develops only ten percent of his latent mental ability." This attribution is incorrect — James never made this claim in this form — but Carnegie's version reached an enormous audience and helped fix the specific 10% figure in popular culture. The self-help genre had strong incentives to promote the idea of vast untapped potential, and the 10% claim served that narrative perfectly.

**The Einstein attribution.** The claim is frequently attributed to Albert Einstein, often as a personal statement about his own brain usage. There is no evidence that Einstein ever said this. Einstein's name attached to the claim likely because Einstein is the cultural archetype of extraordinary intelligence, and the myth requires the existence of geniuses who use more of their brain. Attaching Einstein's name to the claim provided both authority and explanatory power. The attributions to Einstein proliferated through the twentieth century and proved extremely difficult to dislodge — as with all false Einstein quotes, the prestige of the attribution made people reluctant to check it.

**Early neuroscience misreadings.** Some historians of the myth trace it to early-twentieth-century neuroscience. In the 1930s and beyond, neurophysiologist Karl Lashley conducted experiments on rats in which he removed portions of the cortex and found that small lesions often had surprisingly little effect on learned behaviors — the effects of damage were proportional to the *amount* of tissue removed, not to its specific location (within certain limits). This was the basis for his concepts of "mass action" and "equipotentiality." These findings, taken out of context, could be read as suggesting that large portions of the brain are non-essential. But Lashley's results were specific to the cortical regions he studied in rats performing specific tasks; they do not generalize to the claim that 90% of the brain is globally unused.

Similarly, the existence of "silent" areas of cortex — regions that produce no observable motor or sensory output when electrically stimulated during early neurosurgery — may have contributed to the idea that large brain regions are non-functional. What was actually meant by "silent" was that stimulation did not produce immediately observable motor or sensory responses, not that the regions were inactive or non-functional.

**Glial cells and the "support" framing.** The brain consists of approximately 86 billion neurons and a comparable number of glial cells (the exact ratio has been revised by recent work, with the old 10:1 glial-to-neuron ratio now understood as inaccurate). Glial cells support neuronal function in various ways — providing structural support, regulating the chemical environment, facilitating myelination, and participating in immune function. An older framing, since revised, characterized glial cells as purely supportive and non-computational. If one counted only "active" neurons and discounted glial cells and the supporting architecture, one could arrive at a smaller fraction of "computational" tissue — but this framing is both scientifically outdated and does not support the 10% claim.

**The myth's cultural and psychological appeal.** Ultimately, the most powerful explanation for the myth's durability is not its origins but its content. The 10% claim offers three things simultaneously: a flattering explanation for current limitations (you're not dumb, you're just not using your whole brain), an inspiring vision of potential (if only you could unlock the rest!), and a framework that legitimizes self-improvement products, techniques, and philosophies as accessing the dormant 90%.

This structure makes the myth nearly irresistible to the self-help industry, to motivational culture, and to anyone selling cognitive enhancement. Brain training programs, memory supplements, meditation techniques, and learning methodologies have all been marketed with appeals to the dormant 90%. The myth serves commercial interests in a way that guarantees continued promotion.

It also appeals to something deeper: the sense that ordinary human existence is somehow below its own potential. The 10% myth is, at bottom, a myth of transcendence — the idea that we are not fully what we could be, that the gap between our actual and potential selves is vast, and that it might be closed. This is an ancient human preoccupation, and the brain-percentage version gives it a satisfyingly quantitative, scientific-sounding form.

## 4. Verdict

**FALSE** — with a completeness unusual even among debunked myths. Neuroimaging shows activity in all brain regions over the course of normal functioning. Evolutionary and metabolic reasoning makes a 90% dormant brain impossible to explain. Clinical neurology shows that damage to any brain region produces functional effects. Developmental neurobiology shows that unused circuits are pruned, not preserved. The specific 10% figure cannot be traced to any credible neuroscientific source; its origins lie in misattributed quotes, self-help literature, and popular culture, not in any actual measurement of brain utilization.

The gap category is *distorted-but-grounded* only in the narrow sense that brains do not operate all regions at full capacity simultaneously — this is true, as noted above, and might be the thin factual thread from which the myth was initially spun. But the distortion from that thin thread to the claim that 90% of the brain is dormant is enormous and unsupported.

The myth is also actively harmful in a way that places it among the more consequential brain myths. It creates a framework that is exploited by pseudoscience.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The 10% brain myth occupies a special position in the landscape of scientific misinformation because of what it enables. It does not merely exist as an isolated false belief — it functions as a conceptual permission structure for pseudoscientific claims about the mind and human potential.

**Pseudoscience and the dormant 90%.** The implicit logic of the 10% myth is that there exists vast, accessible neural territory capable of supporting extraordinary cognitive abilities — abilities that the ordinary human mind cannot currently achieve. This logic is invoked directly and explicitly by proponents of telekinesis, clairvoyance, extrasensory perception (ESP), and other paranormal cognitive phenomena. The argument runs: "Scientists say we only use 10% of our brain. So there's clearly 90% of potential that's unexplored. Psychic abilities could operate in that unexplored territory." The myth creates an epistemological shelter for claims that would otherwise face immediate objection.

This is not a trivial connection. The James Randi Educational Foundation and similar skeptical organizations have documented the use of the 10% myth as a rhetorical move in promotion of psychic phenomena, "mind power" courses, and pseudoscientific cognitive enhancement products. Debunking the myth removes a key rhetorical support for these claims.

**Films and the public imagination.** The cultural presence of the 10% myth in film is significant. *Limitless* (2011) and *Lucy* (2014) both treat the myth as their central premise. *Lucy* is particularly explicit: it follows a character who gains access to progressively larger percentages of her brain through a chemical compound, eventually achieving omniscience when she reaches 100%. These films reached global audiences of tens of millions. They did not create the myth — it predated them by decades — but they amplified it and gave it a vivid, cinematic form in the cultural imagination.

The persistence of the myth in popular film reflects the entertainment value of the transcendence narrative: the idea of the ordinary person who discovers and unlocks their extraordinary potential is one of the most durable story structures in popular culture. The 10% myth serves this narrative with a neuroscientific warrant. Filmmakers have acknowledged that the premise is scientifically inaccurate but defended their choices on narrative grounds — which is fair for fiction, but which amplifies the myth's reach.

**The "10% of DNA is used" confusion.** The 10% brain myth has a sibling misconception: the claim that humans use only 10% of their DNA, or that 90% of the human genome is "junk DNA." Like the brain myth, this claim has been substantially revised by subsequent research — the ENCODE project (2012) found that the majority of the human genome is transcribed and plays some functional role, though debate continues about how much of this function is biologically significant. The parallel between the two "10%" myths is striking and may reflect a cultural tendency to see unused potential in biological systems — a default assumption of waste or reserve capacity that may not reflect biological reality.

**Neuroscience literacy and the media.** The 10% myth is a particularly vivid example of a broader problem in science communication: neuroscience findings are frequently oversimplified and sensationalized in media coverage. Because the brain is both fascinating and not directly observable to ordinary people, it is especially vulnerable to mythologizing. Claims about the brain that would be easily questioned in other domains — "we only use 10% of our muscles," for instance — pass uncritically when the brain is the subject, because most people have no direct experience to contradict them.

Improving neuroscience literacy is one of the stated goals of science communication efforts like Brain Awareness Week and various science education initiatives. The 10% myth is routinely cited as the target claim to be corrected in these contexts.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The 10% brain myth is one of the most thoroughly debunked scientific myths in the mainstream fact-checking ecosystem. Multiple organizations and publications have addressed it directly and repeatedly.

Snopes has not maintained a dedicated single page for this claim (reflecting its classification as a pervasive cultural myth rather than a specific viral story), but it has addressed related claims about Einstein, James, and specific viral posts invoking the 10% figure. Scientific American addressed the myth in a notable 2008 feature ("Do People Only Use 10 Percent of Their Brains?") and has returned to it multiple times. The article has been one of their most-accessed pieces on neuroscience myths. *Discover* magazine, *New Scientist*, and numerous neuroscience-focused blogs and outreach platforms have published debunking articles.

Barry Beyerstein, a neuroscientist and psychologist at Simon Fraser University, was among the first systematic debunkers of the myth, publishing a detailed analysis in 1999 in the book *Mind Myths: Exploring Popular Assumptions About the Mind and Brain* (edited by Sergio Della Sala). Beyerstein traced the myth's origins, examined its cultural functions, and documented its use by various commercial interests. His work remains a useful reference.

The challenge for fact-checkers is that the myth is not typically embedded in a specific, citable claim that can be straightforwardly evaluated. It operates more as ambient background knowledge — something people believe without necessarily knowing where they heard it. This makes it resistant to the standard fact-checking approach of finding the specific claim, tracing its source, and evaluating the evidence. The correction has to function at the level of the background belief, not the individual viral post.

The most effective corrections combine three elements: direct neuroimaging evidence (showing that all brain regions are active), evolutionary reasoning (explaining why a 90% dormant brain makes no biological sense), and a historical account of the myth's origins (explaining where the 10% figure came from and why it has persisted). Corrections that use only one of these elements tend to be less durable, because the other elements of the myth remain available as implicit support.

For educators, the 10% myth provides an opportunity to teach several things simultaneously: the basics of brain function, the logic of evolutionary medicine, the sociology of scientific misinformation, and the critical reading skills needed to evaluate extraordinary claims. A myth this wrong, this well-documented, and this culturally embedded is an unusually good teaching case.
