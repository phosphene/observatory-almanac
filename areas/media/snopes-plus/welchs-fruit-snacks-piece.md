## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In September 2018, a Facebook user identified as Courtnie Sassenach published a post claiming that after experiencing a "horrid taste" when eating from a newly opened bag of Welch's Fruit Snacks, she examined the bag's contents and discovered that one of the fruit snack pieces had a "tapeworm through it." The post spread widely and prompted significant consumer concern about product safety, eventually reaching Welch's parent company, Promotion In Motion Companies, Inc., which responded with a corporate statement denying the contamination had occurred and explaining the visual anomaly as a manufacturing artifact.

Snopes founder David Mikkelson (2018) rated this claim **Unproven**. The rating reflects the fundamental evidentiary impasse at the center of the dispute: Sassenach claimed that a laboratory tested the piece and confirmed the presence of a dehydrated tapeworm but never produced the lab report to Snopes; Promotion In Motion stated that despite multiple attempts, they were unable to obtain the suspect piece for independent analysis and had received no documentation evidencing any consumer-side laboratory testing. Without a verified chain of custody for the physical sample and without documentation of third-party laboratory confirmation, neither the consumer's claim nor the manufacturer's denial could be definitively established.

This entry examines the evidentiary structure of the competing claims, the biology of tapeworm transmission and the plausibility of tapeworm presence in a commercial food product, the manufacturing process for gelatin-based fruit snacks and its implications for the manufacturer's alternative explanation, the legal and regulatory framework governing foreign body contamination in packaged snack products, and the broader pattern of unverified contamination claims targeting branded food products on social media.

## §2 The Competing Claims and Their Evidential Basis

The claim originated directly from Sassenach's personal Facebook post in September 2018. The post was accompanied by a photograph showing a fruit snack piece with what appeared to be a thin, elongated, off-color protrusion of some kind. The post was shared widely, eventually attracting substantial media coverage and landing on the Chef Boyardee Facebook page's comment section—suggesting the virality of food contamination claims in this period crossed product lines as consumers connected separate incidents.

Welch's brand parent Promotion In Motion Companies, Inc., responded publicly on Facebook with the following statement: "We are confident that the imperfection is not a tapeworm and we continue to stand behind our original statement in which our product experts believe with a high level of certainty that the imperfection shown is nothing more than a fruit snack piece with excess product drizzled on the exterior. This means the snack, while unusual in appearance, is completely safe for consumption. A recall is not warranted and will not be issued" (Promotion In Motion Companies, as reported by Mikkelson, 2018).

This explanation—excess product on the exterior—is technically coherent with the manufacturing process for gelatin-based fruit snacks. These products are typically produced through a batch deposit process in which a hot, liquid gelatin-sugar-pectin mixture is metered from a depositor head into starch or silicone molds where it cools and sets. The depositor heads can occasionally drizzle or trail small amounts of mixture beyond the mold cavity, producing surface irregularities on the finished product. These irregularities can look like attached strings or filaments of color contrasting with the main piece, depending on the coloring of the product formula.

Sassenach's counter-account was that her doctor's laboratory had tested the item in question and determined it was a tapeworm "dehydrated into the piece." This is a scientifically problematic description. Tapeworms—members of the class Cestoda—are endoparasitic organisms that require a living host to complete all life cycle stages that produce infective material. The mechanisms by which a tapeworm would come to be "dehydrated into" a commercially manufactured fruit snack piece, which undergoes high-temperature processing, are difficult to construct biologically. However, Sassenach's claim that she would provide the lab report to Snopes but had not yet done so—and per Mikkelson (2018) apparently never did—means the laboratory finding remains entirely unverified. The claim that such a test was performed cannot be confirmed, and the specific nature of what a physician's office lab would use to "identify" a dehydrated cestode fragment versus a gelatin manufacturing artifact is unclear.

The company's statement that "following multiple attempts, we have been unable to obtain the sample or get the product to an independent third-party for testing" and that "we have not received any documentation that shows the consumer had this product tested" establishes the evidentiary dead end even more starkly. The sample—the single object at the center of the dispute—was apparently neither preserved by the company nor made available to regulatory authorities for examination.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Biology of Cestoda and the Implausibility of Tapeworm Contamination in Manufactured Snacks

The epistemic novelty of this case lies in what rigorously applying parasite biology to the claim reveals. "Tapeworm" is a colloquial umbrella term for cestode parasites, of which hundreds of species are known to parasitize vertebrates. The species most relevant to human health include Taenia solium (pork tapeworm), Taenia saginata (beef tapeworm), Hymenolepis nana (dwarf tapeworm), Echinococcus granulosus (dog tapeworm), and Diphyllobothrium latum (fish tapeworm). Each of these species has a complex life cycle involving definitive and intermediate hosts. The infective life cycle stages—proglottids, eggs, or larvae depending on the species—are found in specific tissues of specific host animals and are transmitted through specific dietary or environmental exposure pathways.

The critical biological point for evaluating the contamination claim is this: tapeworm material does not spontaneously appear in fruit products. Welch's Fruit Snacks are manufactured from fruit juice concentrates, corn syrup, sugar, gelatin, modified food starch, citric acid, and food-grade coloring and flavoring agents. None of these ingredients represents a plausible source of cestode material. T. saginata metacestodes (cysticerci) are found in beef muscle tissue; T. solium cysticerci are found in pork muscle; D. latum plerocercoids are found in freshwater fish muscle. None of these tissues appears in the manufacturing formula for fruit snacks.

The theoretical pathway by which tapeworm material could enter the product supply chain would require either: (a) gross contamination of a raw ingredient with animal tissue harboring a cestode infection—essentially impossible given the botanical and mineral nature of fruit snack ingredients; or (b) contamination introduced during manufacturing by a specific and relatively complex chain of events including an infected worker, cestodan material on that worker's hands or equipment, and transfer into the production batch before gelling. This pathway is not literally impossible, but it strains biological plausibility to a significant degree and would require the organism to have survived the high-temperature gelatin processing step in a form identifiable as a tapeworm.

Dehydrated cestode proglottids—the detachable reproductive segments shed in feces and the most likely macro-identifiable tapeworm material—do have a characteristic flat, granular, rice-grain appearance (García & Bruckner, 2007, *Diagnostic Medical Parasitology*, 5th ed., ASM Press). This appearance is morphologically distinct from extruded gelatin manufacturing artifacts, and a trained parasitologist examining an appropriately prepared specimen could distinguish between them. The claim made by the consumer that a "doctor's lab" performed this differentiation is plausible in principle—clinical laboratories do perform ova and parasite (O&P) examinations—but without the documented test result, the consumer's characterization of what the lab found cannot be accepted as evidence.

The absence of similar reports through official food safety channels is also informative. The FDA's MedWatch and consumer complaint systems, which track reports of adverse events associated with food products, show no documented confirmed tapeworm isolation from any commercially manufactured fruit snack product in the publicly available record. The multi-decade absence of such a confirmed event in commercial confectionery is consistent with the biological implausibility analysis above.

## §4 Manufacturing Process: Fruit Snacks and the "Excess Product" Explanation

The company's alternative explanation—that the visible protrusion was "excess product drizzled on the exterior"—deserves technical elaboration. The manufacturing of gelatin-based fruit snacks involves several steps at which surface irregularities can be introduced; understanding those steps contextualizes the manufacturer's claim.

Gelatin-based fruit snack manufacturing typically begins with batch preparation, in which gelatin, pectin, corn syrup, sugar, and fruit juice concentrate are heated and mixed in large vessels until a homogeneous liquid is achieved. Coloring and flavoring agents are added, and the resulting syrup is held at temperature (typically 70-85°C) to prevent premature gelling. Continuous depositor systems then meter precise volumes of this mixture into shaped molds—starch-lined trays in traditional starch mogul processing, or silicone molds in newer "starchless" lines—where the pieces cool and gel.

The depositor mechanism involves a nozzle or series of nozzles through which the hot gelatin syrup flows. When the metering mechanism cuts off the flow to a mold, a small residual "string" or "tail" of product can form between the nozzle and the newly deposited piece, particularly if the cutoff is not mechanically clean. This tail either retracts into the nozzle, falls back onto the piece as the depositor moves, or adheres to the surface of the newly deposited piece as it cools. When gelatin solidifies, even small surface-adhered amounts can take on a fibrous, string-like appearance that is physically quite different from the bulk texture of the piece itself—particularly if the surface strand cools more rapidly than the interior deposit, or if slight color variation between differently concentrated regions produces a visible difference.

This mechanism is documented in the confectionery engineering literature. Hartel, von Elbe, and Hofberger (2018, *Confectionery Science and Technology*, Springer) describe the phenomenon of "tailing" in gelatin-deposit confectionery as a known process artifact that can be minimized through depositor design and temperature control optimization but is not fully eliminable at commercial production speeds. Visual examination of a commercial fruit snack piece with a surface tail would show an elongated protrusion of similar material to the piece itself, often with a tapered or string-like form—a description plausibly consistent with what the consumer's photograph appears to show.

## §5 Legal and Regulatory Framework: Foreign Object Contamination Standards

The regulatory treatment of alleged foreign object contamination in packaged food products in the United States involves overlapping FDA and company authority, and the Welch's case illustrates how that regulatory interface functions in a low-evidence situation.

Under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, a food product that contains an "added poisonous or deleterious substance" or "any filth" is legally adulterated. Foreign objects—including insect or parasite material—that are not naturally part of a food's composition and that would cause the product to be injurious to health or unacceptable to consumers fall under this provision. When a company receives a consumer complaint alleging foreign object contamination, FDA guidance recommends that the company attempt to obtain and examine the sample, investigate the relevant production batch for potential points of entry of the claimed contaminant, and consider whether a voluntary recall is warranted.

In this case, Promotion In Motion initiated the correct first step—attempting to obtain the sample—and was unable to complete that step due to the consumer not providing the piece. Without physical evidence, neither the company nor the FDA can conduct the investigation that would support or negate the contamination claim. The FDA's decision on whether to open an investigation in response to a single unverified consumer complaint, without physical sample or supporting laboratory documentation, would typically be influenced by the product category, the plausibility of the claimed contamination given the manufacturing process, and the absence of additional similar reports. None of those factors supported escalation in this case.

It is worth noting that the FDA maintains a Consumer Complaint Coordinator network in which consumers can report food safety concerns directly to the agency. Direct reporting to the FDA rather than exclusively through social media would have created an official record and potentially triggered an independent request for the sample from the consumer. Whether Sassenach made such a report is not established in the available record.

## §6 Conclusion: Sample Preservation and the Chain of Custody Problem

The Welch's fruit snack tapeworm claim ultimately founders on what might be called the chain of custody problem in consumer food safety disputes. The physical object at the center of the dispute—the claimed tapeworm piece—was neither preserved independently by the consumer nor successfully obtained by the manufacturer for independent verification, and no documented third-party laboratory result was ever produced or confirmed. The outcome is structurally identical to the Chef Boyardee case from the same period: a viral claim, a corporate denial, a plausible alternative manufacturer explanation, and an unresolvable evidentiary gap.

Mikkelson (2018) appropriately characterized the situation as unproven. Multiple other Facebook users reported "finding something similar" in their Welch's fruit snacks during and after the viral spread of the original post, which Mikkelson noted as consistent with either a widespread manufacturing artifact or a widespread tapeworm problem—but is also consistent with a third explanation: confirmation bias leading unaffected consumers to attend to and misidentify common manufacturing irregularities as potential contamination, heightened by the salience of the viral claim.

The systemic lesson is the same as in the Chef Boyardee case but with an additional element: in this case, the consumer reportedly had access to a laboratory test result that would have definitively resolved the dispute but did not produce it. This is not necessarily evidence of bad faith; people are poor archivists of documentation they may not expect to need. But it underscores the importance of documentation preservation in food safety disputes. Consumers who believe they have discovered a genuine contaminant in a packaged food product are best served by: (1) photographing the object thoroughly with contextual detail; (2) placing the object in a clean, sealed container without further handling; (3) contacting both the manufacturer's consumer services line and the FDA Consumer Complaint Coordinators; and (4) retaining any laboratory documentation obtained. These steps cost little and substantially improve the evidentiary quality of any subsequent claim.
