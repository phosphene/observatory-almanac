## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

The claim under investigation is that Welsh rabbit (also known as Welsh rarebit) is a meat-based dish prepared with rabbit meat. In fact, the dish contains no rabbit whatsoever—it is, at its core, a preparation of melted or cooked cheese served on toasted bread, typically seasoned with mustard, Worcestershire sauce, and sometimes ale. The claim that rabbit meat is a component is False, rated as such by Snopes founder David Mikkelson (2021) in an investigation that focused not only on the dish's actual ingredients but on the dual etymological puzzles attached to it: how a cheese-and-toast preparation came to bear the name "Welsh rabbit," and how that name subsequently mutated into the even more curious form "Welsh rarebit."

These are, in Mikkelson's (2021) framing, "one of the more puzzling etymological mysteries in the culinary world"—a double riddle in which the name both misidentifies the dish's contents and exists in two variant forms whose relationship to each other is itself contested. The Oxford English Dictionary places the first documented print occurrence of "Welsh rabbit" in 1725, while "Welsh rarebit" appears not to have made it into print until 1781—a chronological gap that the OED and most linguistic authorities interpret as evidence that "rarebit" is a corruption of "rabbit" rather than the reverse, though alternative narratives have been proposed.

This entry examines the linguistic and culinary history of Welsh rabbit/rarebit, the competing theories for the origin of the name, the food-cultural ecology of eighteenth-century Britain in which the name arose, the etymology of similar "mock" food names using ethnonyms (geographic humorous mislabeling), and the persistent popular belief—addressed and refuted by the Snopes investigation—that the dish somehow contains rabbit meat.

## §2 The Factual Core: Ingredients and Preparation

Welsh rabbit/rarebit is a dish whose modern canonical form involves the preparation of a cooked cheese sauce—typically combining mature Cheddar cheese, Welsh ale or beer (though non-alcoholic versions exist), English mustard, Worcestershire sauce, and sometimes cayenne pepper or paprika—which is either poured over or melted atop slices of toasted bread and then briefly finished under a broiler or grill to achieve a lightly browned surface. Variations include the addition of a poached egg on top (a preparation sometimes called "buck rarebit" or "golden buck"), the use of a raw egg beaten into the cheese mixture, and purely simple versions consisting of nothing more than sliced cheese melted onto toast under a grill.

No ingredient in any canonical version of the dish involves rabbit, hare, or any other meat. The naming confusion is entirely a linguistic phenomenon and not reflective of any meat-free recipe evolution: the dish was never, at any point in its documented history, made with rabbit meat. This is confirmed by the earliest recipe records. A recipe in the 1747 edition of Hannah Glasse's *The Art of Cookery Made Plain and Easy*—one of the most influential English-language cookbooks of the eighteenth century—includes a recipe for "Welch Rabbit" that consists of cheese melted with ale on toasted bread. There is no version of the recipe in Glasse's text or in any other historical cookery source that includes rabbit meat, confirming that the name has been a false descriptor from the dish's earliest documented existence.

Mikkelson (2021) confirmed this directly: "Welsh rabbit/rarebit is simply toasted bread topped with a melted cheese mixture. It contains no meat whatsoever, rabbit or otherwise." The confirmation is not contested in any credible culinary historical source.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Ethno-Toponymic Mock-Name Tradition and the Semiotics of Geographic Food Labeling

The epistemic novelty in this investigation resides in what it reveals about a specific and historically persistent practice in English-language culinary naming: the application of geographic or ethnic labels to foods with no genuine connection to those geographies or peoples, typically as vehicles for class-based or ethno-national mockery, regional humor, or commercial mystification. The Welsh rabbit is not an isolated case; it belongs to a well-documented tradition of mock-names that simultaneously encode social attitude and semantic misdirection.

Mikkelson (2021) cites a passage from Henry Wharton Griffith's 1843 book *A Lift for the Lazy* that provides one of the most analytically rich discussions of this tradition from the period. Griffith's text invokes several parallel examples: "Albany beef" (sturgeon abundant in the Albany, New York market), "Bristol milk" (Sherris sack, or sherry wine, traditional in Bristol hospitality), "Nuremberg eggs" (early pocket watches manufactured in Nuremberg, shaped like eggs), and "Scotch warming-pan" (from a story about a Scottish servant warming a bed with her own body heat). Each of these examples involves the same semantic structure: a geographic or ethnic label applied to a referent that is not literally the labeled thing, with the label performing one of three functions—origin marking, behavioral stereotype amplification, or exotic-premium signaling.

The linguistic anthropology of ethno-toponymic labeling in English food culture has been analyzed by Quinion (2004, *Ballyhoo, Buckaroo, and Spuds*, Smithsonian Books), who identifies a cluster of "X + ethnic/geographic adjective" constructions in English culinary vocabulary that collectively encode eighteenth- and nineteenth-century British class and regional hierarchy. "Welsh rabbit" belongs to a subgroup that Quinion terms "poverty jokes"—names given to dishes or substitutes that are ironically labeled as if they were the expensive or prestigious food for which they substitute. In this framing, Welsh rabbit says: poor people (specifically the Welsh, stereotyped in English popular culture as too poor to afford even the humble rabbit) make do with cheese on toast and call it rabbit because that is the closest they can come to the real thing.

This analysis connects to the scholarship on what Leith (2013, *A History of Food*, Reaktion Books) terms "displacement naming"—a practice he traces from medieval English culinary manuscripts in which cheap or substitute ingredients were given the names of luxury or aspirational foods to make them more palatable to consumers or to allow them to be sold at a premium. The distinction from the Welsh rabbit case is that displacement naming is typically apologetic (disguising a substitution), while ethno-toponymic mock-naming is satirical (displaying a putative cultural or economic deficiency of the named group for humorous effect). Welsh rabbit sits at the intersection of both functions: the Welsh are mocked as impoverished, while the cook's use of cheese as a meat substitute is implicitly acknowledged.

The later emergence of "Welsh rarebit" as an alternative name adds a second semantic layer. Mikkelson (2021) cites several theories for the mutation: polite correction intended to remove the implied ethnic slur, commercial rebranding by restaurateurs seeking either to avoid confusing unfamiliar customers or to create an exotic-sounding menu item commanding a premium price, or simply a phonological drift process in which "rabbit" became "rarebit" through hearing-based misidentification. Hendrickson (2004, *The Facts on File Encyclopedia of Word and Phrase Origins*) supports the restaurateur-rebranding theory: the "rarebit" form, absent from the documentary record until 1781, first appears in contexts suggesting commercial food service rather than domestic cookery, consistent with a deliberate euphemism introduced for marketing purposes.

The commercial rebranding hypothesis has interesting implications for how we understand culinary language change more broadly. If "rarebit" was introduced by food service operators to make the dish sound less ethnically pejorative (or more exotic), it represents an early documented instance of restaurant menu euphemism—a practice that has become ubiquitous in contemporary commercial food culture, where ugly or informal food names are routinely replaced by appealing alternatives (Jurafsky, 2014, *The Language of Food*, Norton). The frequency with which "Welsh rarebit" appears today compared to "Welsh rabbit" (a reversal of the original chronological relationship) suggests that the commercial rebranding, if that is what it was, ultimately succeeded.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Mikkelson's (2021) investigation is authoritative for its core factual claims and cites the relevant linguistic reference works, but several dimensions of the topic warrant further development.

**The Welsh perspective**: The investigation discusses the dish's name as a product of English mockery of the Welsh but does not address whether Welsh food culture or Welsh historians have any view of the name or its etymology. The question of how the Welsh themselves have historically received or responded to the name—whether with indifference, reclamation, or objection—is entirely absent from the investigation. Contemporary Welsh culinary advocacy groups have, in fact, made efforts to establish Welsh rarebit as a dish of pride rather than mockery, including formal applications to the European Union's Protected Geographical Indication scheme for traditional regional foods.

**Recipe evolution**: The investigation establishes what the dish is and where the name came from but does not trace the culinary evolution of the recipe itself. Welsh rabbit/rarebit recipes vary considerably across the historical record: the 1747 Glasse recipe differs from the late Victorian versions popularized by Escoffier-influenced restaurants, which differ from Welsh pub versions, which differ from the standardized recipe published by the Welsh government in contemporary tourism materials. This recipe variation is not irrelevant to the epistemics of the name: if the dish had ever been made with rabbit-infused stock, rabbit fat, or any other leporine ingredient at any historical moment, the False verdict would require qualification.

**The "rarebit" as non-meat "bit": alternative etymology**: Mikkelson (2021) does not mention one alternative etymology found in some culinary history sources: that "rarebit" may be a folk-etymological construction from "rare bit" meaning a choice or pleasant morsel—a retroactive lexicalization of "rarebit" as a compound meaning "precious little piece" rather than as a corruption of "rabbit." This alternative etymology cannot be authenticated but cannot be definitively ruled out either, and its existence in the scholarly conversation is worth noting.

**Cross-cultural parallels**: The investigation does not mention that similar ethno-toponymic mock-food names exist in other European languages, which would strengthen the argument that the naming practice reflects a wider European pattern rather than an exclusively English cultural quirk. In French, "fromage du pays de Galles" (Welsh cheese) was apparently used in some contexts; in German culinary vocabulary, there are analogs in the "arm" (poor) + food category naming convention.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Welsh Rabbit in British Food Culture and the Persistence of False Folk Etymologies

Welsh rabbit/rarebit's cultural longevity as an object of linguistic confusion is partly explained by the structural characteristics of folk etymology—the cognitive tendency to rationalize opaque or archaic names by reinterpreting them through familiar referents. When contemporary English speakers encounter the name "Welsh rabbit" for a dish containing no rabbit, the mismatch generates a search for explanation. Folk etymology satisfies this search by either inventing a story about how the dish once contained rabbit before the recipe was simplified, or by asserting that "rarebit" is the correct, original form and that "rabbit" is a corruption of the archaic and supposedly more meaningful "rarebit." Both of these folk etymological moves are demonstrably incorrect given the documentary record, but both feel semantically satisfying in a way that "we simply don't know exactly why the Welsh were associated with cheese on toast in 1725" does not.

The persistence of the claim that Welsh rabbit contains rabbit meat specifically is a particularly pure case of what Mehl (2019, *Folklore Studies in the Twenty-First Century*, Routledge) calls "ingredient-name folk etymology"—the assumption that food names transparently encode their contents, and therefore that any animal name in a food product must indicate that animal as an ingredient. This assumption is repeatedly defeated by culinary history: Scotch woodcock contains no woodcock (it is a prepared anchovy and egg dish), poor knights of Windsor contains no knights (it is a form of bread pudding), Angels on Horseback contains no angels or horses (it is oysters wrapped in bacon), and so on. The pattern is widespread enough that etymological research into unusual English food names has become a minor specialty within culinary historical scholarship.

Griffith (1843) and Quinion (2004) between them document this tradition as self-consciously performative—English diners in the public house context understood that "Welsh rabbit" was a joke, not a literal description of the dish's contents. The humor was available only to those who understood the ethnic caricature embedded in the name. The modern confusion—in which the joke's punchline has been lost and the name is taken literally—represents a secondary meaning displacement in which the original ironic register has been forgotten and the name is read at face value.

The Snopes claim investigated here—that Welsh rabbit/rarebit is a "meat-based dish prepared with rabbit"—is a direct product of this meaning displacement. It is the kind of error that becomes especially plausible in an era when the ethnic-mockery traditions of eighteenth-century British food culture are no longer part of the common cultural knowledge needed to decode the name as a joke.

## §6 Verdict Assessment and Research Gaps

Mikkelson's (2021) **False** verdict is unambiguously supported by the culinary, linguistic, and historical evidence. No version of Welsh rabbit/rarebit at any documented point in its history has contained rabbit meat. The dish's name is a product of ethno-toponymic mock-naming (the "poverty joke" tradition in English food culture) and not a literal description of its contents. The OED's chronological evidence strongly supports the interpretation that "Welsh rarebit" is a secondary euphemistic corruption of "Welsh rabbit" rather than its ancestor.

**Epistemic classification**: This is a case of *historical etymology and culinary factual verification*—confirming that a named dish does not contain the ingredient its name suggests, and tracing why the name exists in the first place. The evidence base combines lexicographical authority (OED), culinary historical records (Glasse, 1747), and scholarly linguistic analysis (Quinion, 2004; Hendrickson, 2004).

**EARC gap A (Archival)**: The primary research gap is the absence of archival evidence for Welsh perspectives on the name and any potential Welsh culinary history of the dish itself. It is entirely possible that cheese-on-toast preparations existed in Welsh cuisine independently before being named with the English caricature framing, in which case the relationship between the dish's actual origins and the English mock-name would be more complex than the investigation acknowledges. Secondary gaps include the missing recipe evolution history and the underexplored possibility of a "rare bit" folk etymology.

**Research vectors**: The most productive next steps would involve examination of early Welsh-language cookery manuscripts, Welsh monastery records, and Welsh domestic household accounts for evidence of cheese-on-toast preparations predating 1725. The Oxford Food Symposium proceedings and the historical collection at Prospect Books would be appropriate venues for this work. The Welsh Government's food heritage documentation program may have already gathered relevant evidence.
