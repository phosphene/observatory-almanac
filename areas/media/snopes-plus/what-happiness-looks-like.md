# "Is This 'Walking' Protein What Happiness Really Looks Like?"


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: A viral animated GIF circulating since at least 2014 shows a protein with two "legs" that appears to walk along a cellular filament while carrying a large, undulating spherical cargo. Posts sharing this GIF describe it as follows: "Molecules of the protein myosin drag a ball of endorphins along an active filament into the inner part of the brain's parietal cortex, which produces feelings of happiness." The visual, along with this description, frames the GIF as a literal depiction of the biology of happiness — the moment when a molecule "delivers" the subjective feeling of joy to the relevant brain region.

**Claim specificity and multi-layered nature**: The viral claim is not a single assertion but a constellation of interconnected sub-claims, each of which can be evaluated independently:
- That the protein depicted is myosin (rather than some other motor protein)
- That the cargo being transported is endorphins (rather than some other molecular payload)
- That the destination is the parietal cortex (rather than some other cellular location)
- That this action "produces feelings of happiness" (causal claim about affective neuroscience)
- That the animation depicts a real observed event rather than a computer-generated simulation

**Viral trajectory**: The GIF first appeared on Imgur in 2014 and subsequently spread to 9gag, Reddit, Twitter, Facebook, and dozens of content aggregation platforms. As of the original Snopes investigation in 2017, the image had accumulated millions of shares. The description varies slightly across platforms — some versions emphasize wonder ("This is what happiness really looks like!"), others take a pseudo-educational tone, and others embed the claim in broader content about the mind-body connection, the science of positive emotions, or evidence for the physiological basis of happiness. Regardless of framing, the core narrative — myosin + endorphins + parietal cortex = happiness — has remained stable across versions.

**Emotional resonance of the claim**: Part of the claim's virality derives from its elegance. If true, it would mean we could point to a single, visible moment in the biology of subjective experience — a tangible, almost mechanical image of the cellular delivery of joy. This provides an appealing bridge between the abstract complexity of affective neuroscience and something a non-specialist can look at and immediately comprehend. The "walking" protein is memorable precisely because it looks immediately anthropomorphic: the gait, the cargo, the purposeful motion. It feels like it *should* be a depiction of something meaningful about the human experience of happiness.

**Why the error propagates**: In the era of social media content aggregation, scientific animations and images routinely become detached from their original context and captions. Once an image begins circulating with an inaccurate description, that description becomes the default context for all subsequent viewers who encounter the image without independent curiosity about its origin. The description gains credibility by iteration — audiences reason, implicitly, that something shared so widely must have been verified by someone upstream. The misattribution becomes self-reinforcing.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The real source of the animation**: The animated GIF is a fragment of a computer-generated three-dimensional scientific visualization originally created by medical artist and animator John Lieber. The fragment appears in a 2006 short film titled "The Inner Life of the Cell," produced collaboratively by Lieber and two Harvard University scientists — specifically, the Cell Biology Department — as an educational tool for visualizing molecular processes inside eukaryotic cells. The full film, approximately eight minutes long, takes the viewer through dozens of cellular structures and processes, rendered in vivid color with a degree of artistic interpretive license that makes the cellular interior look more like an underwater ballet than like the densely packed, largely monochromatic environment seen in actual electron microscopy.

**The protein is kinesin, not myosin**: The protein depicted in the viral GIF is a kinesin motor protein. Both kinesin and myosin are classified as molecular motor proteins — they use chemical energy derived from the hydrolysis of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to generate mechanical movement against cytoskeletal filaments. However, they are distinct proteins with different structures, different primary motor substrates, and different functional profiles. Kinesin moves along microtubules (long, hollow protein polymers), while myosin is best known for its role in muscle contraction, where it moves along actin filaments. The iconic bilateral "walk" shown in the GIF — coordinated steps, alternating "feet" taking bipedal strides along the filament — is characteristic of kinesin's processive movement. Myosin, in its classical muscle-contraction role, produces a different mechanical action: a power stroke rather than a coordinated alternating gait. This distinction was confirmed in a 2000 review published by Vale and Milligan in *Science*, "The Way Things Move: Looking Under the Hood of Molecular Motor Proteins" (Vale, R.D. and Milligan, R.A., 2000, *Science*, vol. 288, pp. 88–95), which detailed the structural and mechanistic differences between kinesin and myosin families.

**The kinesin walk was first characterized in 1993**: The fundamental mechanics of kinesin locomotion were first directly measured in a landmark paper published in *Nature* in 1993 by Svoboda et al., using optical trap technology that allowed researchers to observe and measure individual kinesin steps for the first time (Svoboda, K. et al., 1993, *Nature*, vol. 365, pp. 721–727). Each step was measured at approximately 8 nanometers — corresponding to the spacing between tubulin subunits in microtubule lattices. This paper establishes that kinesin's "walking" motion is real, quantified, and well-characterized — though the motion as depicted in the GIF is artistically exaggerated for visual clarity and cannot be directly observed with current microscopy tools at this level of clarity and color.

**The cargo is not endorphins**: Endorphins are neuropeptides — relatively small signaling molecules averaging approximately 31 amino acids for the beta-endorphin variant — produced primarily in the pituitary gland and hypothalamus in response to stress or pain. They interact with opioid receptors throughout the brain and produce analgesia and mild euphoric effects. However, the large, undulating spherical object depicted in the viral GIF is visually far too large to represent a neuropeptide. The animation's "cargo" most closely resembles a vesicle — a membrane-enclosed intracellular transport package that can carry a variety of molecular cargo, including neurotransmitters, lipids, and proteins. The "Inner Life of the Cell" film depicts the transport of a vesicle along the cytoskeletal filament, not the transport of a specific endorphin molecule. Lieber's own description of the film confirms that it depicts processes occurring within a leukocyte (white blood cell), which does not produce or release endorphins.

**The location is not the parietal cortex**: The animation depicts events inside a white blood cell (leukocyte), as confirmed by the film's creators and by the cellular structures depicted (including the exterior membrane, internal cytoskeletal network, and vesicular transport systems characteristic of immune cells). It does not depict a neuron, which is the cell type that would be relevant to the claim about parietal cortex function. White blood cells do not inhabit the parietal cortex; they circulate in the bloodstream and tissues of the immune system. Any claim about the parietal cortex's role in producing happiness feelings would require a depiction of neuronal, not leukocytic, processes.

**Kinesin's actual neurological role**: While kinesin is not doing anything related to happiness in the animation, kinesin motor proteins do play significant real roles in neurons. A comprehensive 2009 review by Hirokawa et al. in *Nature Reviews Molecular Cell Biology* documented the kinesin superfamily's role in axonal transport — the long-distance movement of cargo from neuronal cell bodies down axonal processes to synaptic terminals (Hirokawa, N. et al., 2009, *Nature Reviews Molecular Cell Biology*, vol. 10, pp. 682–696). This includes transport of synaptic vesicles pre-loaded with neurotransmitters, mitochondria, mRNA, and structural proteins. Disrupted axonal transport by kinesin has been implicated in neurodegenerative diseases including Alzheimer's and Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease. So kinesin is genuinely important to neuroscience — just not in the oversimplified, happiness-delivering way described in the viral caption.

**The animation is not actual microscopy**: Current microscopy technology cannot produce real-time, colored, three-dimensional video of single protein molecules with the resolution and clarity shown in the GIF. The best available tools — including cryo-electron microscopy, atomic force microscopy, and super-resolution fluorescence microscopy — can reveal molecular structures and movements, but not with the visual richness and interpretive color-coding of Lieber's animation. The GIF is explicitly an artistic computer simulation designed to convey conceptual knowledge, not to faithfully represent the actual visual appearance of cellular processes.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty — the authority of the beautiful visualization**:

The viral claim about happiness and kinesin endures not primarily because of deliberate deception but because of a deep epistemological vulnerability that modern scientific communication has created: the authoritative beautiful image.

For most of human history, scientific knowledge was communicated in written or oral form — prose descriptions, mathematical notation, and occasionally diagrams. Lay audiences could not access the phenomena being described; they were always filtered through the mediating layer of language. A claim about molecular biology was, for non-specialists, necessarily abstract.

Modern scientific visualization tools — computer rendering, high-resolution microscopy, simulation software — have changed this fundamentally. It is now possible to create images and animations that *appear* to depict scientific phenomena with photographic directness. The GIF of the kinesin protein looks, to untrained eyes, like it might be actual microscopy footage. The protein "looks" real — it has mass, inertia, articulation. The cargo "looks" like a real spherical object being physically dragged. The "walk" looks like something that could be directly observed.

This is a new form of epistemological trap with no historical precedent. Lay audiences have excellent calibration tools for distinguishing "drawing of a molecule" from "photo of a molecule" — the drawing looks schematic and diagrammatic, while the photo would look blurry, indistinct, and grainy (as actual electron microscopy images do). But the computer-generated animation occupies a visual middle ground: it is neither the hand-drawn diagram nor the real-world photo. Its visual richness and three-dimensionality make it feel more like a documentary than like an illustration.

When a caption is attached to such an animation making strong empirical claims — "this is myosin, this is endorphins, this is the parietal cortex" — the audience has no internal signal telling them the caption might be wrong. The animation already *looks* authoritative; the scientific-sounding caption completes the illusion of verified fact. The audience absorbs the claim without skepticism because the epistemological scaffolding that would normally trigger questions — "is this artist's rendering or documentary?" — has been bypassed by the medium itself.

There is also a secondary epistemic mechanism: the biological science of happiness is genuinely complex, contested, and poorly understood at the molecular scale. Audiences who know that happiness is "something in the brain" and that "chemicals cause it" have an accurate but underspecified model that the viral caption fills in with appealing false specificity. The claim gives them a story — myosin carries endorphins to the parietal cortex — that is simple, visual, and satisfying. It resolves genuine scientific uncertainty with a confident, shareable narrative. This is the basic architecture of most viral scientific misinformation: it finds genuine complexity and replaces it with appealing false simplicity.


## 4. The Broader Pattern

The kinesin-endorphin-happiness myth belongs to a well-documented category of scientific misinformation characterized by what might be called **caption drift**: legitimate scientific images or animations become detached from accurate descriptions and reattached to appealing but incorrect ones. The images themselves are real (the GIF exists, the protein exists, the video was made by real scientists) — only the interpretive layer is fabricated or distorted.

Similar caption-drift events have affected other scientific visualizations: photographs of real neurons misidentified as images of the brain "in love" or "on drugs"; real thermographic images misrepresented as evidence of human auras; real microscopy of biological structures labeled as paranormal phenomena. In each case, the image functions as a false witness — lending visual credibility to claims it does not actually support.

The persistence of kinesin-as-happiness across nearly a decade (2014 to the present) also illustrates how correction fails in networked information environments. The Snopes fact-check was published in 2017, three years after the initial viral spread. By that point, the false caption had been associated with the GIF across millions of copies on dozens of platforms. Correcting one instance of the misattribution has no mechanical effect on the remaining millions. Each new platform that hosts the image without encountering the correction regenerates the false narrative independently. This is the asymmetry at the heart of viral misinformation: false claims distribute effortlessly through sharing, while corrections require active seeking and encounter audience resistance from the prior exposure.

Animator John Lieber's original artistic intent — to communicate the wonder and complexity of cellular machinery — was fully realized by the viral spread, but in an inverted form. The animation succeeded in communicating awe at molecular processes. The false caption succeeded in converting that awe into factual misinformation. The result is a large population of people who feel they understand the biology of happiness but who in fact hold a set of false specific beliefs while carrying an accurate general intuition (that cellular processes underlie subjective experience).


## 5. Evidentiary Assessment

**EARC assignment: R (Refuted)**

The core claim ("myosin carries endorphins to the parietal cortex to produce happiness") is refuted on every individual dimension:

- **Protein identity**: The visualized protein is kinesin, not myosin. This identification is confirmed by the animation's creator (Lieber, 2014, Art of the Cell blog), by structural analysis of the depicted locomotion mechanics, and by the scientific literature on motor protein mechanics (Vale & Milligan, 2000, *Science* 288:88–95).

- **Cargo identity**: The cargo depicted is a vesicle, not endorphins. Endorphins are neuropeptides too small to be visually depicted at this scale as a large undulating sphere. The "Inner Life of the Cell" film does not claim to depict endorphin transport.

- **Cellular location**: The cell depicted is a leukocyte, not a neuron in the parietal cortex. This is confirmed by the film's creators and by the anatomical features of the depicted cell.

- **Causal happiness claim**: No scientific evidence establishes that any single molecular transport event maps onto the subjective experience of happiness. The neuroscience of affective states involves distributed neural circuits, multiple neurotransmitter systems (including but not limited to endorphins, dopamine, serotonin, and oxytocin), and long-timescale developmental and experiential factors. Reducing "happiness" to a single molecular delivery event misrepresents the state of affective neuroscience.

- **Observational status**: The animation is a computer-generated simulation, not a direct observation. This is confirmed by the creator and by the technical impossibility of current microscopy to produce equivalent real-time recordings.

**Gap category: scientific-visualization**

This entry addresses a persistent gap in public understanding of how scientific visualizations differ from scientific observations. The gap operates at the metacognitive level: audiences lack clear schemas for distinguishing an artist's rendering from documentary evidence, particularly when the rendering employs photorealistic three-dimensional animation techniques.


## 6. Verdict and Implications

**Verdict**: The claim is **false**. The animated GIF shows a computer-generated visualization of a kinesin motor protein transporting a vesicle within a white blood cell — not myosin transporting endorphins to the parietal cortex to produce happiness. Every specific claim in the viral caption is inaccurate.

**What is true**: The underlying biology, stripped of the false caption, is genuinely remarkable. Kinesin motor proteins do exist, do "walk" along microtubules with a recognizable bipedal gait, and do transport essential cargo within cells. The movement of molecular motors is one of the most striking examples of mechanical work at the nanoscale in all of biology. The animation is based on real science, rendered by a skilled artist collaborating with Harvard scientists. The awe the GIF provokes is epistemically appropriate even if directed at the wrong object.

**Implications for scientific literacy**: The kinesin-happiness myth represents a case where the failure mode is not contempt for science but excessive reverence for an image that appears scientific. Audiences who share this GIF are typically motivated by genuine wonder at biology; their error is not anti-scientific but mis-calibrated trust in visual representation. The corrective for this specific failure mode is not more skepticism about science in general, but more sophisticated visual literacy about the difference between simulation and observation — a distinction that the production quality of modern scientific visualization increasingly effaces.

**Lasting value of the real science**: The molecular mechanisms by which kinesins transport cargo across vast cellular distances — relevant particularly in neuroscience, where axonal transport can cover distances up to a meter in human motor neurons — remain an active and important research area. Disruptions in kinesin-mediated axonal transport have been linked to Alzheimer's disease pathology (Hirokawa et al., 2010, *Nature Neuroscience*), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and Huntington's disease. The real kinesin story is arguably more interesting than the viral myth: not a simple delivery of happiness, but a critical cellular logistics system whose failure underlies some of the most devastating neurological conditions in medicine.
