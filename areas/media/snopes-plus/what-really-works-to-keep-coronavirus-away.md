# What Actually Works to Prevent Coronavirus — Evidence-Based Interventions vs. Myths

## 1. The Claim

The COVID-19 pandemic did not produce a single central false claim; it produced an ecology of competing claims about prevention, stretching from the completely fabricated to the rigorously proven, and filling every grade of evidentiary quality in between. For much of 2020 and into 2021, ordinary people around the world were simultaneously exposed to at least two distinct information streams about how to protect themselves from SARS-CoV-2: the formal public health guidance that evolved as scientific understanding accumulated, and an informal stream of folk remedies, conspiracy theories, political interventions, and commercially motivated health advice that circulated at extraordinary speed through social and messaging platforms.

The formal claims ranged from eventually well-supported (vaccines with high efficacy, high-filtration masks such as N95 respirators, improved indoor ventilation, hand hygiene) to contested (cloth masks, outdoor distancing requirements), to areas where evidence lagged guidance (school closures, surface disinfection of outdoor environments). The informal claims spanned garlic and raw ginger, drinking water every 15 minutes, holding the breath for 10 seconds to self-diagnose infection, consuming alkaline foods to maintain an "alkaline body," drinking bleach or silver ions, exposing the body to ultraviolet light, avoiding cold weather or ice cream, consuming specific herbal teas, and an extensive range of regionally specific folk remedies tightly tied to local plant pharmacological traditions.

This entry does not address a single claim to be confirmed or rejected. It addresses the full landscape — the evidence ecology — of COVID-19 prevention, mapping what the scientific record shows worked, what didn't, what we still don't fully know, and why the myth ecosystem emerged so successfully alongside the scientific one. The central meta-claim being evaluated is implicit in the public's confused exposure to this landscape: that all these interventions existed on equal or comparable evidentiary footing, and that the selection of any particular approach was merely a matter of preference or political orientation. That meta-claim is false. Some interventions worked dramatically well. Others did not work at all. The evidence base is not uniform.


## 2. What's Actually True

**What worked — the strong evidence tier**

Vaccination against SARS-CoV-2 represents the most rigorously established intervention against COVID-19 severity and death across the pandemic period. The phase III randomized controlled trials of the Pfizer-BioNTech BNT162b2 vaccine (Polack et al., 2020, New England Journal of Medicine) enrolled 43,548 participants and demonstrated 95.0% (95% CI: 90.3–97.6%) efficacy against symptomatic COVID-19 at the original SARS-CoV-2 variant. Baden et al. (2021) in NEJM reported 94.1% efficacy for the Moderna mRNA-1273 vaccine in a trial of comparable scale. Voysey et al. (2021) in The Lancet reported 70.4% efficacy for the Oxford-AstraZeneca ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 vaccine. Subsequent real-world effectiveness data — including public health surveillance data from the UK, Israel, and the United States — confirmed that two-dose mRNA vaccination substantially reduced hospitalization and death, with effectiveness against severe disease remaining high even as effectiveness against infection declined with emerging variants of concern.

Mass vaccination reduced not only individual risk but population-level transmission, contributing to the protection of immunocompromised individuals who could not mount vaccine responses and of individuals in settings where vaccine supply lagged. Post-vaccination waning immunity and the emergence of immune-evasive variants (particularly the Omicron lineage, first documented by Viana et al., 2022, in Nature) reduced effectiveness over time, but booster doses partially restored protection. The integrated summary of trial, surveillance, and mortality data across multiple countries consistently placed vaccination as the single most impactful public health intervention deployed against COVID-19 at population scale.

**High-filtration masks: separating the evidence tiers**

The mask debate became one of the most politically polarized domains of COVID-19 information, but the underlying evidence was more graduated than either "masks work" or "masks don't work" framing suggests. Filtration efficiency and fit quality are the two variables that explain most of the variance in mask effectiveness.

N95 respirators (and equivalent European FFP2/FFP3 standards), when properly fitted and used consistently, provide high filtration of aerosol particles in the size range relevant to SARS-CoV-2 transmission. Baral et al. (2021) in Emerging Infectious Diseases reviewed healthcare worker infection studies and found substantially lower infection rates among those consistently using N95 respirators compared with surgical masks. Chou et al. (2020) in Annals of Internal Medicine reviewed occupational exposure data from clinical settings and found N95 use associated with reduced infection rates when PPE supply was consistent.

Surgical masks were less well-supported as personal protective devices, primarily because of poor lateral seal at the face. However, there was accumulating evidence for their utility as source control — reducing exhaled aerosol volume from infected wearers. Wang et al. (2021) in Nature Communications documented that surgical masks substantially reduced the volume of virus-laden aerosols exhaled by infected individuals when speaking or breathing. This source control function meant that even masks with mediocre personal protective performance could reduce population-level transmission if widely adopted.

Cloth masks, which dominated 2020 community mask wearing in many settings before N95/surgical mask supplies recovered, were substantially less effective at filtration than either option and have been re-evaluated downward in more recent evidence syntheses. The Cochrane review by Jefferson et al. (2023) examined physical interventions to interrupt the spread of respiratory viruses and notably found no statistically significant benefit of mask wearing from randomized controlled trials, though the review was widely criticized by public health experts for methodological concerns including the broad aggregation of mask types and the low mask-dispensing compliance in many included trials. The controversy over the Cochrane review illustrates that the best evidence in this domain came from well-controlled quasi-experimental and observational data rather than RCTs, and that the translation of this evidence into policy recommendations required weighing imperfect evidence types.

**Ventilation and indoor air quality**

The role of airborne aerosol transmission — as distinct from larger respiratory droplets — became better understood through 2020 and 2021, with evidence converging that indoor environments with poor ventilation posed substantially higher transmission risk than outdoor settings or well-ventilated interiors. Morawska and Milton (2020) in Clinical Infectious Diseases brought together 239 scientists in an open letter citing evidence for airborne transmission of SARS-CoV-2, which was initially resisted by WHO guidance before being incorporated. Kang et al. (2020) in Building and Environment modeled airborne transmission risks in diverse indoor environments and demonstrated that air exchange rates were a primary determinant of infection risk.

Practical application of this evidence included opening windows, upgrading HVAC filtration (to HEPA or MERV-13 equivalent), deploying high-efficiency portable air purifiers, and avoiding crowded indoor spaces with limited air exchange. These interventions have the advantage of benefiting protection against multiple respiratory pathogens simultaneously and remain underemphasized relative to their evidence base and cost-effectiveness.

**Hand hygiene: protective but roles clarified over time**

Early pandemic guidance placed substantial emphasis on hand washing and surface disinfection, reflecting uncertainty about the relative importance of fomite versus airborne transmission. As epidemiological evidence accumulated, it became increasingly clear that surface-mediated transmission was a minor route relative to airborne aerosol and droplet transmission. Mondelli et al. (2021) in The Lancet Infectious Diseases reviewed evidence on fomite transmission of SARS-CoV-2 and concluded that it represented a negligible contributor to overall infection burden in most settings.

This does not mean hand hygiene is useless — handwashing after touching shared surfaces and before touching the face reduces transmission of multiple pathogens and remains a broadly supported practice — but the extraordinary emphasis on surface disinfection during early 2020, including the theatrical disinfection of outdoor streets and parks seen in many cities, was not well-calibrated to the actual transmission evidence and diverted resources and attention from the more important aerosol pathway.

**What did not work: the myth inventory**

The evidence-free interventions are numerous and have been addressed in dedicated entries elsewhere in this series; the key mythologized COVID-19 preventives included: drinking water at intervals; consuming garlic, ginger, citrus, or other foods with general antimicrobial properties; taking large doses of vitamin C; inhaling steam; using UV lamps on the skin; consuming or gargling with bleach, silver ions, or alcohol; avoiding cold water and ice cream; consuming specific herbs including black cumin (Nigella sativa), turmeric, or holy basil; and self-administering ivermectin or hydroxychloroquine in prophylactic doses outside clinical settings. None of these interventions have demonstrated efficacy against COVID-19 in rigorously designed human trials.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The COVID-19 information environment produced what the WHO formally named an "infodemic" — an overabundance of information, including both accurate and inaccurate content, making it difficult for individuals to find trustworthy guidance when they needed it. Understanding the specific mechanisms through which false prevention claims spread alongside true ones requires examining the psychology of health decision-making during existential threat.

The claim-specific mechanism most central to the spread of COVID-19 prevention myths was **the evidential vacuum exploitation pattern.** This mechanism works as follows: in the early weeks of a novel pathogen outbreak, the genuine scientific uncertainty about transmission routes, risk factors, and preventive measures creates an observable absence of authoritative specific guidance. This absence is not silence — public health bodies issue cautious, hedged statements acknowledging what is unknown — but it is experienced by non-expert audiences as a gap. The gap-filling impulse is a well-documented feature of cognition under threat; Hirschfield et al. (2020) in Psychological Science reviewing pandemic risk perception noted that ambiguity activates protective action tendencies even when those actions have no validated basis, because doing something feels better than doing nothing when a threat is perceived as both severe and unpredictable.

False prevention claims are precisely calibrated — not deliberately, but through social selection — to fill this gap. They provide specific, actionable, seemingly evidence-based guidance at exactly the moment when official guidance is either unavailable, evolving, or couched in uncertainty qualifications that can be misread as ignorance. The "15 minutes" claim fills the "when should I drink water" gap. The garlic claim fills the "what food interventions could help" gap. The "10-second breath-hold" self-diagnosis claim fills the "how do I know if I have it" gap. Each false claim addresses a genuine informational need that official sources were, at the moment of the claim's peak virality, unable to adequately address with equally specific and actionable true information.

The second claim-specific mechanism is **institutional trust asymmetry.** COVID-19 emerged into a global social environment in which institutional trust was already substantially eroded across many democracies and developing nations. Edelman Trust Barometer surveys (2020) documented historically low trust in government health agencies, media organizations, and international bodies in numerous countries. This erosion had domain-specific features relevant to health misinformation: communities with historical memories of failed public health interventions, experimental medical abuses, or pharmaceutical scandals had epistemically reasonable grounds for reduced trust in official health guidance that were then exploited by false claims. Tuskegee, thalidomide, opioid pharma fraud, and colonial-era medical paternalism each left specific communities with specific evidence for health system distrust. False prevention claims addressed to these communities could draw on this legitimate history to position themselves as trustworthy alternatives to (or warnings about) official government guidance.

This dynamic was visible and consequential in Black American communities' response to COVID-19 vaccines, documented extensively by Alsan et al. (2020) in JAMA Internal Medicine, which showed that historical exposure to medical abuses — Tuskegee in particular — was associated with lower COVID-19 vaccine uptake intentions. The false prevention claims circulating in these communities exploited legitimate historical grievances against a medical system with a documented record of abuse, borrowing credibility from that history to position ineffective alternatives as acts of informed resistance.

A third mechanism is the **social hermeneutic overlay.** Many COVID-19 prevention claims became carriers of political identity, such that the factual content became secondary to what adopting or rejecting the claim signaled about group membership. In the United States, mask use became aligned with Democratic-leaning political identity during 2020, while rejection of mandates became aligned with Republican identity. This alignment did not create the mask evidence base (which predated COVID-19 by years), but it profoundly affected how the evidence was received, interpreted, and acted upon. Once an intervention becomes a political identity marker, evaluating its evidence becomes a form of identity expression rather than a purely epistemic act. Kahan et al. (2017) in Nature Climate Change documented this dynamic for climate science and it applied with unusual vigor to COVID-19 prevention in politically polarized democracies.

A fourth mechanism is what might be called **the velocity asymmetry of true and false claims.** False COVID-19 prevention claims could be constructed and distributed in minutes; they required no laboratory infrastructure, no human subject approval, no peer review, no replication. True efficacy claims required months to years of clinical trial design, recruitment, blinding, data monitoring, statistical analysis, peer review, and publication. The temporal gap between the claim's claim and science's answer was, for vaccines, approximately 11 months from initial sequence to emergency use authorization — an unprecedented scientific achievement, but still 11 months during which the claim ecosystem operated without authoritative refutation from the most powerful evidence tier.

Vosoughi et al. (2018) in Science analyzed a dataset of 126,000 true and false news stories shared on Twitter and found that false news diffused significantly faster, farther, and more broadly than true news. The differential was driven primarily by novelty and emotional content — false claims were more novel and more emotionally arousing than true ones. A claim that "garlic cures coronavirus" is substantially more novel and emotionally resonant (hope, naturalness, self-reliance) than "hand washing reduces transmission of fomites, which are a minor route of infection." This asymmetry in emotional architecture gives false claims a systematic structural advantage in social diffusion that is not a product of unusual gullibility but of entirely predictable features of information processing under attention constraints.


## 4. Verdict

**The evidence landscape is real and differentiated.** Not all COVID-19 prevention claims were equally valid, and the existence of wide variation in evidence quality was itself obscured by the infodemic. The following summary reflects the scientific consensus as established by the date of this entry:

**Strong evidence of efficacy:** mRNA vaccines (Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna) and vector vaccines (Oxford-AstraZeneca, Johnson & Johnson) against severe COVID-19 disease and death; N95/FFP2-grade respirators used by individuals with high occupational or household exposure; improved indoor ventilation; social distancing reduction of transmission in early, high-viremia phases of infection; hand hygiene (primarily for face-touch fomite prevention).

**Moderate or conditional evidence:** Surgical masks as source control in community settings; outdoor versus indoor setting choice for extended social interaction; reduced gathering size; quarantine of confirmed and suspected cases; contact tracing during low-prevalence phases.

**Weak or absent evidence / contested:** Cloth masks as personal protectors against infection; surface disinfection as a primary prevention strategy; school closure as stand-alone intervention when weighed against educational harm; many traditional herbal preparations.

**No credible evidence:** Drinking water at intervals, garlic consumption, ginger, large-dose vitamin C as prophylaxis, UV self-treatment, alkaline diets, breath-holding self-diagnosis, colloidal silver, bleach, ivermectin in prophylactic dosing (outside clinical trials), and the many other folk remedies that circulated widely.

The responsible public health claim is that evidence-based COVID-19 prevention exists, is genuinely effective, and is categorically different from the evidential noise generated by the infodemic. Conflating them — treating vaccines as merely one option among many "prevention approaches" of roughly equal standing — is itself a form of misinformation by false equivalence.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The COVID-19 pandemic was the first major global disease outbreak to occur simultaneously with both a fully mature social media ecosystem and a global real-time scientific publication infrastructure, creating a historically unprecedented contest between institutional science and decentralized informal information production.

The pandemic's information environment revealed critical gaps in science communication infrastructure. Ryan et al. (2020) in The Lancet reviewed science communication failures during COVID-19 and identified several recurrent failures: communication of uncertainty (which was unavoidably high in early 2020) was poorly calibrated for public audiences, leading to experienced failures of expert authority when guidance changed; messaging was often insufficiently culturally tailored; and the speed of false claim production consistently outpaced formal debunking infrastructure.

The WHO's development of the Infodemic Management Framework (Fischer et al., 2020, in JMIR Public Health and Surveillance) attempted to systematize the response to pandemic misinformation, establishing taxonomies of COVID-19 false claims and coordinating resources across member-state fact-checking organizations. This represented an institutional acknowledgment that misinformation management had become a public health function requiring the same systematic infrastructure as disease surveillance — a shift in how international health governance understands the epistemic environment in which health interventions must operate.

COVID-19 prevention misinformation also illuminated the limits of simple debunking as a counter-strategy. Cook et al. (2022) in the Annual Review of Public Health synthesized inoculation theory research and found that prebunking — explaining the manipulation techniques used in misinformation before specific false claims are encountered — was more durable than post-hoc debunking in building resistance to future false claims. The practical implication is that public health communication during outbreaks should include proactive metacognitive training: explaining how false health claims are constructed, what emotional and social needs they exploit, and what markers distinguish legitimate scientific uncertainty from manufactured doubt.

The pandemic also demonstrated the costs of politicized health information in democratic systems. Price and Nir (2020) in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences documented that partisan polarization in COVID-19 behavioral responses (mask use, mobility restriction, vaccination uptake) was associated with differential mortality across US counties, suggesting that the costs of politicized health misinformation were not merely epistemic but were measurable in preventable deaths. The infodemic did not merely create confusion; it created differential survival.

The legacy of COVID-19 prevention misinformation extends to preparedness for future outbreaks. Every novel pathogen event will generate an identical informational dynamic: genuine uncertainty in early weeks of outbreak, simultaneous informal claim production filling the gap, and a race between scientific evidence production and informal claim proliferation in which informal claims currently have a structural advantage. The infrastructure needed to narrow this gap — pre-established credible communication networks, pre-approved crisis communication protocols, pre-trained public metacognitive capacity for evaluating health claims — represents the prevention layer for the next infodemic.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not published a comprehensive synthesis article examining the full landscape of COVID-19 prevention interventions and their evidence bases. The platform addressed many individual COVID-19 prevention myths in separate articles throughout 2020 and 2021 — including drinking bleach, UV light self-treatment, and various food-based claims — but does not have a dedicated piece situating these false claims relative to the evidence base for genuine protective interventions. This summary piece represents a gap in Snopes's COVID-19 coverage: a meta-analysis of the prevention claim ecosystem rather than individual claim adjudication.

Individual fact-check organizations that collectively address the landscape include: Reuters Fact Check, AP Fact Check, PolitiFact, Health Feedback, Full Fact, and Africa Check, all of which maintained active COVID-19 myth-tracking resources throughout the pandemic. Their combined output addressed several hundred distinct COVID-19 prevention and treatment false claims across 2020 and 2021.

The WHO's COVID-19 Myth Busters page offered the most systematically maintained authoritative resource for individual claim adjudication, while the Pre-Print Archive COVID Watch database and the CovidMythCheck project tracked emerging claims before they reached mainstream fact-checking infrastructure.

Academic literature addressing the infodemic itself — rather than adjudicating specific claims — includes Zarocostas et al. (2020) in The Lancet, Tangcharoensathien et al. (2020) in the Bulletin of the World Health Organization, and the extensive research output generated by the First Draft COVID-19 Misinformation and Disinformation Tracker project, which documented the global geography of COVID-19 false claims in real time and produced the most comprehensive archive of early-pandemic prevention misinformation available for retrospective analysis.

Snopes's existing COVID-19 coverage, while strong on individual claim adjudication, would benefit from this synthesizing analytical piece that contextualizes individual myths within the broader infodemic structure and explains the mechanisms of infodemic generation and propagation. Absent such synthesis, readers who encounter individual debunks may still lack the metacognitive framework to recognize and resist the next wave of false prevention claims when future outbreaks occur.
