# "The Real Reason Wheat is Toxic"


## 1. The Claim

On November 13, 2014, Sarah Pope published an article on her website The Healthy Home Economist titled "The Real Reason Wheat is Toxic (it's not the gluten)." The piece advanced what would become one of the most persistent and widely shared claims in the online wellness ecosystem: that American wheat is systematically poisoned with Roundup (glyphosate) before harvest, and that this practice — not gluten itself — explains the epidemic of digestive ailments afflicting Americans who eat bread, pasta, and other wheat products.

Pope's central mechanism was straightforward. She claimed that U.S. farmers routinely spray their wheat fields with glyphosate as a desiccant seven to ten days before harvest. This late-season application, she argued, leaves far higher residue levels on the grain than the conventional pre-planting use of the herbicide, because the chemical has no time to degrade before the wheat enters the food supply. The result, according to Pope, is that American wheat products are saturated with a toxic herbicide, causing the surge of gluten sensitivity, celiac-like symptoms, and general digestive distress reported across the United States.

The article's most compelling rhetorical device was the Italian pasta anecdote. Pope observed — and invited her readers to confirm through personal experience — that Americans who suffer digestive symptoms after eating wheat-based products at home report no such problems when eating pasta in Italy. The implication was clear: Italian wheat is clean; American wheat is poisoned. The difference is not the grain; it is the chemical. This comparison had the persuasive force of personal testimony, the romance of European food culture, and the satisfying simplicity of a single villain.

To supply scientific authority for these claims, Pope leaned heavily on the work of Stephanie Seneff, a researcher at MIT's Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory. Pope cited Seneff's 2013 paper in the journal *Entropy*, co-authored with Anthony Samsel, which proposed that glyphosate disrupts cytochrome P450 enzymes and amino acid biosynthesis in the gut microbiome, creating pathways to a broad array of modern diseases including celiac disease, autism, obesity, and cancer. The MIT affiliation provided institutional prestige, and the paper's sweeping scope offered a unified theory linking industrial agriculture to chronic illness.

The article went viral. It was shared millions of times across Facebook, Pinterest, and wellness blogs. It confirmed the suspicions of readers already skeptical of industrial food systems, gave a specific chemical target to diffuse anxieties about modern diet, and offered an actionable conclusion: avoid American wheat, buy organic, eat like the Italians. The claim tapped into a deep vein of appeal-to-nature reasoning that runs through wellness culture — the intuition that traditional foods are safe and industrial modifications are dangerous, that our ancestors' diets were pure and our modern food supply is corrupted.

Pope also cited USDA data to bolster her statistical case. She reported that 99% of durum wheat, 97% of spring wheat, and 61% of winter wheat in the United States is treated with glyphosate, numbers drawn from the USDA's 2012 Agricultural Chemical Use Survey. These figures were presented as evidence that virtually all American wheat is desiccated with Roundup before harvest — a conflation that, as Snopes would later demonstrate, fundamentally misrepresents what the data actually show.


## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes rated Pope's claim as "Mixture," and the rating reflects a genuine layering of truth and falsehood. The fact-check, authored by David Mikkelson and updated by Alex Kasprak, identifies several specific errors and misrepresentations in Pope's article while acknowledging the kernel of truth that gave the claim its initial plausibility.

**The Italian wheat import problem.** Pope's most vivid piece of evidence — that Americans feel fine eating pasta in Italy because Italian wheat is glyphosate-free — collapses under basic trade data. Italy imports up to 40% of its wheat from other countries, including Canada and the United States, where glyphosate is used in wheat production. Italian pasta is not made exclusively from Italian-grown wheat. The anecdote about feeling better eating pasta abroad, while subjectively real for many travelers, cannot be attributed to differences in glyphosate exposure when the wheat supply chains are intermingled. Differences in vacation relaxation, portion size, flour milling practices, fermentation time, and wheat variety are all confounding factors that the glyphosate-only explanation ignores.

**The statistical conflation.** Pope's use of USDA data represents the article's most consequential error. The figures she cited — that 99% of durum wheat, 97% of spring wheat, and 61% of winter wheat are treated with glyphosate — refer to all glyphosate applications across the entire growing season, including standard pre-planting weed control. They do not refer specifically to pre-harvest desiccation, which is what Pope claimed was poisoning the grain. This conflation transforms routine herbicide use (spraying fields before planting, when no crop is present) into alarming evidence of crops being doused with chemicals immediately before harvest. The distinction matters enormously: glyphosate applied months before harvest degrades substantially, while glyphosate applied as a desiccant days before harvest leaves higher residues on the grain.

**The actual scope of pre-harvest desiccation.** Snopes found that pre-harvest glyphosate application as a desiccant is a real practice but a geographically limited one, concentrated primarily in the northern Great Plains states — North Dakota and South Dakota — and parts of the Canadian prairies, where shorter growing seasons and variable weather create harvest management challenges. It covers approximately 5% of U.S. wheat acreage. Furthermore, winter wheat — which accounts for 70–80% of total U.S. wheat production — has the least reliance on glyphosate overall, and pre-harvest desiccation of winter wheat is uncommon because the crop typically dries down adequately on its own in the warmer harvest conditions of the southern and central plains.

**Glyphosate is not a true desiccant.** The Alberta Department of Agriculture provides a useful technical clarification that Snopes references. True desiccants such as Reglone (diquat) are purpose-built harvest management tools that rapidly dry down crop tissue. Pre-harvest glyphosate application is generally used for perennial weed control — killing weeds in the field before harvest to reduce contamination of the grain sample — and while it can be used as a harvest management tool for dry-down, the effects take much longer to appear than with actual desiccants. Conflating glyphosate with desiccation overstates both the prevalence and the purpose of pre-harvest glyphosate use.

**Seneff's credentials and the *Entropy* paper.** The scientific authority Pope invoked was Stephanie Seneff, whose appointment at MIT is in the Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory — not in biology, nutrition, toxicology, or any life science. Seneff holds no credentials in the fields relevant to her claims about glyphosate, gut biology, or disease etiology. Her 2013 paper in *Entropy*, co-authored with Anthony Samsel, has received an editorial Expression of Concern. The paper cites retracted work, contains no original experimental data, and relies on speculative correlational reasoning to link glyphosate exposure to an implausibly wide range of diseases. The *Entropy* paper is a hypothesis-generation exercise that has not been validated by subsequent experimental research, and its speculative conclusions have been widely criticized by toxicologists and agricultural scientists.

**Snopes' conclusion.** The fact-check concludes that glyphosate desiccation of wheat exists but is not common or standard practice across U.S. agriculture, that Pope's statistics fundamentally misrepresent what USDA data show, that the Italian pasta comparison is undermined by international wheat trade, and that the scientific authority cited for the health claims lacks both relevant expertise and empirical support. The "Mixture" rating acknowledges that pre-harvest glyphosate use is real while finding that the scale, mechanism, and health consequences claimed by Pope are unsupported.


## 3. The Epistemic Gap

Snopes' debunking is accurate on every factual point it addresses, but the "Mixture" verdict conceals a richer epistemic landscape that deserves excavation. The gap between what Pope claims and what Snopes debunks leaves several genuinely contested scientific questions unexplored — questions that explain why this particular viral claim proved so durable and why simplistic verdicts, in either direction, miss the real story.

### Glyphosate's genuinely contested safety profile

The most important thing Snopes gestures toward but does not fully unpack is that glyphosate's toxicological status is the subject of genuine, high-stakes scientific disagreement among authoritative institutions. In 2015, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), a specialized agency of the World Health Organization, classified glyphosate as "probably carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2A) based on its evaluation of available evidence from animal studies, mechanistic data, and limited epidemiological evidence — particularly associations with non-Hodgkin lymphoma in agricultural workers. This classification placed glyphosate in the same risk category as red meat and shift work.

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), by contrast, concluded that glyphosate is unlikely to pose a carcinogenic risk to humans at typical exposure levels. These agencies gave greater weight to regulatory toxicology studies and industry-sponsored long-term animal feeding studies that IARC excluded or weighed differently. Portier et al. (2016, *Journal of Epidemiology and Community Health*) provided a detailed technical analysis of why IARC and EFSA reached divergent conclusions from substantially overlapping evidence bases, identifying differences in study selection criteria, statistical methodology, and the handling of industry-funded research as key factors. This is not fringe science versus consensus science — it is a genuine methodological disagreement among credentialed experts at major institutions, and it remains unresolved.

Pope's article exploits this genuine scientific ambiguity by inflating it into certainty that glyphosate causes disease. Snopes deflates the ambiguity by correctly noting that "most government agencies" conclude glyphosate is not a risk at typical exposure levels. But the full picture is that the scientific community is genuinely divided on the question of glyphosate carcinogenicity at occupational exposure levels, and this division creates the epistemic space in which Pope's claims take root.

### The actual trajectory of glyphosate use

Benbrook (2016, *Environmental Sciences Europe*) documented trends in glyphosate herbicide use in the United States and globally that paint a picture more alarming than the one Snopes conveys. Global glyphosate use increased approximately fifteen-fold between 1996 and 2014, driven primarily by the adoption of glyphosate-tolerant genetically modified crops. In the United States alone, agricultural glyphosate use rose from approximately 27 million kilograms in 1996 to approximately 125 million kilograms in 2014. This massive expansion of use is a documented fact, not a conspiracy theory, and it legitimately raises questions about cumulative environmental and human exposure even if Pope's specific mechanism is wrong.

The increase matters because regulatory safety assessments are based on exposure levels, and exposure levels are a function of use volume, application practices, and dietary intake patterns. If glyphosate use has increased dramatically while regulatory exposure assumptions have not been updated proportionally, there is a legitimate regulatory lag question — one separate from Pope's specific claims about desiccation.

### What residue data actually show

Studies measuring glyphosate residues in grain-based food products provide a more nuanced picture than either Pope or Snopes offers. Andreassen et al. (2015, *Food Chemistry*) analyzed glyphosate and AMPA (aminomethylphosphonic acid, a primary metabolite) residues in Norwegian cereals and found detectable residues in a substantial proportion of grain samples, though the vast majority were below established maximum residue limits (MRLs). Similar monitoring studies across Europe and North America have consistently found that while glyphosate residues are detectable in wheat-based foods, they typically fall within regulatory safety thresholds.

This finding simultaneously validates Pope's underlying premise — that glyphosate residues are present in wheat products and that pre-harvest application contributes to higher residue levels — while undermining her conclusion, because detectable does not equal dangerous, and regulatory limits exist precisely to define the boundary between safe and unsafe exposure. The residue data refute the "no glyphosate in your food" reassurance while also refuting the "your wheat is poisoned" alarm. The truth is metrological: residues are present, generally below safety thresholds, and the adequacy of those thresholds is the subject of ongoing regulatory and scientific debate.

### The appeal-to-nature fallacy structure

Pope's argument follows a recognizable logical architecture shared with anti-GMO reasoning, anti-vaccine narratives, and organic food advocacy: (1) identify a synthetic or industrial chemical involved in food production; (2) document that it exists in the food supply at detectable levels; (3) cite a credentialed-sounding authority who claims it causes disease; (4) contrast the modern contaminated food with a traditional, natural, or foreign alternative represented as pure; (5) conclude that the chemical causes the disease and that avoiding it will restore health.

Each step contains either a factual claim with genuine evidentiary support (glyphosate is used; residues are detectable) or a logical inference that does not follow from the evidence (detectable residues cause disease; avoiding them restores health). The appeal-to-nature structure makes the argument resistant to debunking because each individual factual claim can be defended while the overall causal inference collapses. This is why the viral claim persists despite Snopes' accurate fact-check: readers remember the true parts and discount the corrections to the false inferences.

### The celiac disease prevalence question

One of the most effective aspects of Pope's argument is its implicit claim that something has changed — that Americans are getting sicker from wheat, and glyphosate explains why. This premise rests on an empirical question: are celiac disease and non-celiac gluten sensitivity actually increasing in prevalence?

Rubio-Tapia et al. (2009, *Gastroenterology*) provided the most rigorous evidence for increasing celiac disease prevalence. By testing archived blood samples from Air Force recruits collected in the 1950s against contemporary samples using the same serological assays, the study demonstrated that celiac disease prevalence in the United States had increased approximately four-fold over fifty years — from roughly 1 in 500 to roughly 1 in 100. This increase cannot be attributed solely to improved diagnostic awareness because the same assays were applied retrospectively to historical samples.

However, the temporal correlation between rising celiac prevalence and rising glyphosate use does not establish causation. Multiple environmental factors have changed over the same period: wheat breeding has shifted toward higher-gluten varieties, dietary wheat consumption patterns have evolved, infant feeding practices have changed, antibiotic use has expanded, the human microbiome has shifted, and the overall environmental chemical burden has increased. Glyphosate is one candidate among many, and correlational data cannot distinguish among these possibilities. Seneff's work relies almost entirely on temporal correlations of this type, which is precisely why it fails to meet the evidentiary standards required to establish causation.

The genuinely increasing celiac prevalence is a real scientific finding that demands explanation. Pope's error is not in asking the question but in prematurely accepting an answer that has not been empirically validated, then dressing it in borrowed authority and statistical misrepresentation to make it seem conclusive.


## 4. Key Evidence

**Portier, Christopher J. et al. (2016, *Journal of Epidemiology and Community Health*).** Systematic comparison of the methodological differences between IARC's "probably carcinogenic" and EFSA's "unlikely to pose carcinogenic risk" assessments of glyphosate. Identified study selection, statistical handling, and treatment of industry-funded research as key sources of divergence. Demonstrates that the disagreement among authoritative agencies reflects genuine methodological disputes rather than one side simply being wrong.

**Benbrook, Charles M. (2016, *Environmental Sciences Europe*).** Comprehensive analysis of trends in glyphosate herbicide use in the United States and globally from 1974 to 2014. Documents the approximately fifteen-fold increase in global use driven by glyphosate-tolerant crop adoption. Provides the empirical basis for understanding regulatory exposure assumptions in the context of radically changed agricultural practices.

**Samsel, Anthony, and Seneff, Stephanie (2013, *Entropy*).** The paper Pope cites as her primary scientific authority. Proposes that glyphosate suppresses cytochrome P450 enzymes and disrupts amino acid biosynthesis in the gut microbiome, creating pathways to celiac disease, autism, obesity, and multiple other conditions. Contains no original experimental data; relies on speculative correlational reasoning. Has received an editorial Expression of Concern and cites retracted work. Widely criticized by toxicologists for overgeneralized claims lacking empirical support.

**Rubio-Tapia, Alberto et al. (2009, *Gastroenterology*).** Retrospective serological study comparing celiac disease prevalence in archived 1950s Air Force recruit blood samples with contemporary samples. Demonstrated a four-fold increase in celiac disease prevalence over fifty years. Important because it establishes that rising celiac prevalence is real and not solely an artifact of improved diagnosis, while the cause remains undetermined.

**USDA (2013). "2012 Agricultural Chemical Use Survey, Wheat."** The source of the statistics Pope misrepresented. Documents total glyphosate application rates across U.S. wheat acreage by wheat class (durum, spring, winter), covering all application timings. Pope's error was presenting total application figures as pre-harvest desiccation figures.

**Andreassen, Monica et al. (2015, *Food Chemistry*).** Analysis of glyphosate and AMPA residues in Norwegian cereal samples. Found detectable residues in a substantial proportion of samples, generally below maximum residue limits. Representative of the broader European and North American residue monitoring literature showing that glyphosate residues are present in wheat-based foods but typically within regulatory safety thresholds.


## 5. What This Means

The "wheat is toxic" viral article exemplifies a specific failure mode in online health information that is more dangerous than simple misinformation: the laundering of contested science through non-expert authority into actionable wellness content. Understanding how this laundering works is essential to understanding why these claims resist debunking and why they recur in slightly different forms across the wellness information ecosystem.

**The credentialing problem.** Seneff's MIT affiliation is the load-bearing element of Pope's scientific authority. In the attention economy of viral content, "MIT researcher" functions as a proxy for expertise that most readers cannot independently evaluate. The crucial distinction — that Seneff's appointment is in computer science, not in biology, toxicology, nutrition, or any field relevant to her claims — requires domain knowledge that wellness content consumers typically lack. This is a systematic vulnerability in public science communication: institutional affiliation signals competence, but academic institutions house researchers across hundreds of unrelated specialty areas, and the assumption that an MIT computer scientist has authority on gut biology is a credentialing fallacy that content creators exploit, deliberately or not.

**The laundering of legitimate concern.** What makes the "wheat is toxic" claim particularly instructive is that it rests on a foundation of genuine scientific uncertainty. Glyphosate's safety truly is contested among authoritative agencies. Celiac disease prevalence truly has increased. Glyphosate residues truly are detectable in wheat products. Pre-harvest desiccation truly does occur. Each of these facts is real. The laundering process takes these legitimate data points, removes the uncertainty and methodological context, fills the inferential gaps with speculative claims from a non-expert source, and packages the result as a definitive explanation accessible to general audiences. The resulting content is more persuasive than pure fabrication precisely because it contains verifiable truths.

**The mixture problem.** Snopes' "Mixture" rating is technically correct and communicatively challenging. In the binary attention economy of social media sharing, a "Mixture" verdict can read as partial vindication. Readers sympathetic to the original claim can emphasize the "true" components (glyphosate desiccation exists, residues are real) while dismissing the "false" components (scale, causation, scientific authority) as quibbles. This is not a failure of Snopes' methodology — the claim genuinely contains a mixture of true and false components — but it illustrates why claims built on legitimate kernels of truth are far harder to correct than pure fabrication. The factual core provides continuous reentry points for the debunked narrative.

**The regulatory trust gap.** Pope's claim gains traction in a context where public trust in regulatory agencies has been damaged by documented failures — the slow recognition of tobacco harms, the Vioxx withdrawal, the opioid crisis, and ongoing questions about regulatory capture in agricultural chemical approval processes. When regulatory agencies say glyphosate is safe and IARC says it is probably carcinogenic, people who have reason to distrust regulators have reason to credit the more alarming assessment. The "wheat is toxic" narrative does not require its audience to be scientifically illiterate; it requires only that they have experienced or observed regulatory failure, which most adults have.

**The wellness industry's structural incentive.** The Healthy Home Economist and similar wellness platforms operate on an advertising and affiliate revenue model where engagement is driven by alarming content that positions the platform as a source of suppressed truth. Pope's article generates value precisely because it contradicts official reassurances, offers a simple causal explanation for widespread suffering, and directs readers toward premium products (organic wheat, heritage grains, gluten-free alternatives) that advertisers on wellness platforms sell. This creates a structural incentive to inflate genuine scientific uncertainty into definitive health threats, because moderate, evidence-proportionate conclusions ("glyphosate's safety is actively debated; residues are present but generally within safety limits; more research is needed") do not generate the engagement that pays for the content.


## 6. Verdict and Classification

**Verdict: Mixture.** Snopes' rating is correct. The claim contains verifiable truths (glyphosate is used as a pre-harvest desiccant on some U.S. wheat; higher residues result from late-season application; detectable glyphosate residues are present in wheat products) embedded in a framework of statistical misrepresentation (conflating total glyphosate use with pre-harvest desiccation), false authority (citing a computer scientist as an expert on gut biology and toxicology), flawed comparative evidence (the Italian pasta anecdote), and unsupported causal claims (that glyphosate residues at typical dietary exposure levels cause celiac disease and digestive ailments).

**EARC Mode: C (Contested).** The claim intersects with a genuinely contested scientific domain — glyphosate toxicology and its regulatory evaluation — but extends the legitimate uncertainty into unsupported certainty about specific disease causation. The IARC/EFSA disagreement on glyphosate carcinogenicity represents authentic scientific contestation. Pope's article weaponizes this contestation by collapsing the distinction between "glyphosate's long-term effects are debated" and "glyphosate in your wheat is making you sick." Mode C is appropriate because the underlying science is neither settled in Pope's favor nor settled against her concerns — it is genuinely contested, and the epistemically responsible position acknowledges the contestation without resolving it through credentialing fallacy or statistical misrepresentation.

**Gap Category: Credentialing Fallacy / Science Denial.** The primary epistemic failure is credentialing fallacy: the substitution of institutional affiliation (MIT) for domain expertise (toxicology, gastroenterology, agricultural science). Seneff's paper is not merely wrong in its conclusions; it represents a methodological approach — correlational speculation without experimental validation — that would not survive peer review in the fields where its claims apply. The secondary failure is science denial in the specific sense of denying the relevance of regulatory science (USDA chemical use data, EPA and EFSA safety assessments) when it contradicts the preferred narrative, while selectively citing the same institutions (USDA statistics, IARC classification) when their data can be misrepresented to support the claim.

The "wheat is toxic" narrative persists because it occupies the gap between legitimate scientific uncertainty about glyphosate and the public's need for simple causal explanations for complex health trends. Closing that gap requires not just debunking individual false claims but building public capacity to sit with scientific uncertainty — to understand that "we don't fully know yet" is a more accurate and more difficult answer than either "it's poison" or "it's perfectly safe."
