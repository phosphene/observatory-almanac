## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

A widely circulated humorous story describes a factory worker who pushes a wheelbarrow full of straw out through a security checkpoint every single workday for twenty years. Each day, the diligent guard searches the straw and finds nothing suspicious. On the day of the worker's retirement, when he arrives without the wheelbarrow, the guard finally asks what he had been stealing all those years. The punchline: wheelbarrows.

Snopes investigator Barbara Mikkelson rated this story **Legend**—a piece of traditional oral folk narrative rather than a documented historical event. The wheelbarrow story belongs to a large family of tales built around the principle of "hiding in plain sight," and its lineage can be traced directly to the ancient Turkish and Persian trickster tradition featuring the figure of Nasreddin (also rendered as Nasradin, the Hodja, Juha, or a dozen other regional names). The oldest printed form of the modern version that Mikkelson could identify is from Bennett Cerf's 1952 joke book *Good for a Laugh*, where the setting is the Perón construction project in Argentina. Similar stories involving bicycles, trucks, donkeys, and park benches appear across multiple cultural contexts, confirming the narrative's status as a migratory legend morphotype.

This entry examines the textual history of the wheelbarrow story, its connection to the Nasreddin trickster tradition, the cognitive principle of "hiding in plain sight" that gives the story its enduring appeal, the social psychology of guard/checkpoint relationships that the narrative exploits, the narrative's appearances in film and children's literature, and the broader significance of the "purloined object" story structure in folklore and popular culture.

## §2 Textual History: From Nasreddin to the Perón Project

The wheelbarrow legend has a traceable textual genealogy that runs from the ancient Middle East to twentieth-century joke books and then to internet-era forwarded emails. Understanding this genealogy requires distinguishing between the deep structural archetype (a person repeatedly passes a checkpoint with a distractor object while the real object of theft is the container itself or the means of transport) and the specific surface-level instantiations that adapt this structure to contemporary settings.

Barbara Mikkelson (2001) identified the story as originating in the Turkish and Persian Nasreddin tradition. Nasreddin (also spelled Nasreddin Hodja, Nasrudin, or a dozen regional variants) is one of the most widely distributed trickster figures in world folklore, appearing in narratives from Central Asia, the Middle East, North Africa, and the Balkans. His stories number in the hundreds and follow a consistent pattern: Nasreddin uses apparent simplicity, paradox, or misdirection to expose the foolishness of those who think themselves cleverer than him. The "hiding in plain sight" structure is one of the recurring motifs in Nasreddin's corpus, applied to various objects and contexts across different regional traditions.

The oldest identifiable modern print version that Mikkelson found places the story at the Perón construction project in Argentina—the luxury retreat that Juan and Evita Perón were building outside Buenos Aires. In Bennett Cerf's 1952 joke book *Good for a Laugh* (Hanover House, New York, 1952, p. 64), the guard diligently searches the wheelbarrow's straw daily, even having it chemically analyzed, without finding anything suspicious. A year after the project concludes, the guard encounters the worker "evidently enjoying great prosperity" and asks what was actually being stolen. The answer: wheelbarrows. Harry Hershfield's 1959 joke compilation *Laugh Louder Live Longer* (Gramercy Publishing Company, New York, 1959, p. 166) contains another version.

The Reader's Digest variant (Hall, Jeffrey, "Humor in Uniform," *Reader's Digest*, August 1971, p. 98) updates the object to a 2½-ton Army truck, with an MP corporal giving a new recruit a lesson in vehicle searching—checking the back, under the canopy, the tire well, under the hood, under the seat—and waving the vehicle through without realizing the truck itself was being stolen. The *Reader's Digest* version demonstrates the story's adaptability to military and institutional contexts, and its appearance in that widely distributed popular magazine ensured broad circulation in the English-speaking world.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Cognitive Science of the Purloined Object and Search Heuristic Exploitation

The epistemic novelty of this entry lies in an analysis of the specific cognitive mechanisms that the wheelbarrow story exploits—the psychological architecture that makes the guard's failure both plausible and funny, and why the narrative structure maps onto genuinely documented patterns of human search behavior and attentional failure.

At its core, the wheelbarrow trick exploits what cognitive scientists call "inattentional blindness" combined with "schema-driven search." The guard has a cognitive model of what a theft looks like: a worker carrying something small and concealed, perhaps in a pocket or under clothing, or hidden within a container. The guard's search heuristic is therefore organized around finding hidden items within the straw. The wheelbarrow itself—a large, heavy, highly visible metal object—is invisible to this search precisely because it is so conspicuous. It falls outside the mental model of "what could be stolen," and therefore attention does not alight on it.

Research on inattentional blindness—the failure to notice salient stimuli when attention is directed elsewhere—has been extensively documented since Arien Mack and Irvin Rock's foundational work (Mack, A., & Rock, I., 1998, *Inattentional Blindness*, MIT Press, Cambridge, Massachusetts). The famous "gorilla experiment" by Simons and Chabris (1999, "Gorillas in Our Midst: Sustained Inattentional Blindness for Dynamic Events," *Perception*, Vol. 28, No. 9, pp. 1059–1074) demonstrated that observers counting basketball passes failed to notice a person in a gorilla costume walking through the scene. The wheelbarrow operates identically: the guard searching for concealed small items in straw fails to notice the container because that object falls outside the category of "things to search for."

A second cognitive mechanism at work is the familiarity-driven reduction in vigilance for recurring events. The guard sees the worker with the wheelbarrow every day for twenty years. This high-frequency exposure creates a routinized perception—the worker-with-wheelbarrow becomes a background feature of the checkpoint environment rather than an object of active scrutiny. Psychological research on habituation in vigilance tasks has consistently shown that sustained inspection tasks become less effective over time as the signal frequency and predictability increase, and that inspectors tend to focus cognitive attention on deviations from expectation rather than on the expected pattern itself (Warm, J. S., Parasuraman, R., & Matthews, G., 2008, "Vigilance Requires Hard Mental Work and Is Stressful," *Human Factors*, Vol. 50, No. 3, pp. 433–441).

The combination of these effects—schema-driven search (looking in the wrong place) and habituation-driven inattention (the expected is invisible)—creates exactly the vulnerability the wheelbarrow story dramatizes. The story's humor depends on the audience recognizing, in the punchline, both the brilliant simplicity of the theft and their own retrospective failure to have noticed the possibility while reading the setup. This recognitive pleasure—the aha moment of seeing what was invisible—is the cognitive signature of the purloined object narrative, and explains its durability across cultures and centuries.

The variant involving a park bench and a receipt is particularly elegant from this standpoint. The two men who purchase a park bench with a receipt initially attract police attention because carrying a park bench is anomalous. After repeatedly demonstrating ownership via receipt, their bench-carrying becomes familiar—normalized—and they then steal further benches unimpeded by police who have learned to classify them as authorized bench-carriers. This version adds the dimension of social learning and category formation: the police form an explicit cognitive model ("these men carry benches legitimately") that then blinds them to bench-theft by those same men.

## §4 Variants, Diffusion, and the Cross-Cultural Spread of the Purloined-Object Structure

The wheelbarrow legend's geographic and thematic variants document both its deep structural stability and its surface adaptability. Mikkelson (2001) identified settings in Brazil, Mexico, South Africa, and the Soviet Union, in addition to the fictional Argentina Perón framing. The Soviet Union variant has a particular ideological resonance: set in a factory or collective farm where the worker's legitimate product was appropriated by the state, the theft-by-wheelbarrow became a covert narrative of worker resistance—the laborer outsmarts the surveillance apparatus designed to extract value from ordinary people. The ideological valence of the same story can thus shift dramatically depending on who owns the factory being pilfered from.

The border guard variants (bicycle with sack of sand, donkey with sack of rice) operate identically but with different social framing. The border guard is a state authority enforcing national boundaries; the trickster is often implicitly a smuggler or trader moving goods across a heavily controlled frontier. These variants directly invoke the Nasreddin tradition, in which the protagonist's cleverness is typically directed against figures of institutional authority—judges, teachers, nobles, tax collectors. The donkey being smuggled while the guards search the rice sacks has the added dimension that the animal itself is clearly visible—one cannot hide a donkey in a grain sack—but the guards' attention is on the declared goods, not on the transport mechanism.

Henry D. Spalding's *Encyclopedia of Jewish Humor* (Jonathan David Publishers, New York, 1969, pp. 69–70), cited by Mikkelson, contains a version that places the story in a Jewish cultural context, where it joins a long tradition of cleverness-under-oppression narratives in Ashkenazi folklore. Phil Healey and Rick Glanvill's *Now! That's What I Call Urban Myths* (Virgin Books, London, 1996) represents the story's assimilation into the contemporary urban legend genre, where it circulates alongside modern tales of technological or institutional cleverness rather than ancient folk trickster narratives.

The truck variant in *Reader's Digest* (Hall, 1971)—an Army sergeant being trained to search vehicles who waves through a truck being stolen—is also notable for its institutional setting. Unlike the factory or construction site, the Army context adds a layer of bureaucratic irony: the very training exercise designed to prevent theft serves as cover for its execution. The trainer's confidence ("satisfied that the truck was 'clean,' the corporal waved the through, obviously proud of the lesson he had just given me") becomes the mechanism of the theft's success.

## §5 Sightings in Film and Children's Literature

Mikkelson documented two specific cultural sightings of the wheelbarrow legend in identifiable narrative texts, confirming its absorption into the mainstream popular culture of the early 2000s.

The 2001 film *Crocodile Dundee in Los Angeles* (directed by Simon Wincer) included a scene or plot element invoking the wheelbarrow legend, demonstrating the story's utility as a practical joke or clever deception scenario in Hollywood narrative. The Crocodile Dundee franchise represented a genre of fish-out-of-water comedy in which the protagonist's outsider cleverness repeatedly outmaneuvers sophisticated urban institutions; the wheelbarrow story fits naturally into this narrative mode.

Benjamin Elkin's 1962 children's book *Gillespie and the Guards* (illustrated by James Daugherty, Viking Press) adapts the hiding-in-plain-sight principle for a young audience. In Elkin's version, three guards are challenged never to be tricked, and a small boy Gillespie repeatedly passes through the checkpoint carrying objects that the guards fixate on while missing the actual trick. The book won the New York Herald Tribune Children's Spring Book Festival Award in 1963 and was well-reviewed at the time, suggesting that the cognitive principle the story teaches—attention can be misdirected by the conspicuous against the subtle—was considered valuable moral and intellectual instruction for children. That this cognitive lesson encoded in folkloric form appeared in a celebrated children's book indicates that the story was understood even in the 1960s to have didactic as well as entertainment value.

## §6 Broader Significance: The Purloined Object Archetype and the Principle of Visible Concealment

The wheelbarrow legend belongs to one of the most structurally coherent and cross-culturally stable story archetypes in world folklore. Its stability across Nasreddin narratives, Ashkenazi humor, contemporary urban legends, children's literature, and Hollywood film reflects something genuine and universal about the cognitive principle it dramatizes: the most effective form of concealment is not hiding but normalization. This is sometimes called "concealment by salience" or "the purloined letter principle" after Edgar Allan Poe's 1844 story "The Purloined Letter," in which the stolen document is hidden by being left completely in plain view among other documents on a writing table, where investigators searching for hidden objects repeatedly fail to find it (Poe, Edgar Allan, 1844, "The Purloined Letter," in *The Gift: A Christmas and New Year's Present*, Carey and Hart, Philadelphia).

The Poe formulation makes the cognitive principle explicit through the fictional detective Dupin, who explains that the Parisian police's failure stems from their assumption that clever concealment must be elaborate—false bottoms, secret compartments—when in fact the simplest evasion of a schema-driven search is to place the object where the schema does not look: in plain sight. The wheelbarrow story is essentially a folk version of the same argument, made through a relatable workplace scenario rather than a Gothic detective narrative.

The EARC gap designation of **C** (Contextual) reflects the need for a more complete comparative study of the wheelbarrow narrative's specific textual lineage from medieval Nasreddin manuscripts through early modern print culture, into twentieth-century joke books, and finally into internet transmission. While Mikkelson's identification of the 1952 Cerf text as the earliest modern English print version is a useful finding, a full comparative folklore analysis—working with original Turkish, Persian, and Arabic manuscript sources—would likely find earlier print instances and could more precisely chart the story's geographic diffusion. Spalding (1969) and Healey/Glanvill (1996) provide anchor points but not a complete philological history.
