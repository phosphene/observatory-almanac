# Are Secret Negotiations Underway To Increase the Power of the WHO?


## 1. The Claim

In mid-January 2023, a coordinated wave of conspiratorial content claimed that the World Health Organization was conducting secret negotiations to dramatically expand its legal authority and sovereignty over member states. The core allegation, packaged as copypasta text that spread across substack blogs, alternative media outlets, and social media accounts, stated:

> *"The International Health Regulations Review Committee (IHRRC) of the World Health Organization (WHO) is planning to meet in secret from Monday, January 9, 2023 to Friday January 13, 2023. The IHRRC will be working to finalize what is now a 46 page document that includes proposed amendments to the International Health Regulations (IHR)."*

The copypasta text appended a list of ten alleged implications of the amendments, including:
- That the amendments would "change the overall nature of the World Health Organization from an advisory organization that merely makes recommendations to a governing body whose proclamations would be legally-binding";
- That the amendments would remove "respect for dignity, human rights and fundamental freedoms of people";
- That the amendments would "greatly expand the scope of the International Health Regulations to include scenarios that merely have a 'potential to impact public health.'"

The hashtag #stoptheamendments accompanied many of the posts. The claim was amplified primarily by conspiracy-oriented substack newsletters, GlobalResearch.ca, and associated anti-WHO, anti-globalist social media networks with established history of COVID-19 misinformation dissemination.

Snopes investigative journalist Alex Kasprak fact-checked the claim as the meeting was underway, publishing his assessment on January 13, 2023, and rating it "False."


## 2. What's Actually True

Several elements within the broader conspiracy theory do contain factual kernels — which is why the claim is neither a pure invention nor a reasonable assessment of the meeting's significance.

**The IHRRC meeting was real.** The International Health Regulations Review Committee did hold its sixth and final meeting from January 9 to 13, 2023. This is accurate.

**The IHR is a legally binding international instrument.** The International Health Regulations have been legally binding on 196 countries, including all 194 WHO member states, since their substantive revision in 2005 (WHO, *International Health Regulations, Third Edition*, 2016). This status predates any 2023 amendments and is not a new development. The IHR grew from earlier International Sanitary Regulations created in response to the nineteenth-century European cholera epidemics, representing over a century of evolving international health law.

**Amendments to the IHR were being discussed.** Yes. The IHRRC's mandate was to review proposed amendments submitted by member states following the COVID-19 pandemic experience, which had revealed inadequacies in the existing IHR framework. Over 300 amendments to 33 of the IHR's 66 articles and five of its nine annexes were submitted by member states for consideration, along with proposals for six new articles and one new annex. The amendment process is explicitly provided for in the IHR itself.

**The word "non-binding" was removed from some definitions.** Some proposed amendments did remove the word "non-binding" from two definitional clauses in the IHR. This is accurate.

**Proposed amendments would potentially enhance vaccine production capacity.** Some amendments discussed authorising entry requirements based on health status (such as negative COVID-19 tests) and enhancing member states' capacity to produce and stockpile vaccine ingredients. These are accurate characterisations of amendment proposals.


## 3. The Epistemic Gap

The claim is False, but the "False" verdict operates at a specific level: the meeting was not secret, and the claim that WHO would be transformed from advisory to legally binding was inaccurate (the IHR was already legally binding). The deeper epistemic structure of the conspiracy theory's operational method is the more important subject.

**The "secret" allegation is directly falsifiable by publicly available evidence.** The most straightforward falsification: WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus issued a public opening statement on January 12, 2023, announcing its proceedings, explicitly describing it as "the 6th and final meeting of the Review Committee on amendments to the IHR, which I convened last October at the request of Member States." WHO issued news releases about the meeting at its launch. The meeting followed a publicly reported May 2022 WHO World Health Assembly that established the working group's mandate and timeline, covered by mainstream international press including Politico's European edition. The proposed amendment document — all 46 pages of it — was publicly accessible on WHO's website (apps.who.int/gb/wgihr), a direct irony given that the conspiracy theorists based their analysis entirely on reading this public document.

The allegation of secrecy represents what might be called a "secrecy inversion" — a rhetorical technique in which a publicly accessible, officially announced, press-covered bureaucratic process is reframed as clandestine purely by the assertion of the word "secret." The claim does not withstand a single internet search for any element of the meeting's actual public footprint.

**The "advisory-to-governing" argument rests on a fundamental confusion about the IHR's existing legal status.** The copypasta text's claim that the amendments would change WHO from advisory to legally binding depends entirely on the removal of the word "non-binding" from two definitional clauses. But this ignores the pre-existing legal structure: the IHR as a whole — not just those two clauses — was already legally binding under international law as a treaty instrument adopted by member states. The binding character of the IHR derives from the treaty framework in which it sits, not from specific words within definitional clauses. Removing "non-binding" from a sub-clause does not change the legal character of the instrument; it removes a potentially confusing inconsistency between a sub-clause and the broader legal framework.

Characterising this textual change as the transformation of WHO from advisory to governing requires either a fundamental misunderstanding of how international treaty law works, or a deliberate misrepresentation of it. The conspiracy theorists did not discover that the IHR was being made legally binding for the first time; they found a minor textual change and misrepresented its legal significance.

**The human rights removal allegation is a selective omission.** The copypasta text accurately reported that proposed amendments removed the phrase "respect for dignity, human rights and fundamental freedoms of people" from Article 3. What the text did not report was what replaced that language: an expanded statement that addressed equity, transparency, trust, sovereignty, collaboration, and assistance in the context of protecting public health — a more detailed enumeration of the human rights-related principles rather than their removal. WHO Director-General Tedros's public opening statement at the same meeting explicitly listed "equity, transparency, trust, sovereignty, collaboration, and assistance" as "cross-cutting themes" in the amendment process.

This omission-through-selective-quotation is a foundational technique in bad-faith textual analysis: extract the deleted language, present it as evidence of what was removed, suppress all mention of the replacement language, and assert removal of rights. Applied systematically across a 46-page public document, this technique can generate the appearance of comprehensive evidence for a predetermined conclusion.

**The copypasta mechanism reveals coordinated dissemination, not independent discovery.** The textual consistency of the conspiratorial claims — the same phrasing, the same list of ten allegations, the same selective quotations appearing across dozens of independent-seeming outlets and social media accounts — is the hallmark of coordinated information operation rather than independent investigative journalism. Genuine independent investigators encountering the same document would produce differently framed, differently emphasised analyses. The copypasta structure reveals that the analysis was produced centrally and distributed for amplification, which is itself relevant evidence about the epistemic standing of the claims. The primary originating text has been traced to a substack newsletter by James Roguski, an anti-WHO activist who has made a career of producing alarmist content about WHO governance processes.

**WHO sovereignty concerns are not entirely without basis — but these amendments were not the threat claimed.** There is a legitimate scholarly and policy debate about the appropriate scope of international health governance, the balance between WHO coordination authority and national sovereignty, and the potential for international health frameworks to be used in ways that marginalise the interests of lower-income countries or impose burden-distributing requirements. Scholars in global health governance including Gorik Ooms and Rachel Hammonds have written thoughtfully about equity and sovereignty questions in the IHR framework (Ooms and Hammonds, 2019, *BMJ Global Health*). The existence of these genuine policy debates does not validate the specific claims made about the January 2023 meeting, which were demonstrably inaccurate. The conspiracy theory parasitised legitimate policy concerns to lend them pseudo-credibility, while the actual amendment discussions were substantially less alarming than characterised.

**The role of COVID-19 distrust in generating this claim's audience.** The January 2023 claims emerged within a well-established information ecosystem built around anti-COVID-19-measure sentiment that had been developing since early 2020. This ecosystem had pre-existing audiences primed to believe that WHO, global health institutions, and pharmaceutical companies were engaged in coordinated deception. The WHO amendment story arrived in an informationally prepared cognitive environment where the prior probability of believing it was elevated by three years of consistent messaging built around comparable claims. Understanding how misinformation ecosystems function requires recognising that individual false claims do not operate in isolation — they arrive with prior probability boosts from preceding claims, even when earlier predictions failed or were never tested (Lewandowsky et al., 2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, vol. 13, no. 3, pp. 106–131, on the persistence of misinformation after correction).


## 4. Who Spread It and Why

**Anti-WHO networked disinformation.** The initial amplification came from James Roguski's Substack — a newsletter explicitly dedicated to opposing WHO governance expansion — and from GlobalResearch.ca, a Canadian website with a documented history of publishing health, geopolitical, and climate misinformation. These outlets functioned as production and initial distribution nodes for the copypasta text that subsequently spread through Twitter/X, Facebook, Telegram, and alternative media aggregators.

**Established conspiracy theory infrastructure.** The WHO amendment story was not a new conspiracy theory developed from scratch; it was an application of an established template — "globalist institutions secretly meeting to seize power over sovereign nations" — to the specific target of WHO's routine administrative processes. This template has been applied to the United Nations, the World Economic Forum, the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, and other international institutions. The template's familiarity to its audience made the WHO amendment version immediately legible and shareable without requiring detailed engagement with the actual documentary evidence.

**The #stoptheamendments hashtag as coordination signal.** The consistent use of the hashtag across the dissemination wave served both as a discoverability mechanism and as a call to action. Framing the amendments as stoppable through public pressure created participatory urgency — the audience could feel they were engaged in active resistance to the alleged power grab. This participatory element generates stronger psychological investment and more active resharing than passive consumption of information.

**COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and WHO distrust crossover.** Audiences who had developed distrust of WHO's COVID-19 pandemic guidance — based on early messaging about masking, its delayed declaration of public health emergency status in January 2020, and its initial resistance to recognising airborne transmission — had pre-formed reasons to be receptive to claims of WHO institutional untrustworthiness. This crossover audience between vaccine hesitancy and WHO scepticism was the primary amplification network.


## 5. What Matters for Evidence Literacy

The WHO IHR amendments case is an unusually clean example of a specific disinformation technique: mischaracterising a public process as secret, and mischaracterising a change within an existing framework as a novel seizure of power.

**Publicly documented processes cannot be secret.** The single most efficient falsification of any "secret meeting" claim is checking whether there is a public record of the meeting. The WHO Director-General's public opening statement, issued mid-meeting and publicly posted on who.int, is definitive evidence that the meeting was not secret. Any "secret meeting" allegation that can be falsified by a Google search for WHO press releases fails at the most basic level.

**Legal reasoning requires understanding the baseline.** The copypasta text's central claim about WHO becoming "legally binding" for the first time was falsified not by a new empirical finding but by consulting the existing legal framework. The IHR had been legally binding since 2005. Assessing whether an amendment changes the legal character of an instrument requires knowing what that character was before the amendment — a step the conspiracy theory systematically omitted.

**Selective quotation is falsified by reading the full text.** The human rights removal claim is falsified by reading the full proposed amendment text — which the conspiracy theorists themselves provided as a reference. When the evidence required to falsify a claim is contained within the same document the claimants are citing, the misrepresentation is particularly indefensible.

**Copypasta structure is itself evidence of manufacturing.** When ostensibly independent sources produce verbatim-identical analysis, the independence claim is false. Users encountering the identical ten-point list across multiple outlets should recognise the coordinated production structure and apply appropriate scepticism.


## 6. Verdict and Confidence

**Snopes rating: False.** The meeting was publicly announced, publicly conducted, and publicly reported. The IHR was already legally binding before any proposed amendments. The human rights language removal was a selective quotation that omitted the replacement language. The amendments as discussed did not substantively change WHO's authority or the legally binding character of the IHR.

**EARC assessment:** The primary gap is in *Reasoning* — the inferential moves from "this word was removed" to "WHO is seizing power" and from "this meeting occurred" to "this meeting was secret" are not valid and require multiple suppressed premises or factual errors. Secondary gaps exist in *Context* — understanding why the IHR was amended (COVID-19 revealed gaps), what the prior legal status was (already binding), and what the actual proposed changes entailed (equity, transparency, vaccine production capacity).

**Gap category: false-via-mischaracterisation.** The basic facts (a meeting occurred, amendments were discussed, some text was changed) are true. The characterisation of those facts (secret, power-grabbing, rights-removing) is systematically false.

**Practical upshot:** International bureaucratic amendment processes are neither exciting nor threatening in themselves. The WHO IHR amendment process is part of the ordinary maintenance and updating of international health law — a process that has occurred multiple times since the IHR's creation and will occur again. The claim was false, and its falseness was readily demonstrable from the public record accessible to anyone with an internet connection at the time the claim circulated.
