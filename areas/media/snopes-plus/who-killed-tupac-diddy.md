# Grand Jury 'Confirmed' Sean Combs Was Involved in Tupac Shakur's Murder?

## 1. The Claim

On October 5, 2023, a YouTube channel named Spill Today published a video with the title "Diddy's Role in 2Pac's Murder CONFIRMED as Grand Jury Transcript LEAKS." The video, which had garnered over 97,300 views at the time of Snopes' assessment, claimed that a grand jury had "confirmed" Sean "Diddy" Combs's involvement in the 1996 murder of rapper Tupac Shakur. The video was also circulated on TikTok, where one clip amassed over 522,000 views, as well as on Facebook.

The timing of the video was significant. It appeared just days after the September 29, 2023, arrest of Duane "Keffe D" Davis, who was charged with murder in connection with Shakur's death. Prosecutors alleged that Davis, a former gang leader, had ordered the 1996 drive-by shooting that killed Shakur in Las Vegas. On October 4, 2023, one day before the Spill Today video, Davis had made his first court appearance and requested a delay to retain counsel. The convergence of a major arrest, a high-profile court appearance, and a massive public appetite for true-crime content created fertile ground for sensational claims.

Snopes published its fact-check on October 6, 2023, authored by Izz Scott LaMagdeleine, rating the claim "Unfounded (retired)." The article correctly identified that the Spill Today video contained no evidence for its core assertion, that its content appeared to be generated or scripted using artificial intelligence tools, and that the channel itself included a disclaimer stating its content "might be gossip, rumors, or exaggerated." Snopes noted that the claim was made "without evidence whatsoever."

## 2. What's Actually True

The factual assessment that Snopes provides is, at the narrow level, correct. There was no grand jury transcript that "confirmed" Sean Combs's involvement in Tupac Shakur's murder. No official grand jury proceedings had produced such a finding. The Spill Today video was not a reliable source of information; it was a content farm operating in the celebrity gossip and true-crime rumor space, and its title was hyperbolic and misleading.

However, the Snopes article, by limiting its analysis to the specific claim about grand jury confirmation, creates a misleading impression of the broader evidentiary landscape. Several substantial truths about Combs's alleged involvement in Shakur's murder are omitted from the article:

First, Duane "Keffe D" Davis, the man charged with ordering Shakur's murder, had reportedly told investigators years before his arrest that Sean Combs had offered him $1 million to kill Shakur and Suge Knight, the CEO of Death Row Records. This claim was not a YouTube rumor; it was reported by the Tampa Bay Times in 2011 and was cited by Snopes in a parenthetical manner without emphasis or analysis. The existence of this claim, made by the man actually charged with the murder, is a material fact that the Snopes article downplays.

Second, the timing of Davis's arrest and Combs's subsequent legal troubles creates a legitimate context for public suspicion. In the months following the Snopes article, Combs faced a cascade of legal problems: multiple civil lawsuits alleging sexual assault and sex trafficking, a federal raid on his homes in Los Angeles and Miami in March 2024, and ultimately his arrest in September 2024 on federal charges including racketeering conspiracy, sex trafficking, and transportation for purposes of prostitution. While these charges are unrelated to Shakur's murder, they have created a broader narrative environment in which Combs's alleged connection to criminal activity appears increasingly plausible.

Third, the investigation into Shakur's murder has always involved allegations of a connection to the East Coast-West Coast hip-hop rivalry, with Combs as a central figure on the East Coast side. The 2011 book "Murder Rap," by former Los Angeles police detective Greg Kading, alleges that Combs was involved in a conspiracy to kill Shakur and that Davis was the intermediary. Kading's investigation, conducted as part of a joint LAPD-FBI task force, produced sworn statements and corroborating testimony that pointed to Combs's involvement. While Kading's conclusions are contested, they represent a serious investigative effort, not YouTube gossip.

Snopes' failure to engage with this established evidentiary landscape means that the article, while factually correct about the specific YouTube claim, functions as a partial dismissal of the broader question of Combs's involvement. A reader encountering the Snopes article alone would reasonably conclude that claims linking Combs to Shakur's murder are baseless. They are not baseless. They are unproven in a court of law, but they are grounded in investigator accounts, sworn testimony, and a criminal defendant's own statements.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes gets the specific fact-check right: the Spill Today video's claim of a grand jury "confirmation" is unsupported. The video's AI-generated appearance, its clickbait title, and its own disclaimer about containing gossip and rumors all support the "Unfounded" rating. The article correctly documents the video's reach, notes that skeptical commenters correctly identified it as unreliable, and provides context about Davis's arrest and court appearance. These are competent journalistic actions.

However, the article falls short in several significant ways that are consequential for public understanding of a major ongoing criminal case and the broader information ecosystem surrounding it.

**The most significant shortcoming is the absence of the broader evidence linking Combs to the Shakur case.** Snopes mentions Davis's claim that Combs offered money for the killing but does so in a single sentence buried in the middle of the article, without emphasis, analysis, or evaluation of its credibility. The 2011 investigative reporting, Kading's book, and the LAPD task force findings are not mentioned at all. A reader who knows only what Snopes reports would not know that serious law enforcement investigators have spent years pursuing the theory that Combs was involved. The Snopes article, by omission, sanitizes the evidentiary record and implies that the connection is a fringe conspiracy theory rather than an active line of investigation.

**Second, the Snopes article fails to distinguish between different categories of claims.** The Spill Today video made a specific, unsupported claim about a grand jury transcript. The broader claim—that Combs may have been involved in Shakur's murder—is a different claim with a different evidentiary basis. By treating the Spill Today video as representative of "claims linking Combs to Shakur's murder," the article conflates a baseless rumor with a serious investigative hypothesis. This conflation does a disservice to readers trying to understand the case.

**Third, the article's "Unfounded (retired)" rating is unusual and requires explanation that Snopes does not provide.** The "retired" designation suggests that Snopes has evaluated and moved on from the claim, but the article does not explain what retirement means in practice—whether the claim is no longer actively circulating, whether Snopes does not consider it worth updating, or whether the rating system itself has been revised. For readers unfamiliar with Snopes' rating taxonomy, this designation is confusing.

**Fourth, the article does not address the YouTube misinformation ecosystem.** Spill Today is not an isolated bad actor; it is part of a large and growing network of channels that produce AI-generated or loosely scripted content designed to maximize engagement through sensational claims. Snopes mentions that the channel's disclaimer acknowledges its content may be gossip, but it does not analyze the broader economic incentives that drive such channels, the role of YouTube's recommendation algorithm in amplifying them, or the impact they have on public understanding of high-profile cases.

**Fifth, the article misses the opportunity to correct the record about the actual state of the grand jury investigation into Shakur's murder.** A Clark County, Nevada, grand jury did indict Keffe D Davis. That grand jury did not issue findings about Combs. This is a simple distinction that would have been useful for readers to understand: there was a real grand jury, it did act, but its action was against Davis, not Combs.

## 4. Why This Matters

This fact-check matters for reasons that extend well beyond the accuracy of a single YouTube video about a decades-old murder case.

First, it matters because the question of Sean Combs's involvement in Tupac Shakur's murder is not a trivial celebrity gossip story. It is a question that involves a decades-long criminal investigation, allegations of a murder-for-hire conspiracy, and the intersection of the music industry and organized crime. The public's ability to understand this story accurately depends on journalists and fact-checkers treating it with the seriousness it deserves—which means neither sensationalizing unproven claims nor dismissing credible investigative work.

Second, this case illustrates a structural problem in how fact-checking organizations handle claims that are "partially true." The Spill Today video was wrong about a grand jury confirmation, but the broader context—Davis's allegations, Kading's investigation, the task force findings—contains legitimate evidence that Combs may have been involved. By treating the entire claim space as a single falsehood, Snopes performs what communication scholars call "false balance in reverse": it corrects a specific error in a way that implies the whole premise is baseless. This is a form of factual oversimplification that can be as misleading as the original falsehood.

Third, the article's handling of this case underscores the limitations of fact-checking in an environment where AI-generated content is proliferating. The Spill Today video appears to have been AI-assisted or AI-generated, a phenomenon that is becoming increasingly common on YouTube and TikTok. Fact-checkers are now being asked to evaluate not just human-spread misinformation but algorithmically produced falsehoods that can be created at scale with minimal effort. The volume of such content far exceeds the capacity of any fact-checking organization to address, raising questions about what kind of response is appropriate. Snopes' response—a single article evaluating a single video—is a case study in the mismatch between problem scale and response scale.

Fourth, this matters for the celebrity gossip and true-crime information ecosystem. The appetite for content about high-profile crimes is virtually unlimited, and the economic incentives for creating such content reward speed and sensationalism over accuracy. Fact-checkers who engage with this content must decide whether to evaluate every individual falsehood (an impossible task) or to focus on structural analysis that equips readers to evaluate claims themselves. The Snopes article, by choosing the former approach, provides a corrective for one video but does nothing to help readers understand why such videos exist, how they operate, or how to assess similar claims in the future.

Fifth, the article's publication date—October 6, 2023—places it at a pivotal moment in both the Shakur case and Combs's legal trajectory. The arrest of Keffe D Davis was the most significant development in the Shakur murder investigation in decades. Within a year, Combs would face federal charges that fundamentally altered his public standing. The Snopes article, by dismissing the Combs connection as unfounded without acknowledging the seriousness of the ongoing investigation, now reads as a piece that may have been factually correct at publication but that significantly underrepresented the weight of the evidence that would emerge.

## 5. Research Notes

The Snopes article was published on October 6, 2023, by Izz Scott LaMagdeleine, who is identified as a web producer and production editor for Snopes. The article is categorized with a rating of "Unfounded (retired)," a rating Snopes defines as applying to claims for which "we have investigated but found no demonstrable evidence to support it" and which "typically originate as hearsay, speculation, or groundless rumor."

The article's primary evidence consists of: (1) the Spill Today YouTube video itself, which Snopes identifies as the source of the claim; (2) the video's disclaimer about content being gossip or exaggerated; (3) the video's apparent AI-generated appearance; (4) documentation of the video's circulation on TikTok and Facebook; (5) news reporting about Keffe D Davis's arrest and court appearance; and (6) Vanity Fair's reporting on Combs's denial of Davis's allegations.

The article cites four sources: Vanity Fair, the Tampa Bay Archives (referencing the 2011 Ex-Cop's book reporting), the AP News (two articles on Davis's arrest and court appearance), and a previous Snopes article about a related claim. The article does not cite Kading's "Murder Rap" book, the LAPD task force investigation, or any legal filings related to the case.

The article's length is approximately 600 words, making it one of Snopes' shorter fact-checks on a high-profile topic. The brevity is notable given the complexity of the underlying case and the volume of public interest in the story.

The article acknowledges that Davis told investigators Combs offered money for the killings but does not evaluate the credibility of this claim or explore why an individual charged with murder would make such an allegation. The article also does not address the question of whether Davis's claim is corroborated by other evidence, statements, or witness testimony.

The article correctly notes that Spill Today's video included a content disclaimer, but it does not analyze the legal or ethical implications of content producers using disclaimers to insulate themselves from accountability for deceptive content. This is a growing phenomenon in the AI-generated content space and deserves more attention than the article provides.

The article's framing treats the Combs-Shakur connection as a fringe claim propagated by unreliable sources. While the specific claim about a grand jury confirmation is indeed unreliable, the broader connection is a matter of active law enforcement investigation, documented in sworn statements and investigative reports. The article's failure to make this distinction is its most significant limitation.

## 6. Verdict

**Rating: The specific claim is unfounded, but the article's narrow framing significantly underrepresents the evidence linking Sean Combs to the Tupac Shakur murder case.**

The Spill Today video's assertion that a grand jury had "confirmed" Sean Combs's role in Shakur's murder is unsupported and correctly rated as unfounded. Snopes' identification of the video as unreliable, AI-generated, and self-disclaimed as gossip is accurate.

However, the article fails to distinguish between the specific false claim about a grand jury transcript and the broader, evidence-supported hypothesis that Combs may have been involved in Shakur's murder. By treating the entire domain of claims about Combs's involvement as if it were reducible to the Spill Today video, the article creates a misleading impression of the evidentiary landscape. Keffe D Davis, the man charged with ordering the murder, has stated that Combs offered payment for the killing. Former LAPD detective Greg Kading's task force investigation produced evidence consistent with this account. These are not YouTube rumors; they are the subject of active litigation and law enforcement interest.

The Snopes article is a competent narrow fact-check that fails the broader test of contextual accuracy. It corrects a specific falsehood while potentially misleading readers about the state of the evidence. In a case where the difference between "unfounded YouTube rumor" and "unproven but seriously investigated allegation" matters enormously to public understanding, the article's omissions are consequential.

A more complete fact-check would have clearly delineated: (1) what the Spill Today video claimed and why it was false; (2) what evidence does exist linking Combs to the Shakur case and where it comes from; (3) the difference between unsupported YouTube content and law enforcement investigation findings; and (4) guidance for readers on how to evaluate future claims about this fast-moving case. That article has not yet been written.
