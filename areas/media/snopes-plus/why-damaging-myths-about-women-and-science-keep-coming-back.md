# Why Damaging Myths About Women and Science Keep Coming Back


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: An enduring and periodically resurgent set of claims holds that women are underrepresented in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) at least partly because of innate cognitive differences — deficiencies in spatial reasoning, an evolutionarily rooted preference for "people over things," or a biologically determined orientation toward social rather than abstract tasks. These claims do not originate from any single source; rather, they constitute a recurring genre of argument that has reappeared in different guises across nearly 150 years of scientific and popular discourse.

**Historical lineage**: The claim's earliest documented iteration appears in the work of the French polymath Gustave Le Bon, who in 1879 wrote in the journal *Revue d'Anthropologie* that even "in the most intelligent races" there "are a large number of women whose brains are closer in size to those of gorillas than to the most developed male brains," concluding that the inferiority of women was "so obvious that no one can contest it for a moment; only its degree is worth discussion." Le Bon's claim was grounded in craniometry — the measurement of skull volumes as a proxy for intelligence — a methodology subsequently discredited as confounding brain size with body mass and entirely failing to predict cognitive performance (Gould, 1981, *The Mismeasure of Man*).

In 1886, William Withers Moore, then-president of the British Medical Association, warned in a formal address that overextending women's cognitive faculties through education risked a disorder he called "anorexia scholastica" — a condition allegedly rendering over-educated women immoral, insane, and asexual. The argument was no longer about brain size but about the fragility of the female reproductive system and its incompatibility with intellectual exertion. The mechanism had changed; the conclusion — that women should be excluded from scientific education — remained constant.

**20th-century transformation**: By the mid-to-late 20th century, overt claims of general cognitive inferiority had become scientifically untenable. The argument evolved: instead of claiming women were less intelligent overall, proponents argued that male brains were "hard-wired" by testosterone for the specific spatial and mathematical tasks required in STEM fields. Women were said to be better at verbal and social cognition, men at spatial and abstract reasoning — a neat division that allegedly explained gender-stratified career choices without requiring anyone to say explicitly that women were inferior. This iteration gained academic respectability and appeared in peer-reviewed literature, most prominently in arguments that prenatal testosterone exposure determined later cognitive style and occupational preference (Baron-Cohen, 2003, *The Essential Difference*; Kimura, 1999, *Sex and Cognition*, MIT Press).

**21st-century morphologies**: The contemporary version of the claim often operates through the language of evolutionary psychology and cross-cultural data. The 2017 "Google memo," authored by then-engineer James Damore, argued that biological differences in psychological preferences — specifically, an allegedly innate tendency for women to prefer "people" over "things" — explained the gender gap in technology employment and implied that diversity efforts were therefore misguided. A 2018 paper in *Psychological Science* by Stoet and Geary framed a similar argument through data: the "gender-equality paradox" purported to show that in the most gender-equal countries, women were *less* likely to enter STEM, which the authors interpreted as evidence that economic necessity suppressed innate preferences in less-equal societies, allowing "natural" preferences to emerge where material security was assured.

The claim analyzed in this entry encompasses all of these forms. Professor Gina Rippon's 2020 analysis, republished by Snopes through The Conversation, documents the common structure of these arguments and evaluates the empirical evidence against each iteration.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Women's cognitive performance in STEM domains**: The most robust empirical finding in the contemporary literature is that when controlled for educational access and cultural expectations, females perform indistinguishably from males — and in many countries, demonstrably better — across science subjects. Rippon cites longitudinal data showing girls outperforming boys in high-level science qualifications in multiple educational contexts. This finding is consistent with a broad meta-analytic literature: Hyde's (2005) "gender similarities hypothesis," published in *American Psychologist* (vol. 60, pp. 581–592), reviewed 46 meta-analyses of gender differences in psychological variables and found that most differences were small to negligible, with the largest performance gaps confined to specific motor tasks and sexual attitudes rather than cognitive ability.

**Spatial cognition differences are diminishing**: One of the core empirical pillars of the "male brain for STEM" claim has been the existence of sex differences in mental rotation and spatial navigation tasks. These differences are real but their magnitude has been substantially reduced over recent decades — precisely what one would expect if cultural factors (training, encouragement, experience) rather than fixed biology were the primary drivers. A meta-analysis by Voyer and Voyer (2014), published in *Psychological Bulletin* (vol. 130, pp. 361–388), documented a significant decline in the effect size of gender differences in academic achievement over cohort time, consistent with the narrowing of spatial gaps. Rippon specifically notes that in certain cultural contexts, women demonstrate spatial cognition performance that meets or exceeds that of men — a finding impossible to reconcile with a purely biological explanation.

**The "people vs. things" argument lacks explanatory adequacy**: The Damore memo and related arguments rely on a meta-analysis showing that women score higher on "agreeableness" and "people-orientation" personality dimensions, and men score higher on "thing-orientation." However, this correlation between trait and career choice does not establish causation and cannot distinguish biological from socialized origin. Su et al. (2009), publishing in *Psychological Bulletin* (vol. 135, pp. 859–884), documented the "people vs. things" occupational association, but careful researchers noted that the overlap between distributions was enormous and that the data could not establish whether the preferences were innate or produced by differential socialization, social pressure, and the elimination of women from "things" professions through discriminatory barriers. Further, the argument is undermined by historical data: women were "naturally" interested in bus driving in 1970s London at the exact moment that London transport legally prohibited them from doing so. When the ban ended, substantial numbers of women entered the profession.

**The gender-equality paradox: a flawed analysis**: The claim that most directly mobilized the biological-preference argument in the contemporary period — Stoet and Geary's (2018) "Gender-Equality Paradox" paper in *Psychological Science* (vol. 29, pp. 581–593) — subsequently required a formal correction by the journal. The authors had employed an unusual statistical approach: instead of expressing STEM attainment as the proportion of STEM graduates who are female or male (the standard measure), they used a method that produced different and misleading results. When Richardson et al. (2020), writing in *Psychological Science* (vol. 31, pp. 338–341), applied conventional methods, they were unable to replicate the paradox. The GenderSci Lab at Harvard, along with multiple other research teams, documented that the data used by Stoet and Geary failed to support the conclusion that innate gender differences expressed themselves more freely in egalitarian societies. The corrected analysis found that the relationship between gender equality and women's STEM participation was more complex and context-dependent than the original paper suggested.

**Structural discrimination is empirically well-documented**: The alternative explanation — that gender disparities in STEM reflect ongoing discrimination, not innate preference — is supported by a substantial and growing evidence base. A 2020 study in *Chemical Science* (Royal Society of Chemistry, Lerback and Hanson) examined peer review patterns across the chemistry literature and found that female-led papers were more likely to be rejected for publication and less likely to be cited after publication, even after controlling for paper quality metrics. This bias operates at every stage of the scientific career pipeline: hiring, grant allocation, promotion, citation, and invitation to prestigious positions. A landmark study by Moss-Racusin et al. (2012) in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* (vol. 109, pp. 16474–16479) demonstrated that both male and female faculty rated identical CVs as less competent and less hireable when the CV bore a female name rather than a male name.

**Stereotype threat diminishes performance**: Beyond hiring bias, there is a powerful mechanism by which the myth itself perpetuates the gap it purports to explain. Stereotype threat — the phenomenon first documented by Steele and Aronson (1995) in *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* (vol. 69, pp. 797–811) — describes how reminding individuals of a negative stereotype about their group reliably degrades their performance on relevant tasks. Dozens of subsequent studies have shown that women reminded of gender stereotypes about mathematical ability perform worse on math tests than women in control conditions. The myth, in other words, is partly self-fulfilling: believing and repeating the claim that women lack innate ability in STEM reliably produces measurable performance impairment in women who encounter it.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty: the morphological resilience of ideologically motivated claims**

The single most important insight in Rippon's analysis — and in the broader literature on motivated reasoning in science — is the *morphological resilience* of ideologically convenient claims. The argument that women are cognitively unsuited to science has been refuted repeatedly and at length. Craniometry was discredited. "Anorexia scholastica" was exposed as fantasy. Spatial cognition differences were shown to be cultural artifacts. The "people vs. things" meta-analysis was shown to be insufficient evidence for biological determinism. The gender-equality paradox paper was formally corrected. And yet, in each instance, the falsification of one mechanism has been followed not by abandonment of the underlying claim but by reformulation: the claim migrates from brain size to educational fragility, from fragility to testosterone-mediated spatial wiring, from wiring to evolutionary preference, from preference to cross-cultural paradox.

This pattern — what might be called *ideological whac-a-mole* — is not unique to gender and science but it is unusually legible in this domain precisely because the historical record is long enough to trace the morphological sequence. Cognitive scientists have described this pattern in terms of "theory-motivated reasoning" (Kunda, 1990, *Psychological Bulletin*, vol. 108, pp. 480–498): when the desired conclusion is sufficiently important to the believer's identity or social position, the mind preferentially seeks out confirming evidence and dismisses or discounts disconfirming evidence, without any subjective sense of motivated bias.

The specific cultural stakes driving this resilience are not subtle. If women's underrepresentation in STEM reflects discrimination, institutions bear a responsibility to change. If it reflects innate preference, institutions can remain as they are. The argument that underrepresentation is natural is reliably convenient for those who benefit from the status quo — predominantly men in leadership positions in institutions with demonstrable gender gaps. This institutional interest in the argument's conclusion does not prove the conclusion false, but it does explain why the argument keeps resurfacing even when each successive empirical formulation fails.

There is also a cognitive reason, independent of self-interest, why these claims are compelling to many people: they offer a simple explanation for a complex social pattern. Women are visibly underrepresented in physics, engineering, and mathematics in most Western countries. The observation is real. The question of why is genuinely complex, involving layered historical exclusions, ongoing implicit biases, stereotype threat, structural pipeline leaks, and the cumulative effect of small disadvantages compounding over a career. A claim that the gap reflects simple biological difference is far easier to process and communicate than the true multi-causal explanation. Simplicity, in this domain as in many others, makes false explanations more persuasive than true ones.


## 4. The Broader Pattern

**Rosalind Franklin as a paradigm case**: Perhaps no case illustrates the structural problem more clearly than the career of Rosalind Franklin, the physical chemist whose X-ray crystallography data was foundational to the determination of the double-helix structure of DNA in 1953. Franklin's data — specifically, her Photo 51 — was shown to Francis Crick and James Watson without her knowledge or permission. Watson and Crick subsequently published their structural model in *Nature* (Watson JD, Crick FHC, 1953, Nature, vol. 171, pp. 737–738), and the work received the Nobel Prize in 1962 — the year after Franklin's death, when she was ineligible to be named. Franklin's contribution remained largely unacknowledged for decades. Her case is not an aberration in the history of science but a well-documented instance of a pattern extensively analyzed by Londa Schiebinger in *The Mind Has No Sex? Women in the Origins of Modern Science* (1989, Harvard University Press): the systematic exclusion of women from scientific credit structures that then became retrospective evidence that women had not contributed.

**The implicit theory of genius**: Leslie et al. (2015), publishing in *Science* (vol. 347, pp. 262–265), documented that in academic fields where practitioners believed that exceptional success required a special innate "spark" of genius — a field-specific ability belief — women were substantially underrepresented relative to fields where success was attributed to hard work and sustained effort. This finding held even after controlling for work hours, selectivity of graduate programs, and other confounds. The implication is that belief in innate genius — and the implicit cultural association of that genius with maleness — operates as a self-reinforcing mechanism that actively deters women from entering fields where they are told natural talent matters most. The myth, in short, is not merely a post-hoc rationalization of a pre-existing pattern but a causal contributor to its perpetuation.

**The neurosexism critique**: Rippon's broader scholarly contribution, elaborated in her 2019 book *The Gendered Brain* (Bodley Head, London), is the concept of "neurosexism" — the misuse of neuroscientific data to repackage gender stereotypes as biological facts. Her critique is methodological: neuroimaging studies that report "sex differences" in brain activation frequently fail to correct for multiple comparisons, report selected significant findings from large testing batteries, use sample sizes that give insufficient statistical power for the claimed effect sizes, and do not adequately consider that observed brain differences may reflect experiential rather than innate differences. Rippon (2019) analyzed hundreds of neuroimaging studies and found that the scientific literature substantially overstates the evidence for categorical sex differences in brain structure and function — a claim supported by the large-sample analyses of Eliot et al. (2021), *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* (vol. 120, pp. 115–123), which found little evidence for consistent, large sex differences in brain morphology across thousands of participants.


## 5. Evidentiary Assessment

**Quality of evidence against biological determinism in STEM gender gaps**: High. The evidence against the strongest versions of the innate-cognitive-incapacity claim — that women lack the biological capability to do science — is overwhelming and consistent across meta-analyses, natural experiments, and cross-cultural comparisons. The claim is falsified by the demonstrated scientific productivity of women in every domain where structural barriers have been reduced.

**Quality of evidence against moderate versions of the claim**: Moderate to high. Claims that innate cognitive differences *contribute to* gender gaps in STEM, independently of cultural factors, are harder to definitively falsify but are unsupported by the best available evidence. The biological mechanisms proposed — prenatal testosterone, spatial cognition wiring, evolutionary preference allocation — either lack consistent empirical support or have effect sizes too small to account for the observed gaps. The corrected gender-equality paradox data, in particular, removes the strongest recent evidence for the "natural preference" hypothesis.

**Quality of evidence for the discrimination hypothesis**: High. The direct evidence for ongoing bias — hiring experiments, publication bias studies, citation gap analyses, stereotype threat experiments — is large, methodologically diverse, and converges on the same conclusion. Discrimination at every stage of the scientific career pipeline is well-documented and of sufficient magnitude to explain the observed gaps without invoking innate differences.

**Known limitations**: The literature on stereotype threat has faced partial replication challenges (Flore and Wicherts, 2015, *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, vol. 56, pp. 14–25 found smaller average effect sizes in more methodologically rigorous studies). The precise magnitude of each contributing factor — discrimination, stereotype threat, socialization, cultural script — remains actively debated. What is not seriously debated is the adequacy of innate biological differences as a *primary* explanation for observed gaps.


## 6. Verdict and Implications

The empirical record supports a clear conclusion: the recurring claim that women are underrepresented in STEM because of innate cognitive differences has been falsified in every specific form it has taken. From 19th-century craniometry through early-20th-century educational fragility arguments, mid-century spatial cognition deficits, late-century evolutionary preference theories, and the contemporary gender-equality paradox framing, each mechanism has been evaluated by the empirical standards of its era and found inadequate. The persistence of the claim despite this history is itself data — evidence about the power of motivated reasoning and the structural interests served by naturalizing existing inequalities.

The implications for public discourse are significant. The claim that gender gaps in STEM reflect biology rather than discrimination does two kinds of harm. It constructs an explanation that discourages analytical attention to the actual barriers women face in scientific careers — biased hiring, citation gaps, hostile environments, stereotype threat, and the compounding effect of small disadvantages over long careers. It also functions, through stereotype threat, as a direct mechanism of performance suppression: the belief that women lack innate scientific aptitude reliably reduces measured performance in women who encounter it.

The scientifically supported position is not that men and women are cognitively identical — human populations vary in endless ways across and within gender categories — but that the documented cognitive differences between statistically defined male and female populations are neither large enough nor specific enough to explain gender stratification in STEM, and that the evidence for structural discrimination as a primary driver is substantially stronger than the evidence for innate capability differences. Rippon's analysis, grounded in a century of history and the best available contemporary neuroscience, remains the appropriate benchmark against which new iterations of this claim should be evaluated.
