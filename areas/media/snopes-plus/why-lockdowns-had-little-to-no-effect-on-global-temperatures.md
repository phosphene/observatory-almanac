# Why Lockdowns Had Little to No Effect on Global Temperatures

## 1. The Question

In the spring of 2020, something unprecedented happened. One-third of the world's population was placed under lockdown. Streets emptied. Factories fell silent. Airports became ghost terminals. Car travel dropped by 50%. Flights plummeted by 75%. Industrial activity contracted by roughly 35%. Global carbon dioxide emissions fell by approximately 17% compared to the same period in 2019 (Le Quéré et al., 2020, *Nature Climate Change*, vol. 10).

It was, in effect, the largest unplanned experiment in human history on what happens when industrial civilisation abruptly slows down. And the question on many minds — from policymakers to the public — was straightforward: if we stop burning so much fossil fuel, will the planet cool?

The intuitive answer feels obvious. Less activity means fewer emissions. Fewer emissions should mean less warming. The skies over Delhi cleared. Satellite imagery showed nitrogen dioxide vanishing over European cities. Venetian canals ran clear. Surely, this dramatic reduction in human emissions would register on the global thermometer.

It didn't. Not in any meaningful way.

Global temperatures during the lockdown period showed no statistically significant deviation from what climate models had already predicted. The planet continued to warm along the same trajectory it had been following for decades. The 2020 lockdowns, despite being the most dramatic reduction in human economic activity since the Second World War, left essentially no fingerprint on the global temperature record.

This result surprised many people. It should not have surprised atmospheric scientists, who had long predicted exactly this outcome. But the *reason* it didn't surprise them reveals something profound about how the climate system actually works — and how badly our common-sense intuitions can mislead us when dealing with complex systems governed by competing feedback loops.

The explanation comes from research conducted by Scott Archer-Nicholls, a postdoctoral research associate in atmospheric science at the University of Cambridge, and James Weber, a PhD candidate in atmospheric chemistry at Pembroke College, University of Cambridge. Their work, along with contributions from their colleagues, was published in *Geophysical Research Letters* in 2020 (DOI: 10.1029/2020GL090326) and later made accessible through The Conversation.

What they found upends the simple narrative — and teaches a lesson that extends far beyond climate science into how we think about any complex system.

## 2. What the Research Found

Archer-Nicholls and Weber's research team ran detailed computer model simulations covering the lockdown period from mid-February to mid-June 2020. Their models incorporated not just carbon dioxide reductions, but the full spectrum of pollutants that changed during lockdowns — including aerosols, nitrogen oxides, soot, and sulphur dioxide.

The headline numbers were dramatic in their own right. The drop in global CO₂ emissions by approximately 17% was the largest year-on-year reduction ever recorded. Le Quéré and colleagues, publishing in *Nature Climate Change* (vol. 10, 2020), documented these reductions in detail: transport was the most heavily affected sector, with car travel halving and aviation falling by three-quarters. Heavy industry — steel production, cement manufacturing, energy generation — contracted by roughly 35%.

But CO₂ was not the only thing that fell. And this is where the story becomes far more interesting than a simple tale of "less pollution, less warming."

When the researchers modelled the full atmospheric effects of lockdown, accounting for all the different pollutants that were simultaneously reduced, they found that the opposing effects of different pollutants largely cancelled each other out. The net climate influence was small, temporary, and — crucially — exactly what the science had predicted.

The lockdown did not produce a detectable cooling signal. It produced something closer to a wash: a slight reshuffling of heating and cooling effects that, when summed globally, amounted to nearly nothing on the thermometer.

To understand why, we need to look beyond carbon dioxide at the full cast of atmospheric characters that were affected by lockdown — and at the physics that governs each one.

## 3. The Competing Feedbacks Problem

Here is where the lockdown story becomes a masterclass in why complex systems routinely defeat naive intuition.

### The Intuitive but Wrong Mental Model

The most natural way to think about climate change is linear and additive: humans burn fossil fuels, which releases CO₂, which traps heat, which warms the planet. Under this mental model, if you reduce fossil fuel burning, you should reduce warming. The more you cut, the more the planet cools. It's clean, it's intuitive, and it's wrong — or at least, it's so incomplete as to be misleading.

The error is not in any individual link of the chain. Each of those statements is true in isolation. The error is in assuming that CO₂ is the only thing that comes out of smokestacks and exhaust pipes, and that all the things that come out have the same effect.

They don't. Industrial civilisation emits a complex cocktail of substances into the atmosphere, and these substances have different — sometimes *opposite* — effects on the Earth's energy balance. When you shut down this cocktail simultaneously, you don't get a simple sum. You get a contest between competing forces.

### The Aerosol Cooling Mask

The critical insight that Archer-Nicholls and Weber's work confirms is that industrial pollution has been simultaneously warming *and* cooling the planet. The warming comes from greenhouse gases like CO₂ and ozone. The cooling comes from aerosols — tiny particles suspended in the atmosphere that reflect incoming solar radiation back into space before it can heat the Earth's surface.

This phenomenon is known as **global dimming**. Since the onset of industrialisation, the aerosols pumped into the atmosphere by coal burning, steel production, and cement manufacturing have been reflecting a significant fraction of incoming sunlight. This creates a cooling effect that partially masks the warming caused by greenhouse gases. In other words, the actual amount of warming caused by CO₂ and other greenhouse gases is *larger* than what we observe, because aerosol cooling has been offsetting some of it.

Previous studies had already predicted what would happen if industrial activity suddenly stopped. Samset and colleagues, publishing in the *Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres* (2018), ran model simulations showing that a sudden cessation of industrial emissions would, counterintuitively, produce short-term *warming* — not cooling. The reason: the reflective aerosols would clear from the atmosphere within days to weeks, removing the cooling mask almost instantly, while the greenhouse gases already accumulated would persist for decades to centuries.

The COVID-19 lockdown was not a complete cessation, of course. But it was the closest the real world had come to testing this prediction. And the prediction held.

### Why CO₂ and Aerosols Demand Different Mental Models

This is the deepest lesson of the lockdown experiment, and it concerns **timescales**.

Carbon dioxide, once emitted, remains in the atmosphere for centuries. Methane persists for roughly a decade. These are "long-lived" greenhouse gases. They accumulate. Every tonne emitted adds to the total already present, and that total only decreases very slowly through natural absorption processes. Even the 17% emission reduction during lockdown was not enough to stop CO₂ concentrations from *continuing to grow*. The Mauna Loa Observatory recorded CO₂ levels that kept climbing right through the pandemic. The rate of increase may have slowed fractionally, but the concentration — the thing that actually determines warming — kept rising.

Aerosols, by contrast, are "short-lived." Sulphur dioxide, black carbon, and the particles they form typically remain in the atmosphere for days to weeks before they are washed out by rain or settle to the surface. They do not accumulate in any meaningful sense. Their cooling effect is maintained only by continuous emission. Stop emitting, and within days the aerosol cooling vanishes.

This timescale asymmetry is the engine of the counter-intuitive result. When lockdowns reduced emissions across the board:

- The greenhouse gas concentration barely changed, because what's already up there overwhelms a few months of reduced additions.
- The aerosol concentration dropped rapidly, because aerosols don't accumulate — stop emitting and they clear out.

The warming influence stayed roughly constant. The cooling influence weakened. The net effect was, at best, neutral — and in some regions, lockdown actually produced a slight *warming* signal.

### The Systems Thinking Lesson

The lockdown teaches something that applies far beyond climate science: **in any system with competing feedback loops operating on different timescales, disrupting all inputs simultaneously does not produce the effect you'd expect from disrupting any one input in isolation.**

If you *only* reduced CO₂ without touching aerosols, you would expect (very slow, very gradual) cooling over decades. If you *only* reduced aerosols without touching CO₂, you would expect rapid warming. When you reduce both at once — as lockdown did — the fast-acting aerosol effect dominates the short-term response, and the slow-acting CO₂ effect is imperceptible.

This is a general principle of complex systems: naive subtraction rarely works as expected. The economy, the immune system, ecosystems, and the atmosphere all share this property. Removing a single factor in isolation might produce a predictable result. Removing multiple interacting factors simultaneously will not, unless you understand the feedback structure in detail.

For mental models of climate, the lockdown should permanently retire the simple "less burning = cooler planet" heuristic and replace it with: "the climate response depends on *which* emissions change, *how fast* they leave the atmosphere, and *what balance of heating and cooling forces* they were maintaining."

## 4. The Chemistry in Detail

The researchers identified three key pollutants — beyond CO₂ — whose lockdown-driven reductions shaped the temperature response: sulphur dioxide (SO₂), black carbon (soot), and nitrogen oxides (NOₓ). Each has a distinct atmospheric chemistry and a distinct effect on the planet's energy balance.

### Sulphur Dioxide and the White Veil

Sulphur dioxide is emitted primarily by coal-burning power plants and heavy industrial processes like steel and cement production. Once in the atmosphere, SO₂ undergoes chemical reactions that produce fine white sulphate aerosol particles. These particles are highly reflective. They scatter incoming sunlight back to space, creating a net cooling effect — the "global dimming" mentioned above.

During lockdown, the contraction in heavy industry reduced SO₂ emissions significantly, particularly in regions with large coal-dependent industrial sectors. If SO₂ were the *only* pollutant affected by lockdowns, the prediction is clear: removing the reflective particles would allow more solar energy to reach the surface, and the result would be transient warming. The cooling mask would thin, and we would feel more of the underlying greenhouse warming.

### Black Carbon and the Dark Absorber

Black carbon — soot — is produced by the incomplete combustion of fossil fuels, biomass, and biofuels. It is emitted heavily by older diesel vehicles, industrial furnaces, and open burning. Unlike sulphate aerosols, black carbon *absorbs* sunlight rather than reflecting it. When soot particles absorb solar radiation, they heat the surrounding atmosphere directly. Black carbon deposited on snow and ice reduces the surface's reflectivity (albedo), causing additional warming.

The lockdown disproportionately affected the transport sector, which is a major source of black carbon emissions — particularly in countries with older vehicle fleets. The reduction in black carbon had a cooling effect: less soot in the atmosphere meant less solar absorption, which partially counteracted the warming effect of losing reflective sulphate aerosols.

### Nitrogen Oxides and the Ozone Puzzle

Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) are emitted by internal combustion engines, aircraft, and industrial processes. In the lower atmosphere (the troposphere), NOₓ drives chemical reactions that produce ozone (O₃). Tropospheric ozone is itself a greenhouse gas — it traps outgoing infrared radiation and contributes to warming.

Satellite imagery from March and April 2020 revealed dramatic reductions in NOₓ concentrations over European cities and industrial regions during lockdown. With less NOₓ being emitted, less tropospheric ozone was formed, which reduced the greenhouse warming contribution from ozone. This represented another cooling influence, joining the black carbon reduction in opposing the warming from SO₂ loss.

### The Competing Effects, Quantified

The largest emission reductions during lockdown were in the transport sector — precisely the sector that produces the most NOₓ and black carbon relative to SO₂. Heavy industry, which produces the most SO₂, was reduced but not as dramatically as transport. This sectoral asymmetry was key to the near-cancellation of effects:

- **Transport shutdown → less NOₓ and black carbon → cooling effect** (less ozone greenhouse warming, less atmospheric heat absorption by soot).
- **Industrial contraction → less SO₂ → warming effect** (loss of reflective sulphate aerosols).

The cooling from reduced transport pollutants roughly offset the warming from reduced industrial aerosols. The net global temperature signal was therefore negligible.

### Regional Variations: The World Is Not Uniform

While the global average told a story of cancellation, the regional picture was more textured. The Cambridge team's simulations revealed distinct geographical patterns:

**The Middle East** experienced a slight cooling during lockdown. The mechanism: reduced black carbon emissions meant less soot deposited on desert sands. Desert sand is naturally highly reflective, but soot darkens it. With less soot deposition, the desert surface reflected more solar energy back to space, producing localised cooling.

**Eastern China** showed the opposite pattern — a slight warming. China's heavy industrial base generates enormous quantities of SO₂. The contraction in Chinese steel, cement, and coal production reduced SO₂ emissions substantially, thinning the sulphate aerosol layer over the region and allowing more solar radiation to reach the surface. The warming from aerosol loss outweighed the cooling from reduced transport emissions in this heavily industrialised region.

These contrasting regional responses illustrate the spatial complexity of aerosol-climate interactions. Different regions have different emission profiles, different atmospheric dynamics, and different surface characteristics. A change that cools one region can warm another — and the researchers noted that these differential heating patterns could, if sustained, affect large-scale weather systems including monsoon cycles.

## 5. What Lockdown Revealed About Climate Science

### An Invaluable Natural Experiment

Climate scientists acknowledged that the lockdown was an imperfect experiment. Model simulations, however sophisticated, involve approximations and uncertainties. Real-world conditions are messier than any model can fully capture. But the researchers also recognised that the pandemic provided something rare and precious: a real-world perturbation of the global emission system that could be compared against model predictions.

And the models performed well. The predicted near-cancellation of competing effects was borne out by observations. This provides a degree of validation for the atmospheric models used to study aerosol-climate interactions — models that are central to understanding one of the most uncertain aspects of climate science.

Aerosol-climate interaction has long been identified as a primary source of uncertainty in estimates of *climate sensitivity* — the amount of warming expected from a doubling of atmospheric CO₂ (Nature Scitable, 2012). The global dimming effect of aerosols could be large or moderate, and the range of estimates has significant implications for how much warming we should expect this century. The lockdown provided a natural test case that helps constrain these estimates.

### Long-Lived vs. Short-Lived: The Fundamental Asymmetry

The lockdown starkly illustrated the difference between pollutants that accumulate and pollutants that do not. NOₓ, SO₂, and black carbon clear from the atmosphere in days to weeks. Their climate effects are maintained only by continuous emission and vanish almost as soon as emissions stop. CO₂ and methane, by contrast, persist for decades to centuries. CO₂ concentrations continued to rise throughout 2020 despite the largest emission reduction in modern history.

This asymmetry has a profound implication: **global heating stops only when greenhouse gas emissions reach net zero.** Not when they are reduced. Not when they fall by 17%. Not even when global civilisation grinds almost to a halt for months. The accumulated stock of CO₂ in the atmosphere — built up over more than a century of fossil fuel combustion — continues to trap heat regardless of what this year's flow of new emissions looks like.

### The 2008 Comparison

The lockdown was not the first time a global economic shock had reduced emissions. The 2008 financial crisis produced a similar, though less dramatic, contraction in industrial activity and a corresponding dip in global CO₂ emissions. Peters and colleagues, publishing in *Nature Climate Change* (2012), documented what happened next: emissions quickly rebounded and then exceeded pre-crisis levels, driven in part by fossil-fuel-heavy economic rescue packages.

The pattern is instructive. Temporary disruptions produce temporary emission reductions. But the infrastructure — the power plants, the vehicle fleets, the industrial processes — remains in place. When economic activity resumes, emissions resume with it. Unless the recovery is structured around fundamentally different energy systems, the dip is just a dip. It leaves no lasting imprint on the atmospheric CO₂ concentration or on the trajectory of global warming.

The 2020 lockdown replayed this pattern at larger scale. The emission reduction was historic, but it was also temporary and driven by economic contraction rather than structural change. As economies reopened, emissions returned to near-baseline levels.

## 6. Implications for Climate Action

The lockdown experiment carries several implications that are directly relevant to climate policy and public understanding.

### Emission Reductions Must Be Structural, Not Temporary

If the largest peacetime reduction in human economic activity failed to register on the global thermometer, then it is clear that temporary disruptions to economic output are not a climate strategy. The atmospheric CO₂ concentration responds to the accumulated total of emissions over time, not to the rate in any given month or quarter. Only a permanent transition away from fossil fuels — replacing the infrastructure, not just temporarily idling it — will alter the long-term trajectory.

This does not mean emission reductions are futile. Every tonne of CO₂ that is not emitted is a tonne that will not contribute to warming for centuries. But the benefits of emission reductions are realised on decadal timescales, not monthly ones. The lockdown reduced emissions for months; the climate system, dominated by the century-long accumulation of greenhouse gases, understandably did not flinch.

### Aerosol Cleanup Is a Double-Edged Sword

The lockdown provided a vivid real-world demonstration of a challenge that climate policymakers already understood in theory: cleaning up industrial air pollution, while essential for human health, removes the aerosol cooling mask and reveals more of the underlying greenhouse warming. This does not mean we should keep polluting — the health costs of particulate air pollution are enormous and well-documented. But it does mean that as nations clean up their industrial emissions (as China has been doing aggressively), they should expect a transient acceleration in local warming and plan accordingly.

### Beware the Simplification Trap

Perhaps the most important implication is epistemological. The public discourse around climate change frequently relies on simplified narratives: more CO₂ means more warming; less burning means less warming; if we just stop emitting, the problem will reverse. Each of these statements contains a kernel of truth, but each can also mislead when applied to a system as complex as the global atmosphere.

The lockdown demonstrated in real time that the climate system is not a simple thermostat connected to a single dial marked "emissions." It is a network of interacting processes, operating on different timescales, with opposing effects that partially cancel. Reducing *all* emissions simultaneously does not produce the same result as reducing *one type* in isolation. The system's response depends on the specific mix of pollutants affected, the sectors and regions involved, and the timescales over which different atmospheric constituents act.

### The Path Forward

The research by Archer-Nicholls, Weber, and their Cambridge colleagues reinforces a conclusion that climate science has delivered with increasing clarity over recent decades: only a decisive, permanent shift away from fossil fuels will stabilise global temperatures. Not a temporary dip. Not an economic shock. Not even a pandemic that confines a third of humanity to their homes.

The lockdown was many things — a human tragedy, an economic upheaval, a social experiment. It was also an invaluable atmospheric experiment, one that confirmed the predictions of climate models, illuminated the competing forces at work in our atmosphere, and demonstrated with uncomfortable clarity that the climate crisis is not amenable to quick fixes. The CO₂ already in the atmosphere will continue to warm the planet for decades regardless of what we do tomorrow. What we do tomorrow determines whether we add to that burden, or begin the long work of stabilising it.

The thermometer barely moved during the largest economic disruption of the twenty-first century. That is not evidence that climate action is pointless. It is evidence that climate action must be structural, sustained, and — above all — aimed at the root cause: the fossil-fuel infrastructure itself.
