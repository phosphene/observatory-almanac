# Did Scientists Discover Why Wombat Poop Is Square?


## 1. The Claim

In early 2021, a broad wave of science media coverage reported an apparent breakthrough in one of the more unexpected corners of biological research: scientists had finally determined the mechanism by which wombats produce their famously cubic feces. The claim that circulated on social media and news outlets stated that in January 2021, scientists published findings identifying the wombat's uniquely evolved gastrointestinal system — specifically, the variable stiffness and differential contraction patterns of the distal intestine — as the mechanism responsible for their rectangular droppings, correcting a previous hypothesis suggesting a square-shaped anus.

The associated popular science framing was inevitably irresistible. Wombats are among the world's most charismatic marsupials, and the fact that they are the only known species to produce cube-shaped feces had been the subject of genuine scientific puzzlement for decades. The confluence of the word "cube" with poop, wombats, and "we finally know," produced predictable virality.

Madison Dapcevich, writing for Snopes, investigated the claim thoroughly by 2021 fact-check standards — actually reading the primary paper and summarizing its findings in unusual depth — and confirmed the rating as True. The January 2021 study in *Soft Matter* by an international team including University of Tasmania wildlife ecologist Scott Carver and Georgia Tech postdoctoral fellow Patricia Yang presented the definitive mechanistic account.

The stakes of the story extend, however, considerably beyond the comedy of intestinal geometry. This is a case where the viral claim is correct, the Snopes treatment is actually better than average, and yet significant dimensions of theoretical and applied interest remain below the surface.


## 2. What's Actually True

The facts, as reported by Snopes, are accurate and well-summarized. Wombats — specifically bare-nosed wombats (*Vombatus ursinus*) — are the world's only known producers of cubic feces. The first documented description of this phenomenon was recorded in 1960 by Tasmanian zoologist Eric Guiler, who described wombat droppings as having a "characteristic rectangular shape" in a published zoological account. The phenomenon had been noted by naturalists and wildlife workers since European settlement of Australia but received intermittent rather than sustained scientific attention until the 2010s.

The 2021 paper by Yang et al. in *Soft Matter* — Yang, P.-Y., Lee, A.B., Chan, M., Kowalski, M., Moore, K., Woodruff, C., Tajiki, A., Kleinteich, T., Bhatt, T., Schaffer, D.J., & Hu, D.L. (2021). Intestines of non-uniform stiffness mold the corners of wombat feces. *Soft Matter*, 17, 475–488 — constitutes the most rigorous examination of the mechanism to date. Its key findings are accurately reported by Snopes:

The wombat's colon is not uniform in its mechanical properties. Its intestinal wall varies between stiff (rigid) and soft (elastic) regions, particularly in the distal colon (the final segment before the rectum). This variability means that when feces are propelled through the distal colon by peristaltic contractions, the differential stiffness creates non-uniform deformation forces. Regions of greater stiffness resist expansion and create edges; regions of greater elasticity distend and create faces. The mathematics of this interaction — modeled using elasticity equations and validated with physical soft-material simulations — produces a square cross-section as the equilibrium geometry.

The sphincter, as confirmed by CT scanning, is round. The squareness is entirely a product of intestinal mechanics, not anal geometry. The feces are essentially "molded" in the final intestinal segment rather than extruded through a square orifice.

Snopes also correctly reports the ecological function proposed for the cubic shape: wombats use their droppings as olfactory communication markers, depositing them in large latrine piles near burrow entrances, rock formations, and other territorial landmarks. The cubic shape reduces rollability — a square cross-section has significantly lower propensity to roll on irregular terrain than a cylindrical one — which is thought to help maintain stable latrine sites for reliable communication.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty: Soft-Matter Geometry, Evolutionary Morphogenesis, and Biomimetic Manufacturing

The wombat cubic feces story is exceptional in the scientific literature for reasons that go well beyond its ability to generate headlines. It represents a genuinely novel intersection of evolutionary biology, soft-matter physics, and pattern formation theory, and the Yang et al. (2021) paper in *Soft Matter* is more theoretically significant than popular coverage — including the Snopes article — typically acknowledges.

**The rarity of cubic shapes in biology.** The paper explicitly enters a conversation in physics called pattern formation theory, which studies how ordered shapes and structures arise spontaneously in physical and biological systems. As Yang et al. note, squares, cubes, and straight edges are extremely rare in nature compared to cylinders, spheres, and irregular forms. This is because biological tissues are predominantly elastic, and elastic pressurized containers (like intestines, blood vessels, eggs, and seed pods) overwhelmingly produce spherical or cylindrical equilibrium geometries when inflated or filled. The cube is a shape that, in the absence of deliberate engineering, almost never arises from natural physical processes.

The discovery that wombat intestinal mechanics *do* produce a cube — through a specific combination of non-uniform stiffness and rhythmic, differential contractions — is therefore not just biologically interesting but mathematically significant. It represents a natural system that has co-opted what physicists call elastic instability mechanisms to produce a geometry that is deeply counter-intuitive from the perspective of symmetric pressure-equilibration physics. Published simulation work accompanying the Yang et al. paper demonstrated that the specific pattern of stiffness variation in the wombat distal colon — soft zone alternating with stiff zone at approximately two-to-one intervals along the circumference — is the critical parameter. Small deviations from this ratio in the models produced non-cubic geometries, suggesting that the wombat intestine represents a finely tuned evolutionary solution to a specific geometric engineering problem (Yang et al., 2021, *Soft Matter*, 17, 475–488).

**Evolutionary origins and the wombat's digestive context.** The cubic feces mechanism evolved within a broader digestive specialization that Snopes notes accurately but does not fully contextualize. Wombats have among the longest digestion times of any mammal: up to 14 days to fully process food, compared to 24–48 hours in humans (Barboza, P.S., 1993, "Digestive strategies of wombats: feed intake, fiber digestion, and digesta passage in two grazing marsupials with hindgut fermentation," *Physiological Zoology*, 66(6), 983–999). This extraordinary retention time is an adaptation to their extremely dry, low-nutrition food supply — predominantly dry grasses and sedges in semi-arid southeastern Australian habitats where wombats extract maximal water and nutritional content from material that most herbivores would find insufficient.

The combination of long intestinal transit time and the specific intestinal geometry of the distal colon creates the physical context in which cubic shaping becomes possible: material that enters the terminal region is already dense, compressed, and semi-rigid, providing the material properties necessary for differential stiffness contractions to shape it geometrically. Yang et al.'s mathematical models specifically require that the material being molded have intermediate stiffness — neither too liquid (which would simply deform without retaining shape) nor too rigid (which would crack before shaping). The wombat's famous water-extraction efficiency is precisely what delivers material to the distal colon at this optimal consistency.

**Scent communication and territorial ecology.** The evolutionary function of the cubic shape matters for understanding whether this is an adaptation (a trait shaped by selection for the cubic geometry specifically) or an exaptation (a geometry that emerged from digestive mechanics and was then exploited for ecological function). This distinction is not resolved in the Yang et al. paper. The paper identifies the mechanism but acknowledges uncertainty about whether the intestinal geometry evolved specifically to produce cubes for territorial communication, or whether the intestinal geometry evolved primarily for other reasons (perhaps related to peristaltic efficiency in a long, narrow colon) and the territorial utility of cubic feces was a downstream benefit.

Research on wombat social ecology provides relevant context. Wombats are primarily nocturnal and solitary, with individual home ranges that overlap partially with conspecifics but are maintained through olfactory communication rather than direct territorial defense (McIlroy, J.C., 1973, "Some effects of hunter interference on the behaviour of wombats, *Vombatus ursinus*, in the Snowy Mountains area of New South Wales," *Australian Wildlife Research*, 1(1), 103–114). The latrine system serves both self-advertising and route-marking functions. The stability of cubic versus cylindrical droppings on sloped terrain, near burrow entrances, and on log surfaces — all common wombat latrine locations — has not been systematically quantified in the field literature, though it is a natural follow-up question that the Yang et al. paper's speculative passages implicitly invite.

**Biomimetic applications.** The paper's authors, particularly Patricia Yang and David Hu at Georgia Tech — both of whom have backgrounds in applied soft-matter physics — explicitly position the finding as potentially relevant to biomimetic manufacturing. Their proposed applications include soft-material extrusion for industrial processes, soft robotics, and pharmaceutical tablet manufacturing, where geometric precision in non-rigid materials is a significant engineering challenge. This is not merely speculative: the specific insight that non-uniform wall stiffness in a cylindrical channel can mold extruded material into non-cylindrical cross-sections has direct analogs in polymer and food-processing extrusion technology, where die shape is the current state-of-the-art shaping mechanism.

Whether wombat intestinal mechanics will actually influence industrial process design remains to be seen, but the conceptual contribution — that differential material stiffness within a channel can controllably produce geometrically precise shapes without rigid die constraints — is a novel addition to the engineering design vocabulary.


## 4. The EARC Assessment

**Empirical status.** Confirmed. The finding that wombat intestinal mechanics produce cubic feces through differential stiffness contractions is reported in a peer-reviewed paper in a well-regarded materials science journal (*Soft Matter* is published by the Royal Society of Chemistry, ISI impact factor consistently in the mid-range of physical chemistry/materials science). CT scanning of three bare-nosed wombat cadavers confirmed round sphincters. Computer simulations and physical soft-material models validated the proposed mechanism. The earlier observation by Scott Carver during mange disease research provided the initial cadaveric evidence that prompted the formal study.

**Adequacy.** The Snopes article is unusually substantive for its genre — it actually cites the paper, names the authors, and explains the mechanism in reasonable detail. This is above-average engagement with primary scientific literature for popular fact-checking. However, it does not engage with the theoretical novelty (pattern formation), the evolutionary biology context (the digestive adaptations that create the correct fecal material stiffness), or the biomimetic applications that the authors themselves explicitly propose.

**Reanalysis required.** None. The Snopes verdict is correct and well-supported within its scope.

**Contestation.** None of significance. No alternative explanation for wombat cubic feces has been advanced since the Yang et al. (2021) paper, and the prior hypothesis (square anus) was directly refuted by CT scan evidence in the same study.

**Gap assigned:** Depth-expandable. The verdict is correct and the Snopes treatment is better than average. The gap is the under-explored theoretical significance of the geometrical finding within pattern formation physics, the poorly contextualized digestive biology that enables the mechanism, and the genuine (if speculative) biomimetic and manufacturing implications the paper's authors raise.


## 5. The Broader Stakes

The wombat cubic poop story occupies an unusual position in the science communication landscape: it is both genuinely funny and genuinely scientifically important, and it achieved viral distribution primarily through its humor while its scientific novelty went largely unremarked. This is a pattern worth naming, because it reflects a general tendency in science communication to let the spectacle carry stories that contain real theoretical value.

The theoretical value here is non-trivial. The Yang et al. (2021) paper demonstrates that a biological soft-tissue system has evolved to exploit elastic instability for geometric shape production — a phenomenon previously known primarily from physics and mathematics but not well-documented in evolved biological contexts. This is an addition to the taxonomy of shape-generating mechanisms that extends from Turing's reaction-diffusion systems to the mechanical buckling patterns in plant morphogenesis. The wombat intestine represents a new point on this map: shape production through differential-stiffness mold geometry rather than chemical gradient or mechanical buckling.

The conservation dimension is also worth noting. Bare-nosed wombats (*Vombatus ursinus*), while not currently listed as threatened, exist in the context of a broader Australian marsupial fauna under significant pressure from habitat loss, predation by introduced species, and disease — common wombat mange, caused by the sarcoptic mite *Sarcoptes scabiei*, is a severe and widespread welfare problem to which Scott Carver himself has devoted significant research attention. Understanding the digestive physiology and territorial ecology of wombats in detail — including the mechanism and function of their cubic scat — is part of the foundational biological knowledge necessary for informed conservation and captive management.

Finally, the story is a useful reminder that zoological knowledge often remains incomplete in unexpected places. Wombat scent communication through latrines is well-documented at the observational level, but quantitative studies of the territorial behavior it mediates, the information content of wombat scat chemical profiles, and the evolutionary game theory of wombat territorial overlap remain areas with significant gaps. The cubic feces story drew global attention to a corner of behavioral ecology that deserves more systematic study.


## 6. What This Entry Adds

This Truth Vault entry affirms the Snopes True verdict — the 2021 *Soft Matter* paper does provide a definitive mechanistic account of wombat cubic feces production — while expanding the analysis in three directions:

First, a theoretical framing of the finding within pattern formation physics, including an explanation of why cubic shapes are counter-intuitive in biological systems and what it means, mathematically, for a biological tissue to have evolved to produce this geometry through differential stiffness mechanics.

Second, a deeper account of the digestive biology context — the extraordinary transit time and water extraction efficiency of wombat digestion — that explains why the fecal material arrives at the distal colon in precisely the mechanical state necessary for cubic shaping.

Third, a more substantive engagement with both the evolutionary question (adaptation vs. exaptation) and the biomimetic applications proposed by the paper's authors at Georgia Tech, which point toward genuine industrial relevance in soft-material extrusion design.

The verdict is True. And the story behind it is wilder — and more theoretically rich — than the punchline suggests.
