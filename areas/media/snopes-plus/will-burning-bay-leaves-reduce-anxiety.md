# Burning bay leaves reduces anxiety and stress


## 1. The Claim

A widely circulated wellness claim holds that burning dried bay leaves — most commonly *Laurus nobilis*, the culinary bay or sweet bay — is an effective home remedy for reducing anxiety and stress. Proponents describe the practice as a form of domestic aromatherapy: the leaf is lit, allowed to smoulder, and the resulting smoke is said to contain compounds that produce measurable calming effects within minutes. The claim typically appears on wellness blogs, natural-living social media accounts, and alternative health platforms, often prefaced by some variant of the phrase "science says burning a bay leaf for ten minutes and inhaling the smoke will calm you down."

The claim has circulated in particularly vigorous form since roughly 2014 and takes several specific shapes. In some versions, it focuses on a compound called linalool, describing it as the primary anxiolytic agent released when the leaf burns. In others, the active ingredient is variously identified as eugenol, α-pinene, or an unspecified ensemble of volatile organic compounds. Many versions cite a specific experiment performed at a university, often described as "researchers at SUNY (the State University of New York) Albany" without further bibliographic detail. In other tellings, the attributed institution shifts to unnamed "German researchers" or "a team of scientists," indicating that the specific scholarly provenance has been largely lost or was never firmly attached to begin with.

The therapeutic context offered varies as well. In more modest framing, burning bay leaves is presented as a mild, pleasant complement to existing stress management practices — a way to create a calming atmosphere without pharmaceutical intervention. In stronger versions, the claim implies that the practice provides benefit comparable to over-the-counter anxiolytic preparations or can substitute for medically supervised anxiety management. It is the stronger versions that raise the most significant concerns about accuracy and potential harm, particularly for users who might delay seeking professional support for anxiety disorders.

The practice has an extremely long history. Bay leaves have been used in ritual and medicinal contexts across Mediterranean and Mesoamerican cultures for millennia. The Oracle at Delphi was associated with laurel, and classical writers including Dioscorides and Pliny the Elder described medicinal applications of bay. This deep historical lineage is routinely invoked in contemporary wellness content to suggest that the claim has been validated by centuries of accumulated human experience — a form of argumentum ad antiquitatem that surfaces repeatedly in wellness marketing.

The claim is not dismissed out of hand at every margin. Bay laurel essential oil has a genuine pharmacological research literature, modest in scope but real, and some compounds it contains do show measurable biological activity in controlled settings. The serious evaluative question is whether any of this laboratory work translates to meaningful effects from the practice being advocated.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The phytochemistry of Laurus nobilis.** Bay laurel leaves contain a complex mixture of volatile aromatic compounds that are released in greater concentrations when the plant material is heated or burned. Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry analyses of bay laurel essential oil have consistently identified 1,8-cineole (eucalyptol) as the dominant compound, typically comprising 35–50% of the essential oil, along with significant proportions of α-terpinyl acetate, sabinene, linalool, α-pinene, β-pinene, and eugenol. Compositional variation occurs across cultivar, geographical origin, and time of harvest, but the broad volatile profile is well characterised. Senatore, Napolitano, and Ozcan (2004, published in *Flavour and Fragrance Journal*) provided an authoritative compositional analysis of leaf essential oils across five different geographic sources and documented substantial variation in the ratio of major constituents while confirming the consistency of the core compound profile.

**Laboratory findings on anxiolytic activity.** Several of the compounds present in bay laurel essential oil have been studied in animal models for effects on the central nervous system, and some positive findings have been reported at the individual compound level. Linck, da Silva, Figueiró, Caramão, Morrone, and Elisabetsky (2009, *Pharmacology Biochemistry and Behavior*, vol. 92, pages 361–366) investigated inhaled linalool at doses of 1–3% v/v in a closed chamber and observed significantly reduced anxiety-like behaviour in mice in the elevated plus maze paradigm, compared with vehicle-exposed controls. The effect was naloxone-reversible, suggesting involvement of the opioid system. This is a real and cited piece of evidence that linalool has measurable CNS action under controlled conditions.

Separately, Brum, Figueiró, Pedersen-de-Masi, Bentes, Schröder, and Elisabetsky (2020, *Phytomedicine*, vol. 77) examined linalool pharmacodynamics and proposed that the compound interacts with the GABAergic system in ways that parallel benzodiazepine action at high enough concentrations. Eucalyptol (1,8-cineole), the dominant bay laurel volatile, has also been studied: Bastos, de Moura Linck, Fernandes, de Lima, Figueiró, Zuanazzi, and Elisabetsky (2011, *Phytomedicine*, vol. 18, pages 303–307) demonstrated sedative and anxiolytic-like effects in rodent models, attributing the mechanism in part to interaction with adenosine A1 receptors.

**The translation problem: dose and delivery.** The critical flaw in the popular claim is the chasm between these laboratory findings and the actual dosimetry of burning a bay leaf in a domestic room. The Linck et al. (2009) study used concentrations of 1–3% linalool by volume in a small enclosed chamber for sustained exposure. A single dried bay leaf (*Laurus nobilis*) weighs approximately 0.5–1.5 grams when fully dried. Based on the essential oil yield of bay laurel (approximately 0.5–1.0% of dry leaf mass by steam distillation, as documented by Sangun, Aydin, Timur, Karadeniz, Caliskan, and Ozkan, 2007, in the *Journal of the Chilean Chemical Society*), one dried leaf contains roughly 5–15 milligrams of essential oil in total. When combusted — rather than diffused — a proportion of these volatile compounds are destroyed by the heat rather than aerosolised intact. The smoke that results contains combustion products alongside a small residuum of partially intact volatiles, distributed across the volume of a typical room (roughly 40–70 cubic metres). The resulting concentration of any single bioactive compound in inhaled air is orders of magnitude below the concentrations used in rodent chamber studies, making a meaningful pharmacological dose pharmacokinetically implausible under normal circumstances.

**What is actually producing the calming effect.** The subjective calming effect widely reported by people who burn bay leaves is most parsimoniously explained by mechanisms that have nothing to do with the phytochemicals in the smoke. Kabat-Zinn (1990, published by Delacorte Press as *Full Catastrophe Living*) and an extensive subsequent literature on mindfulness and controlled breathing have established that slow, diaphragmatic breathing reduces sympathetic nervous system activation, lowers heart rate and cortisol levels, and decreases subjective anxiety regardless of the atmosphere in which it is performed. The practice of burning a leaf and sitting quietly to observe it — as most online tutorials prescribe — involves exactly this slow, deliberate breathing, combined with a focused attentional task. Chen, Kung, Tseng, Lan, and Chen (2017, *Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine*, vol. 23, pages 617–621) found that pleasant scent conditions enhanced participants' self-reported relaxation relative to odourless controls regardless of the pharmacological profile of the scent, consistent with a hedonic expectancy account rather than a specific pharmacological one. The leaf is, in this reading, a ritual prop that scaffolds a breathing and attention practice that has independent well-documented efficacy.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The aromatherapy halo effect and ritual relaxation conflation.** The most claim-specific mechanism underlying belief in bay leaf smoke as an anxiolytic is what might be termed the aromatherapy halo effect: the widespread cultural assumption, heavily reinforced by wellness marketing, that inhaling pleasant natural scents produces pharmacologically meaningful therapeutic effects. This halo applies broadly to lavender, chamomile, sandalwood, and other botanicals, and it transfers readily to bay leaf given its warm, distinctively aromatic profile. When a person burns a bay leaf and feels calmer, the causal attribution defaults to the smell rather than to the breathing pattern, the cessation of other stimulating activity, the reduced visual stimulation of watching a smouldering leaf, or the expectation of calm itself. The mechanics of what is actually happening — a composite psychophysiological shift induced by a quiet, ritualistic pause — are invisible to introspection in a way that the smell is not, making misattribution highly predictable.

**Genuine partial accuracy as credibility anchor.** The claim does not circulate in a vacuum of complete falsehood. The genuine laboratory evidence showing that linalool and eucalyptol have CNS activity in rodent models provides readers who encounter fragment-citations with a reason to believe the broad thesis. The phrase "researchers have found that bay leaves contain compounds that affect the nervous system" is defensible if stated carefully, and versions of this appear in popular treatments. Readers absorb the affirmative finding and do not simultaneously grapple with the dose-translation problem, which requires scientific background to identify and is rarely mentioned in accessible sources.

**Deep historical lineage.** The association of bay laurel with healing, prophecy, and altered consciousness stretches back to classical antiquity. The Delphic Oracle burning laurel, athletes and scholars being crowned with laurel, the widespread use of bay in Renaissance and early modern herbalism — these deep cultural associations prime contemporary readers to view the medicinal use of bay as established rather than speculative. Historical use is not evidence of pharmacological efficacy, but it functions as cognitive evidence that the claim is not absurd, lowering the threshold of critical scrutiny.

**Social media incentive structures.** The claim that you can reduce anxiety by burning an inexpensive kitchen herb already in your pantry presses several simultaneously appealing buttons: it is cheap, safe, natural, ancient, and backed (in the common telling) by scientific research. This combination is extremely shareable, particularly in wellness communities where pharmaceutical alternatives may be viewed with scepticism. The claim's popularity is less a reflection of its evidentiary quality than of its perfect alignment with the preferences and aesthetic preferences of its primary distribution channels — Instagram, Pinterest, wellness TikTok — which reward simplicity, positivity, and actionability over nuance.


## 4. Verdict

**Exaggerated — a real but narrow pharmacological finding is overstated into a household remedy.** Some volatile compounds in *Laurus nobilis* essential oil, particularly linalool and 1,8-cineole, have demonstrated dose-dependent anxiolytic-like activity in rodent models under controlled inhalation conditions. This is genuine laboratory evidence worth acknowledging. The claim fails when it asserts or implies that burning a dried bay leaf in a domestic setting produces meaningful anxiolytic effects in humans: the dose delivered by smouldering one dried leaf across a standard room is pharmacologically negligible relative to effective doses in animal models, and the subjective relaxation reliably reported by participants is fully explained by the well-documented psychophysiological effects of slow, mindful breathing during a quiet ritual pause.

There is no credible human clinical trial demonstrating that the smoke from burning bay leaves reduces anxiety by a measurable amount beyond what would be expected from the placebo/ritual effect. Presented to a clinical psychologist, the practice would be seen as a low-risk relaxation ritual, not a pharmacotherapy. Describing it as scientifically validated anxiety reduction misrepresents both the laboratory evidence and what that evidence can support.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The bay leaf claim represents a pattern that is ubiquitous in natural-remedy spaces: a genuine phytochemical finding from essential oil or extract research is conflated with the effects of burning, inhaling, or consuming the raw plant at domestic doses. Essential oil research is conducted with purified, concentrated compounds or highly concentrated extracts — a mL of essential oil represents the volatile content of a far larger quantity of plant material than any realistic domestic use. The step from "this purified compound at high concentration shows biological activity in a mouse" to "putting a bit of this plant near you will make you feel better in the same way" skips multiple inferential rungs, none of which have been formally demonstrated.

This conflation has real-world consequences that vary in severity. For mild anxiety related to everyday stress, engaging in a pleasant, quiet ritual is genuinely beneficial on its own terms, and the misattribution is more of an intellectual error than a harm. The risk increases when people with clinically significant anxiety disorders substitute this practice for evidence-based treatments — cognitive behavioural therapy, pharmacotherapy, or structured mindfulness-based interventions with clinical trial evidence behind them. Anxiety disorders are undertreated in most countries, and the appeal of a natural, accessible, cost-free intervention makes the bay leaf claim particularly likely to be adopted by exactly the population for whom delay of effective treatment is most costly.

The scientific literature on plant aromatics and CNS effects is a legitimate and developing field. Buchbauer, Jirovetz, Jäger, Dietrich, and Plank (1991, *Pharmaceutical Research*, vol. 8, pages 1000–1001) reported differential CNS-modulating effects of inhaled essential oil compounds in mouse models, establishing an early framework for the field. More recent work has refined mechanistic proposals. The problem is not that this research fails to show any effects — some effects are real — but that the research is almost never conducted at doses and delivery routes comparable to domestic aromatherapy practice, making translation to "burn this leaf for ten minutes" claims methodologically unjustified. The wellness information ecosystem consistently fails to make or maintain this distinction.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim has not been formally addressed by Snopes, PolitiFact, or similar dedicated fact-check organisations as of the time of this writing. It exists in the large and poorly mapped territory of wellness misinformation that conventional fact-checking organisations do not systematically cover, partly because the claim is not tied to a specific newsworthy event and partly because it occupies the ambiguous grey zone between straightforward falsehood (which generates a clean verdict) and accurate-but-overstated findings (which require longer exposition than most fact-check formats allow).

The most useful evaluative approach for this type of claim is a three-step dosimetric audit: (1) identify the specific bioactive compound(s) cited; (2) locate the primary research supporting activity claims and record the doses and delivery routes used; (3) estimate the dose and delivery route in the practice being advocated and compare. For the bay leaf claim, this exercise immediately reveals the several-orders-of-magnitude gap between laboratory and kitchen, which collapses the strong version of the claim. A secondary step — checking for human clinical trial evidence, which in this case does not exist — confirms the verdict.

Media literacy educators treating this claim should emphasise the distinction between "a compound in X does Y in a laboratory" and "using X in a common domestic way does Y to humans." This distinction, simple to state, requires explicit instruction to apply routinely, because it runs counter to the intuitive logic that if something contains an active ingredient, exposure to the thing will deliver the active ingredient. That intuition is systematically wrong whenever dose, bioavailability, or delivery route differ substantially between research conditions and real-world use — which is the majority of natural-remedy contexts.
