# Women in ancient Rome used turpentine and urine as cosmetics or cleaning agents


## 1. The Claim

The claim arrives in several related but distinct forms across internet listicles, "weird history" social media posts, natural health commentary, and popular history podcasts. In its most dramatic framing, Roman women regularly drank turpentine so that their urine would smell pleasantly of roses—a cosmetic strategy allegedly prized for impressing suitors or indicating bodily refinement. In softer versions, urine was a routine and accepted ingredient in Roman cosmetic routines: used to whiten teeth, cleanse and exfoliate skin, and produce the gleaming white togas associated with upper-class Roman dress. Turpentine, derived from pine resin, appears alongside urine in many popular accounts as another counterintuitive ingredient in Roman grooming and cleaning—a resinous substance applied externally or consumed internally as part of a pre-modern beauty toolkit.

These claims carry the pleasing structure of weird-but-true historical facts: they violate modern sensibilities about hygiene and bodily products, invoke the strangeness of ancient life, and suggest that cosmetic vanity is a constant across centuries even while its specific expressions are radically different. The "Roman women drank turpentine" version gained particular traction online through natural health entrepreneur Joseph Mercola's commentary on urine analysis, which included the observation: "If you're a woman from ancient Rome and your urine smells like roses, you've probably been drinking turpentine." This was subsequently repeated by websites including Neatorama, Reddit discussion boards, and numerous "strange historical facts" compilations.

The fuller claim encompasses both cleaning and cosmetic dimensions. On the cleaning side, accounts describe Roman laundries—the *fullonicae*—as places where workers trod clothing in vats of collected urine, using the ammonia content to dissolve grease and whiten fabric. On the cosmetic side, accounts describe Roman women applying aged urine to their faces and using it as a mouthwash, alongside more dramatic declarations about turpentine ingestion to perfume bodily secretions. The combination produces a picture of ancient Roman beauty culture as simultaneously sophisticated (extensive and systematic) and alien (built on substances that contemporary Westerners regard as waste products).

The claim travels through contexts as varied as popular history books, chemistry education examples, feminist historical writing about the lengths to which women endure discomfort for beauty standards, alternative medicine commentary treating pre-modern practices as lost natural health wisdom, and straightforward curiosity-driven internet browsing. The specific combination of urine and turpentine within a single claim, however, conflates practices with very different evidentiary footings—a conflation that obscures which elements are well-documented Roman practice and which are later embellishment or simple misattribution.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Urine in Roman cleaning: robustly documented.** The single most thoroughly evidenced element of the entire cluster of claims concerns the *fullonicae*, the professional laundries that were a standard feature of Roman urban life from at least the Republican period through the late Empire. Archaeological evidence from Pompeii is particularly rich: excavated laundry establishments at this site reveal large stone vats, treading areas, and drainage systems consistent with the large-scale processing of clothing and textiles using urine as a primary agent.

Ancient literary sources corroborate the physical evidence. Pliny the Elder's *Naturalis Historia* contains multiple references to urine's cleansing properties. The Roman satirist Catullus famously mocked a rival for whitening his teeth with Spanish urine, treating the practice as disgusting but recognizable. The poet Martial made sardonic references to urine's commercial value in urban Rome—a value confirmed by the emperor Vespasian, who reportedly levied a tax on urine collection from public facilities (*vectigal urinae*), giving rise to the still-used phrase *pecunia non olet* (money does not smell). The ammonia produced by the decomposition of urea in aged urine functions as a surfactant capable of breaking down fats and greases; this is not a folk belief but basic chemistry, and it made collected urine a genuinely useful industrial cleaning agent before synthetic detergents became available.

The fuller process of Roman fulling combined urine with wooden ash (*cinis*), which provided additional alkalinity, and required workers to tread heavily on soaking garments to mechanically work the cleaning agents through the fibers. This was hard, unpleasant labor, and the fullers (*fullones*) occupied a low social status despite the economic importance of their work. The cleaning of woolen cloth—the predominant textile in Roman dress—required precisely the kind of alkaline chemical action that urine provided. This is well-established Roman social and economic history, not myth.

**Urine in Roman cosmetics: documented but more nuanced.** The use of urine in oral hygiene and skin care is attested in ancient sources, though the evidence base is more fragmentary and context-specific than popular accounts suggest. Pliny the Elder discusses the use of urine as a topical treatment for various skin conditions, in a medical rather than specifically cosmetic context, and notes that some physicians recommended it for wound care and dermatological complaints. Roman medical writers including Galen and Celsus describe urine in contexts that overlap with what modern readers might call cosmetic use: application to skin for particular effects, incorporation into preparations for teeth and gums.

The teeth-whitening use is specifically attested by Catullus as described above, and the chemical basis is sound — the alkaline ammonium compounds in aged urine have mild bleaching and abrasive properties that would produce some whitening effect on dental enamel. Whether this was a widespread practice among Roman women specifically, as opposed to a general folk remedy known to Roman society as a whole, is harder to establish from surviving sources. Catullus frames it as remarkable enough to be insulting rather than as routine female behavior.

**Turpentine: the pharmacological reality and the historical gap.** The claim that Roman women drank turpentine to perfume their urine occupies fundamentally different evidentiary territory from the urine claims. The pharmacological premise is accurate: turpentine does impart a scent resembling violets (not roses, as many popular accounts incorrectly state) to urine, a fact documented by physicians for centuries. The 1749 *Pharmacopoeia Universalis* explicitly notes this effect, and it is mentioned in medical literature from the medieval period onward. First-century Roman physicians were aware of turpentine's medicinal properties and use as a diuretic, and were therefore well-positioned to have observed its effect on urine odor.

The critical gap, as Snopes's 2017 investigation established, is between pharmacological knowledge and documented social practice. No Roman source — not Pliny, not Ovid's *Ars Amatoria* (which is actually quite detailed about Roman beauty practices), not the medical compilations of Galen or Celsus, not Martial or any other satirist who documented Roman social habits in detail — records Roman women regularly drinking turpentine to perfume their urine. This is an important negative finding, because these sources are rich in similar practices: they record lead-based face powder for skin lightening, crocodile dung face packs, ground horn for dental care, and numerous other practices that seem bizarre by modern standards. The silence about turpentine-drinking in these sources is not conclusive proof of absence (absence of evidence is not evidence of absence), but it does mean the claim cannot be presented as established historical fact.

The toxicology also complicates the picture. Turpentine is hepatotoxic, nephrotoxic, and capable of causing severe pulmonary damage when ingested. Even in the quantities that might perfume urine, regular consumption would carry substantial health risks. Roman physicians who knew of turpentine's properties also knew something of its toxic potential; the claim that women routinely drank it as a cosmetic practice, without any protective framing in Roman medical or satirical literature, seems historically implausible.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The claim's persistence depends on the fact that its documented component is genuinely strange enough to lower skepticism about the undocumented component. Once a reader learns that Roman laundries really did use vats of urine to clean clothing — a fact available in any reputable ancient history textbook and confirmed by Pompeian archaeology — the additional claim that Roman women drank turpentine for cosmetic purposes seems barely more improbable. The first fact primes acceptance of the second.

This is a specific instance of a broader pattern in "weird history" content: anchoring a well-documented but surprising truth alongside a much less documented or entirely undocumented claim, relying on the veridical strange thing to authenticate the merely rumored strange thing. The effective propaganda move is to say "And you thought Roman urine-laundries were weird." The reader, having accepted the urine laundry fact, has already mentally downgraded their skepticism threshold. The turpentine claim slides in under that lowered threshold.

The roses/violets error in most popular accounts is additionally revealing. If the claim derived from careful reading of historical pharmacological sources, it would say violets — because that is what the sources record. The consistent substitution of roses, which is both more romantically resonant and less pharmacologically specific, suggests the claim is being transmitted through popular channels that are distant from any primary or even secondary historical source. The roses formulation is the story-telling version, not the scholarly version.

There is also a strand of feminist historical commentary that uses Roman beauty practices — including the more extreme claims about turpentine — as evidence of the lengths to which women across history have been coerced into uncomfortable and dangerous beauty routines. This is a defensible and important theme in gender history, and genuine Roman beauty practices (lead-based cosmetics, hair-dye formulas using toxic compounds) do support it powerfully. When the turpentine claim is recruited into this argument, the political purpose partly substitutes for source-checking. The goal of demonstrating historical beauty coercion is served better by colorful examples than by carefully distinguished facts and near-facts.

Natural health communities contribute a different motivation for propagating the claim: the treatment of ancient practices as repositories of wisdom lost to modern pharmaceutical medicine. In this framing, the Roman use of urine for skin care and oral hygiene represents insights vindicated by modern understanding of urea's properties, while turpentine represents an alternative medicine modality with ancient precedent. This motivation, again, is served by confident broad claims rather than careful evidential distinctions.


## 4. Verdict

**MIXTURE** — The claim is distinguishable by its component parts, and Snopes correctly rates it a "Mixture." The use of urine in Roman cleaning (*fullonicae*, textile processing) is one of the best-documented practices in Roman economic history, confirmed by multiple archaeological sites, ancient literary sources ranging from Pliny to Catullus, and basic chemistry. The use of urine in cosmetic and oral hygiene contexts is attested in Roman sources, though with less systematic documentation than the industrial cleaning applications.

The turpentine-drinking claim occupies a substantially different evidentiary position. The pharmacological effect on urine odor (producing violet-like rather than rose-like scent) was known to Roman and post-Roman physicians. However, no Roman literary, medical, or satirical source specifically documents Roman women regularly drinking turpentine as a cosmetic practice. The claim cannot be stated as established historical fact; it is at best an inference from pharmacological knowledge to practice, unsupported by direct textual evidence. The consistent roses/violets error in popular versions suggests the claim has travelled far from any historical source.

The compound claim — women in ancient Rome used turpentine and urine as cosmetics or cleaning agents — is therefore true in its urine-cleaning dimension, partially true and more thinly evidenced in its urine-cosmetics dimension, and undocumented in its turpentine-drinking dimension. Consumers of popular history should distinguish between these three quite different evidentiary footings rather than treating the compound claim as uniformly true or uniformly false.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Roman hygiene and cosmetics case illuminates a recurring challenge in popular history communication: the tendency to present heterogeneous evidentiary clusters as uniform facts. The *fullonicae* urine cleaning practice belongs to the category of well-documented and archaeologically confirmed ancient history. It is taught in undergraduate ancient history courses, discussed in serious scholarly works on Roman economics and urban life, and verifiable through primary sources available in translation. The turpentine-drinking claim, by contrast, belongs to the category of pharmacologically plausible but historically unattested folk attribution — the kind of thing that sounds like it could be true and might even have been true, but for which we lack documentary confirmation.

This distinction matters not only for historical accuracy but for understanding how ancient bodies and ancient practices are constructed in popular imagination. Roman cosmetics were genuinely varied, extensive, and often involved ingredients that modern readers find surprising: white lead (*cerussa*) as a skin foundation, red lead (*minium*) as a lip and cheek pigment, antimony-based kohl for eye makeup, crocodile dung face packs (mentioned by Galen), and numerous plant-based preparations. These documented practices are remarkable enough to bear the full weight of popular interest in ancient beauty culture; the addition of undocumented turpentine-drinking does not add to the picture and contributes misleading specificity.

Ancient Roman medicine was genuinely sophisticated in some areas and wildly mistaken in others. The systematic pharmacological works of Dioscorides (*De Materia Medica*) and Galen represent serious intellectual achievements that organized knowledge of hundreds of plant-derived and mineral-derived substances. Roman physicians' awareness of turpentine's diuretic properties and urinary odor effects fits within this tradition. Yet the transmission of pharmaceutical knowledge into cosmetic practice involved social, economic, and cultural mediations that popular accounts characteristically ignore, jumping directly from "known to medicine" to "practiced by women" without attending to the intermediate steps.

The Roman approach to bodily products and their uses also sits within a different symbolic economy of purity and pollution than the one dominant in modern Western hygiene culture. Douglas Turner's anthropological work on purity and pollution norms, and Keith Hopkins's sociological work on Roman social structure, both provide frameworks for understanding why urine could simultaneously be a waste product and a commercially valuable cleaning agent without the contradiction that contemporary sensibilities would feel. Roman material culture was deeply pragmatic in ways that structured perceptions of what was appropriately used for what purposes.

The urine economy of ancient Rome is, in retrospect, a reasonably efficient response to the problem of obtaining alkaline cleaning agents before industrial chemical synthesis. The *vectigal urinae* — the urine collection tax levied by Vespasian — is itself a fascinating case study in how Roman state infrastructure organized the conversion of what would otherwise be an environmental nuisance (human waste products from a city of approximately one million people) into an economically productive resource. The tax apparently caused social controversy, prompting Vespasian's famous response to his son Titus's objection — that money does not smell — which captured a genuine Roman pragmatism about the relationship between economic value and social stigma.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes addressed a specific formulation of this claim in 2017, authored by David Emery, rating it "Mixture." The treatment correctly distinguishes: the pharmacological claim (turpentine imparts violet-like scent to urine, known to first-century Rome) is confirmed as true; the factual error in popular versions (roses rather than violets) is noted; and the absence of documentary evidence for widespread Roman women's practice of drinking turpentine cosmetically is flagged as the critical undetermined element.

The Snopes treatment is notable for the care it takes to distinguish between pharmacological knowledge and social practice — a distinction that popular versions of the claim collapse entirely. The "What's Undetermined" category in their rating structure proves particularly apposite here: not everything unknown is false, and the turpentine-drinking claim sits appropriately in the space of "not proven by available sources" rather than being straightforwardly debunked.

What the Snopes treatment does not fully explore is the difference in evidentiary quality between the urine-cleaning claim (excellent evidence from archaeology and multiple independent literary sources) and the urine-cosmetics claim (moderate evidence from medical and satirical sources) and the turpentine claim proper (pharmacological basis confirmed, social practice not documented). The "Mixture" rating accurately captures the heterogeneous status of the compound claim but may not communicate to general readers that these components have very different degrees of historical support.

For media literacy educators, this case is valuable precisely because it involves a three-part compound claim with a sliding scale of evidential support. Teaching readers to ask "which part of this?" rather than simply "true or false?" develops more sophisticated engagement with historical claims — particularly claims about ancient practices where documentation is inherently uneven and the temptation to extrapolate from confirmed weirdness to unconfirmed weirdness is always present. The roses/violets error provides an additional useful teaching tool: if a claim contains a specific pharmacological detail that turns out to be consistently wrong in popular transmission, that error itself is evidence that the claim is travelling at distance from its sources, and all elements should be re-examined against primary or secondary historical scholarship.
