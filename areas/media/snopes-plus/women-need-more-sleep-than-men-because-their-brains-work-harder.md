# "Women Need More Sleep Than Men Because Their Brains Work Harder"

*A Truth Vault investigation into one of the internet's most persistent zombie claims — and the real science buried underneath it.*

---

## The Claim

The headline travels under various guises, but its core proposition is remarkably stable: *Women need more sleep than men because their brains work harder during the day.* Sometimes the number is specified — twenty minutes more, or an extra hour — and sometimes a prestigious institution is invoked, most often "researchers at Duke University" or "scientists at Loughborough University." The story has circulated in some form since at least 2010, with periodic resurgences in 2013, 2016, 2018, and again in 2022, each time accumulating new layers of distortion like sediment around a sunken ship.

The claim typically rests on three interlocking assertions:

1. **The authority claim:** A named sleep researcher — almost always Jim Horne, formerly of Loughborough University's Sleep Research Centre — is quoted as saying women *need* more sleep than men.
2. **The mechanism claim:** The reason women need more sleep is that their brains are "more complex" or "work harder" due to multitasking, and sleep is the brain's recovery period, so harder-working brains require more of it.
3. **The consequences claim:** A study, usually attributed to Duke University, found that women who don't get enough sleep suffer worse health consequences than men — elevated inflammation, depression, cardiovascular risk — proving that women's sleep deficit is medically dangerous.

Each of these assertions contains a fragment of something real. But the way they have been stitched together — quotes reattributed, findings conflated, caveats stripped, and a speculative mechanism elevated to established fact — produces something that is, in Snopes's careful assessment, "mostly false." We would add: it is a case study in how the internet's hunger for tidy narratives about gender differences can turn cautious science into confident mythology.

### The Provenance Trail

Tracing the viral claim to its origins requires navigating a tangle of secondary sources, each citing each other rather than primary research. The earliest identifiable catalyst is a 2012 *Daily Mail* article summarising comments Jim Horne made while promoting his book *Sleepfaring: A Journey Through the Science of Sleep* (originally published in 2006). In that book and in subsequent interviews, Horne discussed observations about sex differences in sleep duration — mainly that women *tend to sleep* slightly longer than men. He offered a speculative explanation involving multitasking and cortical recovery. The *Daily Mail* compressed this into a headline-friendly claim.

From there, aggregation sites picked it up. Crucially, at some point in the re-blogging chain, Horne's comments were mixed with a completely separate body of work: a 2008 Duke University press release summarising research by Edward Suarez on the differential health consequences of poor sleep quality in men and women. The Suarez research said nothing about how much sleep women *need*; it examined what happens when sleep is *disrupted*. But in the aggregation blender, the two became one story: women need more sleep (Horne) *because* poor sleep harms them more (Suarez), *because* their brains work harder from multitasking (Horne again, speculating).

By the time the compound claim reached Facebook and Instagram infographics, it had acquired the false sheen of a single, coherent scientific finding. Quotes from Horne were placed in Suarez's mouth and vice versa. The hedge "tend to take" became the imperative "need." And a hypothesis about multitasking became a stated neurological fact.

## What's Actually True

Underneath the viral distortion, there exists a legitimate body of research on sex and gender differences in sleep. It is more nuanced, more interesting, and more uncertain than the viral claim allows. Here is what the primary literature actually says.

### Women Do Sleep Slightly Longer — On Average

Jim Horne's own research, including a 1996 study of approximately 400 individuals, found that women slept somewhat longer than men on average. This observation is not unique to Horne. The epidemiological literature, reviewed comprehensively by Kenneth Lichstein in *Epidemiology of Sleep* (2004), consistently shows that women report slightly longer total sleep time (TST) across multiple populations and measurement methods. A large-scale study by Groeger and colleagues (2004), surveying roughly 2,000 British adults, found similar patterns: women and men reported comparable amounts of sleep, but women were more likely to report dissatisfaction with their sleep quality and more likely to report sleep-related difficulties.

These are population-level averages with enormous individual variation. The difference in duration is typically modest — on the order of ten to twenty minutes — and it is not at all clear that the difference reflects a biological *need* rather than social patterns, hormonal cycles, caretaking schedules, or differences in how men and women report subjective sleep quality on surveys.

This distinction — between what people *do* and what they *need* — is central to the misrepresentation at the heart of the viral claim. Horne himself was careful about this. In his academic writing and in *Sleepfaring*, he described the observation that women "tend to take" more sleep. He did not declare that women biologically require more sleep. The gap between those two statements is enormous, and filling it requires evidence that the viral claim simply does not provide.

### Sleep Deprivation May Affect Women Differently

The research most commonly attributed to "Duke University" in viral versions of the claim comes from Edward Suarez's work at the Duke University Medical Center, portions of which were summarised in a 2008 university press release. Suarez studied a sample of healthy men and women and measured associations between self-reported sleep quality and various biomarkers of health risk — including C-reactive protein (an inflammation marker), insulin resistance, and indicators of psychological distress.

His findings were genuinely interesting: among the women in the sample, poor sleep quality was associated with elevated inflammation markers, higher fasting insulin levels, and greater psychological distress. Among men, the same associations were weaker or absent. Suarez proposed that poor sleep might carry sex-specific cardiovascular and metabolic risks, with women potentially more vulnerable.

However — and this is the critical point the viral claim obliterates — Suarez's research did not study sleep *amount*. It studied sleep *quality*. It did not find that women need more sleep. It found that when sleep is poor (fragmented, non-restorative, fitful), women may experience greater physiological consequences. This is a claim about vulnerability to sleep disruption, not about optimal sleep duration. It is also a finding from a single research group studying a specific sample, one that has generated further research but not the kind of rock-solid consensus that would justify the confident factual tone of the viral claim.

### The Multitasking Hypothesis Is Speculative at Best

The viral claim's mechanism — that women's brains "work harder" because women are better multitaskers, and therefore need more sleep for cortical recovery — rests on two sub-claims, both problematic.

**First, that women multitask more or better than men.** This is one of those propositions that feels intuitively obvious to many people but has remarkably thin empirical support. David Hambrick, a cognitive scientist who has studied individual differences in cognitive abilities, stated in 2009 that he "could not find a single scientific report" demonstrating that women are superior multitaskers compared to men. The two most commonly cited studies on this question, both from 2013, produced mixed results. Timo Mäntylä's 2013 study found that men actually outperformed women on a laboratory multitasking paradigm, though this advantage was mediated by spatial ability — a finding that complicates rather than confirms any straightforward gender-multitasking link. Gijsbert Stoet and colleagues (2013) found some support for women performing better in a form of task-switching that was sequential rather than truly simultaneous, but the effect was modest and the researchers cautioned against over-interpretation. Neither study comes close to establishing that women's brains are systematically "working harder" during the day in a way that would mechanistically require more nocturnal recovery.

**Second, that multitasking creates a special need for sleep-based cortical recovery.** Horne's reasoning here draws on the established neuroscience of sleep as a restorative process for the cerebral cortex. This part is well-supported: sleep, particularly slow-wave sleep, does appear to serve cortical maintenance and recovery functions. What is not established is the specific causal chain that more daytime cognitive load of the multitasking variety translates into a need for more sleep. The relationship between cognitive effort during waking hours and sleep architecture is complex and not fully understood. There is some evidence that learning new tasks can increase slow-wave sleep activity in relevant brain regions (the "local sleep" hypothesis explored by Giulio Tononi and Chiara Cirelli), but this does not straightforwardly map to "multitaskers need more minutes of sleep." Horne's explanation was a hypothesis, offered in a popular book and in media interviews — not a conclusion drawn from experimental evidence.

### Hormonal and Biological Factors Are More Promising

Ironically, the most robust explanations for observed sex differences in sleep — the ones the viral claim ignores — involve hormonal and reproductive biology. Menstrual cycle fluctuations, pregnancy, postpartum hormonal changes, and menopause all affect sleep architecture in well-documented ways. Progesterone has mild sedative-like properties and fluctuates dramatically across the menstrual cycle and during pregnancy. Menopausal hot flashes disrupt sleep directly. These are concrete, measurable biological mechanisms — far more scientifically grounded than "multitasking makes your brain tired."

Additionally, there is growing evidence that sex differences in circadian rhythm regulation may contribute to observed differences in sleep timing and duration. Research on circadian phenotypes suggests that women may have slightly shorter intrinsic circadian periods than men, which could influence both sleep timing preferences and total sleep duration.

The real science of gendered sleep differences is, in short, a legitimately active and interesting area of research. It just doesn't support the tidy, flattering narrative that the internet prefers.

## Why People Believe This

The longevity and reach of this claim cannot be explained by the strength of the evidence behind it. It persists because it taps into deep currents in how we think about gender, science, and the relationship between effort and rest.

### It Flatters While Appearing Scientific

The claim works as a compliment disguised as a fact. Telling women that their brains "work harder" and therefore require more recovery is superficially validating. It takes the subjective experience that many women have — of feeling stretched thin, of juggling competing demands, of being exhausted — and provides a biological imprimatur. You're not tired because of structural inequality or unequal domestic labour distribution. You're tired because your brain is *more complex*. That's a seductive reframe: it transforms a social complaint into a biological identity, one you can be proud of rather than angry about.

This pattern — the biologisation of social experience — is a recurring feature of viral gender-science claims. It appears in stories about female intuition being "hard-wired," about women's brains being "built for empathy," about testosterone "explaining" male risk-taking. In each case, a real and complex social phenomenon is given a simple biological explanation that makes it seem natural, inevitable, and — crucially — not anyone's fault. The appeal is obvious. The epistemological cost is enormous.

### It Validates Existing Beliefs About Gender

Psychologists who study motivated reasoning have consistently shown that people are far more likely to accept, share, and remember claims that align with their pre-existing beliefs. The proposition that women multitask more effectively than men is widely believed, even though — as we have seen — the empirical evidence for it is weak. And the idea that women's brains are fundamentally different from men's in ways that manifest in everyday life has a long and complicated cultural history, one that includes both sexist pseudoscience and legitimate neuroscience findings about hormonal effects and brain connectivity patterns.

The viral claim slots neatly into this framework of belief. It doesn't need to be proven; it needs to *feel* right. And for many people — women who feel chronically under-rested, partners who observe different sleep patterns, and a general public primed by pop-neuroscience to expect dramatic brain-based gender differences — it feels very right indeed.

### The Architecture of Aggregation Amplifies Distortion

The claim's survival is also a product of how science journalism operates in the digital age. The original source material — Horne's interviews, Suarez's press release — was cautious and qualified. But each step in the aggregation chain stripped away qualifications. The *Daily Mail* simplified Horne. Aggregators simplified the *Daily Mail*. Facebook pages simplified the aggregators. Each iteration selected for the most shareable version: the boldest claim, the most surprising number, the most authoritative attribution. By the time the claim reached its widest audience, it bore almost no resemblance to the cautious, hedged original.

This is not unique to this claim. It is the standard dynamics of viral science misinformation: a legitimate finding, stripped of context, simplified past recognition, and amplified by platforms that reward engagement over accuracy. But this particular example is unusually instructive because the distortion happened across *multiple* separate sources. It wasn't just one study being oversimplified. It was two or three completely different research programmes being *merged* into a single narrative, with quotes shuffled between researchers and findings transposed from one study to another. It's not simplification. It's fabrication through recombination.

### The "Zombie Claim" Lifecycle

Media researchers have identified a class of viral stories they call "zombie claims" — assertions that are debunked, die down, and then resurface months or years later as if they had never been challenged. This sleep claim is a textbook example. Every two to three years, the cycle repeats: a new outlet publishes the claim, it goes viral, fact-checkers debunk it, the debunking reaches a fraction of the viral audience, and the claim goes dormant — until the next cycle.

The zombie claim lifecycle is sustained by several factors: the absence of effective correction mechanisms on social media, the fact that debunking articles rarely achieve the same reach as the original claims, and the emergence of new audiences who encounter the claim for the first time with each cycle. Each resurgence is also slightly mutated — new details added, old details altered — making it harder for automated fact-checking tools to flag as a repeat and giving it the appearance of freshness.

## Verdict

**Rating: Mostly False**

The viral claim that "women need more sleep than men because their brains work harder" is a composite fabrication assembled from misattributed quotations, conflated studies, and an unproven mechanism.

**What Jim Horne actually said** was that women *tend to take* slightly more sleep and that this *might* be related to greater use of the cortex during the day — a speculative hypothesis, not a confirmed finding. **What the Duke University research actually showed** was that poor sleep quality is associated with worse health biomarkers in women — a finding about sleep disruption vulnerability, not sleep duration requirements. **What the scientific literature actually says about multitasking** is that the popular belief in women's superior multitasking ability has negligible empirical support.

There *are* real sex differences in sleep. Women report longer sleep times on average, women report more sleep difficulties, and women's sleep is affected by hormonal fluctuations across the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and menopause. Sleep deprivation may indeed carry sex-specific health consequences, as Suarez's biomarker work suggests. These are legitimate areas of active research deserving of public attention.

But the viral version of this story — the neat, flattering, shareable version — is not a reflection of that research. It is a distortion of it, one that replaces real complexity with false clarity, swaps cautious observation for confident assertion, and confuses the gratifying with the true.

The claim is not *entirely* fabricated. It is not plucked from thin air. It is stitched together from real threads — real researchers, real studies, real observations — but stitched into a garment that none of those researchers would recognise or endorse. And that makes it, in some ways, more misleading than a pure fabrication would be, because it comes wrapped in the trappings of legitimate science.

## The Wider Picture

This case is worth examining beyond its specific claims because it illuminates several recurring patterns in how science travels — and mutates — through public discourse.

### The Misattribution Cascade

One of the most instructive features of this viral claim is the way it mixed up its sources. Horne's observations were attributed to Suarez, and Suarez's findings were attributed to Horne. Quotes were detached from their original speakers and reattached to whoever seemed most authoritative for the point being made. This is not mere sloppiness. It follows a logic: the claim needs both a mechanism (why would women need more sleep?) and a consequence (what happens if they don't get it?), and no single researcher provided both. So the aggregation process unconsciously performed an act of synthesis — combining two separate research programmes into one coherent story — that no peer-reviewed process had authorised.

This kind of misattribution cascade is common in viral science stories. A researcher makes a finding. A different researcher, in a different context, makes a different finding. A journalist or aggregator — often working under time pressure, without deep scientific literacy, and with a brief to produce shareable content — sees the two findings as complementary components of a single narrative. The combination is their own creation, but it is presented as established science. The researchers whose work was combined may be completely unaware that their names are being attached to claims they never made, about connections they never proposed.

### The Biologisation of the Social

The claim's appeal rests heavily on a move that is simultaneously flattering and reductive: it takes women's reported experience of exhaustion — a phenomenon with obvious social determinants (unequal distribution of domestic labour, caregiving responsibilities, the "second shift," workplace discrimination) — and reframes it as a biological characteristic. Women aren't tired because the world demands more of them without adequate support. They're tired because their brains are *literally different*.

This pattern is worth naming explicitly because it recurs constantly in popular science reporting about gender. The biologisation of social experience serves multiple rhetorical functions simultaneously. It validates individual experience ("you're right to be tired — it's biology"), depoliticises structural problems ("it's not about inequality — it's about neuroscience"), and naturalises gender differences in a way that forecloses the question of change ("women's brains just work differently"). Each of these functions is attractive to different audiences for different reasons, and their combination gives biologically framed gender claims an almost irresistible appeal.

None of this means that there are no biological sex differences in sleep, cognition, or neurology. There clearly are. The point is that the *selection* and *framing* of biological facts in public discourse is driven not by scientific completeness but by what stories we want to hear — and that the stories we most want to hear are often the ones that most need scrutiny.

### The Humility Gap in Science Communication

Horne's original statements, in his books and interviews, were characterised by appropriate hedging. He said women "tend to" sleep more. He offered multitasking as one "possible" explanation. He referenced his own data and acknowledged its limitations. These are the hallmarks of responsible scientific communication.

The problem is that hedging does not travel well. Each act of simplification, summarisation, or quotation strips away a hedge. "Women tend to take more sleep" becomes "women need more sleep." "One possible explanation" becomes "scientists have found." "A study of 400 people suggested" becomes "research has proven." The process is not driven by malice. It is driven by the structural incentives of media production: space is limited, attention is scarce, and uncertainty is boring.

This creates a systematic humility gap between what scientists say and what the public hears. And it is a gap that individual debunking cannot close, because it is generated not by individual bad actors but by the structure of the communication system itself. Addressing it would require changes to how science journalism is incentivised, how social media platforms handle claims of scientific authority, and how scientific institutions communicate with the public — changes that go far beyond correcting any single viral claim.

### What Gets Lost

Perhaps the most regrettable consequence of the viral claim's dominance over public understanding of gendered sleep differences is the displacement of genuinely interesting science. The hormonal influences on sleep architecture — the way progesterone modulates sleep spindle activity, the way menopausal vasomotor symptoms fragment sleep, the way pregnancy reshapes sleep staging — are fascinating topics with direct clinical relevance. The emerging research on sex differences in circadian regulation has implications for shift work policy, medication timing, and public health interventions. The epidemiological questions about why women report both more sleep and worse sleep quality simultaneously point to deep issues in how we measure subjective health experiences.

All of this gets crowded out by a flattering meme. The complex truth — which would require paragraphs of explanation, acknowledgement of uncertainty, and engagement with real biology rather than folk neuroscience — cannot compete with a shareable graphic that says your brain is special and you deserve a nap.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim has been evaluated by multiple fact-checking organisations, and their approaches reveal both the strengths and limitations of the fact-checking enterprise.

### Snopes's Approach

Snopes rated the claim "Mostly False" and published a detailed investigation tracing the provenance of the viral story. Their methodology was exemplary in several respects: they tracked down the original Horne interviews and books, they separated the Horne material from the Suarez material, and they identified the specific points of distortion — the shift from "tend to take" to "need," the conflation of sleep quality with sleep quantity, and the lack of evidence for the multitasking mechanism.

Snopes's investigation also highlighted a crucial structural feature of the claim: it is not a single falsehood but a *composite* of partial truths, misattributions, and unsupported connections. This makes it harder to debunk cleanly than a simple fabrication, because pointing to the things that are true (women do sleep somewhat more on average; poor sleep may be more harmful for women) can inadvertently seem to validate the overall narrative.

### The Mostly-False Dilemma

The "Mostly False" rating itself illustrates a recurring challenge in fact-checking. Binary true/false ratings are poorly suited to claims that contain genuine fragments of truth assembled into a misleading whole. "Mostly False" attempts to capture the nuance — but in the social media environment where the claim circulates, nuance is lethal. A "Mostly False" rating can be read by motivated believers as an acknowledgement that the claim is partially true, which — to someone who already wants to believe it — is validation enough.

Some fact-checking organisations have adopted more granular rating systems to address this problem. The Washington Post's Pinocchio scale, PolitiFact's Truth-O-Meter, and Snopes's own range from True to Unproven to False each attempt, in different ways, to capture the complexity of claims that are not simply right or wrong. But all face the same fundamental tension: the complexity necessary for accuracy competes with the simplicity necessary for communication.

### What Fact-Checking Can and Cannot Do

Theresa Fisher's 2016 debunking on Van Winkle's (a now-defunct sleep-focused publication) represents another model for handling this kind of claim. Fisher's piece went beyond simple verification to examine *why* the claim was so appealing and how the aggregation process had distorted the underlying research. This more explanatory approach is arguably more effective at inoculating readers against similar claims in the future, because it teaches a transferable skill (scepticism toward neatly packaged science claims) rather than just correcting a specific factual error.

The limitation of all these efforts is reach. The viral claim has been shared millions of times across multiple platforms over more than a decade. The various fact-checks, collectively, have probably reached a small fraction of that audience. And even those who encounter a fact-check may not update their beliefs: research on the "continued influence effect" shows that corrections are often less memorable and less persuasive than the original misinformation, especially when the misinformation aligns with existing beliefs.

### The Meta-Lesson

The fact-checking ecosystem's engagement with this claim reveals a broader truth about the relationship between misinformation and correction in the digital age. Fact-checking is necessary but not sufficient. It operates downstream of the problem — catching individual claims after they have gone viral — rather than addressing the upstream conditions that generate them: the incentive structures of digital media, the scientific literacy deficits in journalism, the platform designs that reward engagement over accuracy, and the deep human appetite for stories that make complex realities feel simple.

This does not mean fact-checking is futile. It means it needs to be understood as one component of a larger epistemic infrastructure, not as a complete solution. And it means that we should judge the success of a fact-check not only by whether it corrects a specific claim but by whether it equips readers to recognise the *pattern* — so that when the next neatly packaged gender-science claim arrives, dressed in lab coats and trailing footnotes, they know to ask: *who actually said this, what did they actually find, and what got lost along the way?*
