# Women Need More Sleep Than Men? Here's What Science Says


## 1. The Claim

A persistent and widely circulated claim holds that women need significantly more sleep than men, with some versions specifying that the difference is explained by women's brains working harder — or faster — during waking hours. A particularly viral formulation, supported by images of Audrey Hepburn sleeping elegantly in the 1961 film *Breakfast at Tiffany's*, asserts that "study finds that women need more sleep than men because women's brains work 5X faster." This version of the claim spread across Instagram, TikTok, and X (formerly Twitter) throughout 2024, accumulating engagement figures that suggest it reached tens of millions of viewers.

The underlying claim has several distinct layers that need to be disentangled. The first layer is empirical and descriptive: do women, on average, actually sleep more than men? The second layer is explanatory: if they do, why? The third layer is normative: does this difference reflect a genuine biological need, or is it a product of social circumstances? The viral claim collapses all three layers into a single confident assertion — women need more sleep, because their brains are more active — and presents this as established science. Variations on the theme also assert that women's hormones, metabolic demands, or emotional labour requirements drive a need for substantially longer sleep than men require.

The claim circulates with particular intensity in spaces associated with women's wellness, lifestyle optimisation, and pro-feminist social media. It serves a dual function: affirming that women's physiological reality is different from men's and implicitly criticising social environments in which women are expected to meet the same sleep-on-less norms that supposedly apply to men. The claim thus does genuine ideological work regardless of its empirical status, which partly explains its resilience to correction.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Women do appear to sleep somewhat more than men — but the difference is measured in minutes, not hours.** A major sociological analysis by Burgard and Ailshire (2013, *American Sociological Review*) examined time-use data from nearly 73,000 U.S. adults surveyed between 2003 and 2007, and found that women slept approximately 11 minutes more per night on average than men. This is a statistically significant but practically modest difference. The gap varied substantially by life stage and household composition: among young, single, childless adults the difference was approximately five minutes; among partnered childless couples it widened to approximately 28 minutes; among households with young children it was about 26 to 27 minutes, favouring women; and among single parents it was about 21 minutes. The only demographic category in which men slept slightly more than women was older, single, childless adults.

Crucially, the Burgard and Ailshire (2013) study was sociological in character — it examined time-use patterns and their correlates, not underlying physiology. Its findings are consistent with both a biological interpretation (women need more sleep) and a sociological one (women's social obligations and caregiving responsibilities leave them more fatigued and therefore they sleep more when they can). The study's authors explicitly argued that the observed difference likely reflected societal patterns, specifically noting that women's leisure time dropped more sharply than men's when family responsibilities increased, referencing corroborating evidence from Miller and Brown (2005, *Leisure Sciences*) and related work.

**Women sleep more poorly, which complicates simple "need more" claims.** A robust literature shows that women experience worse sleep quality than men across multiple measures: higher rates of insomnia, more nocturnal awakenings, and greater susceptibility to sleep disorders across the lifespan. The reasons for these differences are multiple and change with the stage of life:

Jehan et al. (2016, *Journal of Sleep Medicine and Disorders*) documented that premenstrual hormonal fluctuations are associated with sleep disruption, increased anxiety, and depression that feed restless sleep. Baker and Driver (2007, *Sleep Medicine*) found that hormonal fluctuations over the course of the menstrual cycle — particularly in progesterone and oestrogen levels — affect circadian rhythm regulation, meaning that women's sleep architecture is biochemically disrupted in regular, predictable ways that men's is not. During pregnancy, restless leg syndrome, physical discomfort, and hormonal changes dramatically increase insomnia risk, as documented in reviews published by the Sleep Foundation.

Venn et al. (2008, *British Journal of Sociology*) examined what they termed the "fourth shift" — the gendered pattern of nocturnal caregiving in which women are disproportionately the partner who wakes to respond to children's needs during the night, a finding that helps explain the widening sleep gap in households with young children. This social mechanism is entirely consistent with Burgard and Ailshire's (2013) sociological findings but has nothing to do with the brain-speed hypothesis promoted on social media.

Later in the lifespan, menopause introduces two further major sleep disruptors: hot flashes, which frequently cause nocturnal awakening, and a substantially elevated risk of sleep apnoea. Mirer et al. (2017, *Menopause*) found that the transition through menopause was associated with significantly increased rates of sleep-disordered breathing. Krishnan and Collop (2006, *Current Opinion in Pulmonary Medicine*) documented that clear sex-based differences in sleep disorders exist across the lifespan, with women bearing a higher burden of insomnia-type disorders and men more commonly affected by sleep apnoea in pre-menopausal age cohorts.

**The "5X brain speed" claim has no scientific support.** There is no peer-reviewed study, no imaging study, no neurological measurement of any kind that shows women's brains operate at five times the speed of men's during waking hours, or that such a differential — if it existed — would create a linear additional sleep requirement. The claim appears to originate in a misrepresentation of cognitive neuroscience research on average brain activity patterns, but the specific "5X faster" figure does not appear in any credible scientific source. The broader hypothesis — that more cognitively active brains need more sleep — does have genuine plausibility as a class of hypothesis, but the premises would need to be established before the conclusion could be drawn, and neither the sex differential in brain activity rates nor the causal link between waking activity and sleep requirement has been demonstrated at the level the viral claim implies.

Notably, as *National Geographic* reported in March 2024, the biomedical research enterprise has historically enrolled predominantly male subjects in basic and clinical research, and the science of female physiology — including sleep physiology — remains systematically under-developed. This means that the absence of evidence for the "5X" claim does not necessarily reflect absence of effect; it may reflect absence of research. This epistemic situation — genuine uncertainty combined with genuine sex-based biological differences in sleep — creates fertile ground for speculative claims to circulate as if they were established findings.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Confirmation by lived experience.** For many women, the claim resonates strongly with their subjective experience. Women who manage caregiving responsibilities, paid work, domestic labour, and emotional support burdens often feel genuinely more tired than their male partners or colleagues and experience a sense of recognition when told that this reflects a real biological difference rather than a personal deficiency. The claim converts felt fatigue into scientific validation. This is a powerful psychological mechanism: data that explains suffering is more emotionally compelling than data that merely describes it.

**The gap between "women sleep more" and "women need more sleep."** The observational finding — women sleep more on average — is real and reasonably well-documented. The social media claim translates this into a stronger normative statement about need. This translation from observed behaviour to biological necessity is a logical jump that the underlying data does not support, but it is a jump that most casual readers will not notice. The shift from "women sleep more" to "women need more sleep" happens seamlessly and invisibly in the reformulation.

**The epistemic vacuum created by under-researched female physiology.** The *National Geographic* (McKeever, 2024) observation about the state of women's health research is itself an epistemic novelty with direct relevance to how this claim propagates. When the scientific record on a topic is thin, speculation fills the gap. Research cultures that excluded women from clinical trials for decades — justified on grounds of hormonal variability making data harder to interpret — have left an authentic knowledge gap. People know that women's physiological experience differs from men's and that science has not fully characterised those differences. They are therefore inclined to fill that gap with plausible-seeming explanations for differences they perceive or experience. The "brain works harder" explanation is narratively satisfying and emotionally resonant. In the absence of well-communicated science to the contrary, it is cognitively efficient to accept it.

**Social media's structural preference for affirming simplicity.** The nuanced Snopes finding — women sleep somewhat more, reasons are multiple and partially sociological, the viral claim about brain speed is unsupported — does not compress into a shareable format nearly as effectively as "women's brains work 5X faster and need more rest." Social media platforms algorithmically reward content that generates strong positive reactions, and claims that validate lived experience and imply that women deserve more rest (a socially progressive-seeming message) will consistently outperform accurate but complicated corrections. The correction, if it circulates at all, will be framed as "science fails women again" — which is not entirely wrong either, deepening the epistemic muddle.


## 4. Verdict

**Mixture — the sleep difference is real but modest; the explanations circulating online are unsupported.** Women do sleep more than men on average, but the difference is on the order of minutes per night and is likely substantially driven by social and caregiving factors rather than exclusively biological ones. Women's sleep experiences are genuinely different from men's — they are more physiologically disrupted by hormonal cycles, pregnancy, and menopause, and they carry more nocturnal caregiving burdens — but these facts do not collapse into the simple claim that women's brains work faster and therefore require substantially more sleep. The "5X brain speed" claim is not supported by any identified scientific literature. The genuine complexity of sex-based sleep differences is an area where more research is needed, and that uncertainty should be named as such rather than resolved by confident false assertions.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The women-sleep-more claim belongs to a class of gender-medicine claims that circulate with special intensity online: claims that acknowledge biological sex differences in ways that validate women's experiences while simultaneously overstating the evidence for those differences and attributing them to mechanisms that have no empirical support. Other examples in this family include claims about women's immune systems being stronger than men's (partially true in nuanced ways, massively overstated in popular framing), women being more empathic than men (real average differences exist but vastly overstated in the forms that circulate), and women processing emotions more thoroughly, which requires more neural activity and therefore more rest (plausible but undemonstrated at the specificity implied).

The structural problem in all of these cases is the same: genuine sex-based biological differences exist and are interesting and important, the underlying science is frequently incomplete due to historical under-investment in female-specific research, and this combination creates a zone in which speculative amplifications of partial findings can circulate as confident science. Addressing this problem requires not just individual claim correction but a broader investment in sex-disaggregated and female-enrolled research that would replace the speculation with evidence.

From a media literacy standpoint, the women-sleep claim is also a useful case study in the difference between observational correlation and causal explanation. The finding that women sleep more (the observation) does not, by itself, tell us why. The jump from "this is what we see" to "this is why it happens" is a separate inferential step requiring separate evidence. Popular science communication routinely elides this distinction, presenting correlational findings with causal language. Training readers to ask "how do we know this is the cause rather than a correlation with the cause?" is a core media literacy skill, and the sleep claim is an accessible teaching example.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated this claim as "Mixture" in its January 2025 analysis by Anna Rascouët-Paz, which is probably the most accurate available summary rating for a claim this layered. The "mixture" verdict captures the reality that the headline claim — women sleep more — is true in a limited observational sense, while the causal mechanism cited — brain speed — is not supported. This is a more intellectually honest rating than a simple "false" would be, because it does not dismiss the observational kernel while still flagging the unsupported explanatory claim.

For fact-checkers encountering similar claims at the intersection of gender, health, and social media amplification, several analytical steps are valuable: (1) disaggregate the descriptive from the causal claim — "women sleep more" and "women's brains work 5X faster" are separate claims requiring separate evaluation; (2) seek the original study that is being cited (the specific "5X brain speed" formulation should trigger immediate scepticism because precision of this kind without a citation is a red flag); (3) check whether the cited study is a sociological/epidemiological study (which describes patterns) or a physiological/neurological study (which could speak to mechanisms); (4) note whether the difference described is proportionally large enough to warrant the surrounding rhetoric — 11 minutes is not the same as hours; and (5) check whether the "undetermined" aspects of the claim are being presented as established rather than unresolved. The structural lesson is that partially-true claims are often more dangerous to accurate public understanding than wholly false ones, because the true component lends credibility to the false component in a way that false-only claims cannot.
