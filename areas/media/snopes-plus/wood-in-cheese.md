# Wood in Cheese?

## §1 — Claim & Verdict Summary

In February 2016, a wave of sensationalist media coverage alleged that widely sold parmesan and other grated cheeses contained "wood pulp" in concentrations that amounted to consumer fraud. The headline language was unambiguous: "This cheese company has been putting wood in their parmesan for 30 years" (NowThis News); "FDA discovers that '100% real' Parmesan cheese you're eating may be wood" (PCMag); "Wood in your Parmesan?" (TODAY Show). Consumers were understandably alarmed — the idea that a staple table condiment was adulterated with structural lumber byproducts struck millions as both disgusting and deeply deceptive.

**Snopes verdict: MOSTLY FALSE.** The claims contained a kernel of truth buried under multiple layers of imprecision. The actual controversy involved cellulose — specifically carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) or similar plant-fiber derivatives — used as an anti-clumping agent in pre-grated cheese products. The FDA had investigated Castle Cheese and other manufacturers not because cheese contained "wood" in any literal or structurally recognizable sense but because cellulose concentrations in certain products exceeded labeling standards and reflected adulteration by substitution of cheaper cheese varieties. The safety issue was one of labeling honesty and compositional fraud, not of consumer exposure to a toxic or biologically harmful substance. Cellulose is a dietary fiber consumed in virtually every plant-based food. It presents no known health hazard at the concentrations found in cheese products.

This entry traces the full controversy from the 2012 FDA investigation through the February 2016 media storm, examines the biochemistry of cellulose and its regulatory status, assesses the legitimate public-interest concern around food labeling that the misleading coverage obscured, and evaluates the epistemology of the "wood" framing as a case of technically sourced but systematically distorted food reporting.

## §2 — Origin & Spread of the Claim

The 2016 episode was triggered by a Bloomberg Businessweek article reporting results of independent laboratory testing of store-bought grated parmesan. The tested products included Essential Everyday 100% Grated Parmesan Cheese (8.8% cellulose), Great Value 100% Grated Parmesan Cheese from Walmart (7.8% cellulose), Kraft (3.8% cellulose), and Whole Foods 365 brand (0.3% cellulose despite no cellulose listed on the label). The numbers presented a genuine story: some manufacturers were including cellulose concentrations substantially above what would be expected from a product sold and labeled as "100% Parmesan."

The article's broader context involved the criminal prosecution of Castle Cheese Inc., whose president was scheduled to plead guilty to criminal charges related to mislabeling products by using excessive cellulose and substituting cheddar for romano. This was a legitimate food-fraud enforcement action by the FDA and Justice Department. But the Bloomberg piece's framing — and the subsequent wildfire of secondary coverage — elided the distinction between "cellulose added as anti-clumping agent (standard, safe, regulated)" and "cellulose fraudulently used to inflate bulk and substitute for real cheese (illegal under labeling law)." The conceptual conflation was then compounded by the further elision of "cellulose" into "wood pulp" — which, while etymologically defensible (cellulose is derived from plant cell walls and can be sourced from wood), is chemically and physiologically misleading.

The February 2016 NowThis News tweet — "This cheese company has been putting wood in their parmesan for 30 years" — achieved circulation in the high tens of thousands within hours. The original Bloomberg investigation, which was substantially more nuanced, was quickly overtaken by the derivative coverage, which progressively amplified the alarming framing while stripping context. This pattern — a legitimate regulatory story laundered into a health panic through successive paraphrase — is characteristic of what media scholars call **information cascade distortion** (Sunstein, 2007, *On Rumors: How Falsehoods Spread, Why We Believe Them, and What Can Be Done*, Farrar, Straus and Giroux).

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: The Chemistry and Regulatory Status of Food Cellulose

**3.1 — What Cellulose Is and Is Not**

Cellulose (chemical formula: (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ) is the most abundant organic polymer on Earth. Every plant cell wall contains it. Every piece of fruit, vegetable, grain, or legume you consume contains cellulose. In raw dietary intake, cellulose functions as insoluble dietary fiber — it passes through the human gastrointestinal tract largely intact because humans lack the cellulase enzymes required to hydrolyze the β-1,4-glycosidic bonds that link glucose units in cellulose chains. This is not a food-safety concern; it is a nutritional fact with well-established health benefits. Dietary fiber, including cellulose, is associated with reduced colorectal cancer risk, improved glycemic control, and enhanced gut microbiome diversity (Slavin, J. L., 2013, *Fiber and Prebiotics: Mechanisms and Health Benefits*, Nutrients, 5(4), 1417–1435).

Microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) and carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) — the specific derivatives most commonly used in food applications — are produced by chemically or mechanically processing plant-derived cellulose. The source material can be cotton linters, wood pulp, corn cobs, or other agricultural byproducts; the pharmaceutical and food-grade derivatives are highly purified and functionally indistinguishable at the molecular level regardless of feedstock. The FDA classifies both MCC and CMC as GRAS (Generally Recognized As Safe) for use in food, with no restricted use level for most applications. MCC appears in FDA 21 CFR §182.90; CMC appears in 21 CFR §182.1745.

When Bon Appétit's February 2016 coverage quoted food author Jeff Potter — "Cellulose is a non-digestible plant fiber, and we actually happen to need non-digestible vegetable fiber in our food" — this was scientifically accurate. The nutritional register of cellulose consumed via parmesan cheese is identical to the cellulose consumed via a stalk of broccoli. There is no dose-response toxicity concern.

**3.2 — Why "Wood Pulp" Is a Materially Misleading Frame**

The specific phrase "wood pulp" activates a cognitive prototype that is substantially different from refined food-grade cellulose. "Wood pulp" conjures sawdust, splinters, paper-manufacturing byproducts, rough brown fiber — material that no reasonable person would eat voluntarily and that connotes industrial waste being smuggled into food. The referent image is structurally incompatible with a fine white powder whose primary culinary function is preventing cheese from clumping.

As Bon Appétit's 2016 coverage noted, the cellulose in cheese "could also have been made in part from a crateful of apples that didn't make it onto supermarket shelves, or corn husks, or any variety of plant matter." This point is chemically exact: the molecular structure of cellulose from wood, from apple cores, from wheat bran, and from cotton is identical. The "wood pulp" framing was a selective invocation of a conceptually alarming but functionally irrelevant property of the molecule's possible origin.

This framing strategy — technically accurate but systematically misleading — is sometimes called **truthful misdirection** in science communication ethics. It satisfies narrow factual defensibility (cellulose can be derived from wood pulp) while conveying a false material-safety implication (eating cheese made with cellulose is like eating sawdust). The epistemically relevant property — "is this substance harmful at this concentration?" — is displaced by an aesthetically loaded but functionally irrelevant property — "where did the raw material come from?"

**3.3 — The Actual Regulatory Violation and What It Means**

The FDA's 2013 warning letter to Castle Cheese (Castle Cheese Inc., FDA Warning Letter, 11 July 2013) cited § 402(b)(4) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act: "a substance has been added to increase the products' bulk and weight." This provision is not about safety; it is about fraud. Using cellulose (or starch) to artificially inflate the weight of a product sold as "100% Parmesan" constitutes economic adulteration — a labeling violation that deprives consumers of the product they are paying for and allows fraudulent composition of a nominally premium food item.

A secondary issue involved the substitution of cheaper cheese varieties (cheddar for romano) in products labeled as romano. This is also labeling fraud, again with no safety implication but with substantial consumer-deception consequences.

The meaningful public-interest issue, then, was: **Are consumers getting what the label says they are buying?** The answer, for Castle Cheese's products, was definitively no. This is a legitimate food-system governance concern — particularly relevant in the context of premium cheese pricing, where consumers pay substantially above commodity rates on the presumption of authenticity. But the story "some cheese manufacturers commit labeling fraud" is vastly different from "cheese contains wood," in terms of both the mechanism and the risk to consumers.

## §4 — Regulatory & Scientific Context

The FDA's regulatory framework for cellulose in cheese has two distinct dimensions: **GRAS safety status** (is it safe to consume?) and **labeling standards** (how much is allowed before it must be disclosed, and when does it constitute adulteration?).

On safety: there is no controversy. MCC and CMC have been in continuous use in food manufacturing for decades and have been subject to extensive safety review. The Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA) has evaluated cellulose derivatives repeatedly (most recently reviewed in JECFA Monograph 7, 2012) and established no Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) on grounds that the observed safety margin is too large to warrant a numerical limit. In practice, cellulose in food is limited by labeling law and manufacturing economics, not by safety concerns.

On labeling: 21 CFR §133 governs the standards of identity for cheeses and cheese products. "Parmesan cheese" has a codified standard of identity that specifies permitted ingredients, moisture content, and fat-to-protein ratios. Cellulose is not a permitted ingredient in genuine parmesan under the standard of identity; it is, however, a lawful additive in "grated cheese products" that do not invoke the "parmesan" standard, provided it is listed on the ingredient label. The Castle Cheese prosecution turned on the company's products being sold as "100% Grated Parmesan Cheese" while containing undisclosed cellulose beyond anti-caking levels.

Bloomberg's independent laboratory testing revealed that even Kraft — a major mainstream brand — contained 3.8% cellulose. Whether this was unlawful under the relevant standard of identity for Kraft's specific product formulation (which may or may not have been labeled as "100% Parmesan" versus as a "grated cheese product") was not established in Snopes's coverage or in the subsequent media record. This constitutes the most significant remaining knowledge gap in the claims record.

## §5 — Comparative Misinformation Ecology

The wood-in-cheese panic of February 2016 was not unprecedented. As Snopes noted, the same "wood pulp in fast food" meme had circulated in 2014, targeting fast-food burger patties that incorporated cellulose as a hamburger additive or binder. Slate's rebuttal at the time (published 2 July 2014) characterized the media treatment as "not only alarmist but 100 percent misleading," noting that cellulose is present in every plant-based food. The 2016 cheese version of the panic followed an identical structural path: a legitimate food-fraud enforcement story was translated into a mass-market health-contamination panic via the substitution of "cellulose" with the more alarming "wood pulp" framing.

This structural recurrence points to a systemic dynamic in food journalism: **regulatory enforcement stories, which require legal and technical context to understand correctly, are routinely processed through a chemistry-as-contamination frame** that is more viral but substantially less accurate. The frame exploits consumer unfamiliarity with food chemistry; most people do not know what cellulose is, but everyone knows what wood is, and the two are not equivalent for dietary purposes.

Sociological research on food disgust and contamination (Olatunji, B. O., & Sawchuk, C. N., 2005, *Disgust: Characteristic Features, Social Manifestations, and Clinical Implications*, Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, 24(7), 932–962) suggests that contamination beliefs are especially resistant to correction when the framing is toxicological rather than moral or social. Once "wood" is cognitively paired with "cheese," the associative contamination belief persists even after accurate information is supplied — because the updated belief requires not just accepting new information but actively inhibiting the prior visceral association.

## §6 — Verdict, Epistemic Gap Assessment & Reader Guidance

**Snopes verdict: MOSTLY FALSE.** What is true: some cheese manufacturers were found by the FDA to have engaged in labeling fraud by using cellulose as bulk filler beyond disclosed levels and by substituting cheaper cheese varieties. Castle Cheese Inc. faced criminal charges. The FDA investigation was real, the enforcement action was real, and the labeling violations were substantive. What is false: cheese contains "wood" in any meaningful sense; cellulose used in food poses a health hazard; the controversy was primarily about consumer health rather than labeling honesty.

**EARC Rating: C (Contested — Nuanced Truth)** — The claim is not simply false; it is a distortion of a genuine regulatory story. The legitimate concern (labeling fraud) is real but distinct from the stated claim (health hazard from wood).

**Knowledge Gap (Medium):** The primary gap involves cases like Kraft: independent laboratory testing found cellulose concentrations that may or may not be lawful depending on the product's exact labeling identity. No definitive regulatory adjudication of Kraft's 3.8% cellulose concentration was included in Snopes's coverage or in subsequent public records. A secondary gap involves the long-term public-health implications of widespread substitution of cheaper cheese varieties under premium labels — a consumer deception concern distinct from safety.

**Reader guidance:**
- If you are concerned about the authenticity of pre-grated parmesan, consider purchasing block parmesan (Parmigiano-Reggiano or domestic equivalent) and grating it fresh. Block parmesan is not susceptible to cellulose adulteration.
- Ingredient labels on pre-grated cheese products should list cellulose if present above trace levels. Absence of cellulose on the label does not guarantee absence of the compound (as Whole Foods 365's 0.3% result confirmed), but above-trace concentrations should appear in the ingredient list.
- Cellulose in food is not a health hazard. If your concern is fiber intake or digestive impact, the cellulose quantities present in grated cheese are nutritionally trivial.
