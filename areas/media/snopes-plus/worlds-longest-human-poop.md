## 1. The Claim

Since at least 2006, a remarkably persistent piece of gross-out internet lore has circulated across social media platforms, image boards, and email chains. The claim holds that a woman named Michelle Hines set an unofficial world record in 1995 by producing a human bowel movement measuring 26 feet in length. The feat, according to the story, was accomplished through a combination of a "super fiber-rich diet" devised by nutritionists at the University of Michigan and the use of a specifically designed plug to "curtail any premature excretions" during a weeklong preparatory period. The alleged record was documented on video at the Cranbrook Kingswood High School Bowling Alley in Bloomfield Hills, Michigan, where the length of the alley provided sufficient space for the measurement process.

The claim has circulated in various forms for nearly two decades. It appears in Google search results for "longest human poop," returning credible-looking summaries that many readers accept at face value. It received renewed attention in March 2021 when a user on X (then Twitter) posted a screenshot of Google search results accompanied by a cheeky celebration of Women's History Month, suggesting that the record was a little-known achievement properly credited to a woman. The tweet went viral, prompting fresh rounds of credulous sharing and the inevitable calls for verification.

The Snopes fact-check, published March 8, 2021 and written by Dan Evon, investigated the claim and rated it "False." The article explained that no such record exists in the Guinness Book of World Records, that the human large intestine is only about five feet long, making a 26-foot excretion biologically impossible, and that the entire story originated as an art hoax. Michelle Hines is a real artist who created a series of elaborate fictional world records as part of an installation titled "Within the Contest of No Contest." The 26-foot poop was one of several hoaxes in the series, which also included a six-foot-tall corncob, a bigfoot sighting, and crop circle imagery.

## 2. What's Actually True

The truth is more interesting than the gross-out legend. Michelle Hines is a Brooklyn-based conceptual artist who produced the "World Record" series as a deliberate meditation on the nature of belief, fame, and the absurdity of the records people pursue. In her portfolio, she explicitly described the project: "World Record #4: Peristaltic Action, 1995, video stills (6 of 8)." The accompanying text presented the hoax with a straight face, describing the "super fiber-rich diet," the assistance of University of Michigan nutritionists, and the employment of a "specifically designed plug" to ensure the weeklong endurance.

The artist's deadpan presentation is crucial to understanding the hoax's effectiveness. Hines did not add any ironic framing or wink-and-nudge signals to her description. She presented the story in the same language and format as a genuine world record submission. The accompanying photographs showed a woman grimacing on a bowling lane with what appeared to be an enormous brown extrusion, documented with time-and-date stamps that lent an air of documentary authenticity. The presentation was so effective that the art curator John Massier, who introduced Hines' work at the Hallwalls Contemporary Arts Center in 2006, described her as a "completely unrepentant pants-on-fire liarhead" who lies well "because she understands the truths behind the lies."

Massier's commentary is worth reading in full. He wrote that Hines' work "strikes that essential quality about world records: that there needs to be something vaguely unbelievable about the record in order for it to impress." The world records Hines concocts, Massier observed, "are fairly ludicrous, but they are believable." This is the key insight: a world record must stretch credulity to be impressive, but it must not break it entirely. The 26-foot poop was just unbelievable enough to be impressive and just believable enough to be taken seriously by people who did not pause to consider the basic physics of human digestion.

The hoax achieved a level of cultural penetration far beyond what Hines likely anticipated. It was included in filmmaker John Waters' 2003 book "Art: A Sex Book," which brought it to the attention of a broader audience. It has been cited in discussions of anatomical anomalies, featured in listicles about bizarre world records, and turned into a regular reference point in online discussions about improbable bodily achievements. The false story has, in a sense, become more famous than the real art project it was originally part of.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes's fact-check of the 26-foot poop claim is a model of concise, well-sourced debunking. The article identifies the origin of the hoax, traces it to the artist's portfolio, explains the biological impossibility of the claim, and provides the curator's commentary to contextualize the work as art rather than fact. The rating of "False" is unambiguous and correct. The article also does the useful service of explaining how the Google search result that gave the claim its viral second wind came to appear, noting that it was a genuine screenshot of search results that reflected the story's prominence in web content, not an endorsement of its truth.

Where the Snopes article falls short is in its treatment of the art project's meaning. The article presents Hines' hoax as a curiosity—an amusing footnote to a debunking exercise—but does not engage with the artistic and philosophical questions the project raises. Hines was not trying to deceive people maliciously. She was using the form of a world record to explore questions about what people believe and why. The Snopes article, by treating the hoax solely as a claim to be judged true or false, misses the opportunity to discuss the more interesting questions about how and why people form beliefs in improbable things and what role art can play in exposing the mechanisms of belief.

The article also does not address the specific dynamics that made this hoax so successful online. The 26-foot poop claim circulates because it is, in Massier's phrase, "just believable enough." It triggers a particular kind of cognitive response: something so vivid and specific that the mind accepts it as plausible before the critical faculties have a chance to intervene. This is the same mechanism that makes many viral hoaxes effective, and the Snopes article could have contributed to inoculation against future hoaxes by explaining it.

Furthermore, the Snopes article does not address the broader phenomenon of art hoaxes that escape their intended context. The trajectory from art installation to internet meme to debunked false claim is a well-worn path in the digital age. Works of conceptual art that depend on a certain amount of friction or ambiguity for their meaning often lose that context entirely when they are extracted from the gallery setting and circulated on social media. The Snopes article treats the loss of context as a simple error, when it is in fact a structural feature of how information moves online.

## 4. Why This Matters

There is a temptation to dismiss the 26-foot poop claim as trivial—a harmless piece of internet nonsense that does not deserve serious attention. That temptation should be resisted. The mechanisms that make this hoax successful are the same mechanisms that make more dangerous hoaxes successful. If a story about an anatomically impossible 26-foot excretion can circulate for nearly two decades and achieve viral resurgence multiple times, it is not because people are stupid. It is because the human brain is wired to accept vivid, specific stories before it pauses to evaluate them, and the online information ecosystem is optimized to exploit that wiring.

The 26-foot poop hoax is a case study in three important phenomena. First, it demonstrates the power of the "just barely credible" threshold: the most successful hoaxes are those that are just plausible enough to pass a rapid mental filter but just improbable enough to be interesting. Second, it illustrates the context collapse that occurs when content moves from one domain (art) to another (social media). The meaning of Hines' work depended on its presentation as art within an art context. Stripped of that context and recirculated as fact, it became something entirely different. Third, it shows how even the most absurd false claims can achieve a kind of institutional memory through Google search rankings. The fact that a 2021 screenshot of Google results for "longest poop" included the Hines story is a testament to the persistence of false information in search indexes.

There is also a genuine question about the ethics of Hines' project. The artist was not intending to deceive maliciously, but her work has had the effect of deceiving people for nearly two decades. An art curator like John Massier can appreciate the conceptual cleverness of the hoax, but the people who shared it as true in 2021 were not participating in an art appreciation exercise. They were spreading false information that they believed to be true, contributing to a culture in which it is increasingly difficult to distinguish real from fabricated claims.

The question of who bears responsibility for this is complex. The artist created the hoax with artistic intentions. The art world validated it as meaningful work. The internet divorced it from context and amplified it. The platforms that recommended it and the search engines that indexed it provided the infrastructure for its spread. Snopes's debunking was effective but reached only a fraction of the people who had seen the original claim. Responsibility is distributed across the entire information ecosystem, which means that no single intervention can fully address the problem.

## 5. Research Notes

The primary source for the Michelle Hines art project is the artist's own portfolio, which includes the "World Record" series and the specific "Peristaltic Action" entry. This portfolio is hosted on a personal website that has been preserved and is cited by multiple secondary sources. John Massier's exhibition notes for the Hallwalls Contemporary Arts Center, published in 2006, provide the most insightful commentary on Hines' work and its relationship to belief and truth. Massier's description of Hines as a "completely unrepentant pants-on-fire liarhead" is both colorful and analytically precise.

The inclusion of Hines' work in John Waters' 2003 book "Art: A Sex Book" is an important vector for the hoax's spread. Waters, a filmmaker known for his transgressive and camp aesthetic, was an ideal curator for Hines' brand of boundary-testing conceptual art. Waters' endorsement gave the work a cultural legitimacy that it might not otherwise have achieved, while also signaling to those in the know that the work was not meant to be taken literally.

The Guinness Book of World Records does not list any entry for longest human bowel movement. This absence is itself significant. Guinness maintains stringent verification standards for physiological records, and any claim involving bodily functions would require medical documentation and independent witnesses. The fact that there is no such record is not merely evidence of absence but a strong indication that no credible attempt to verify the Hines claim has ever been made.

The biological impossibility of the claim is straightforward but worth documenting. The human large intestine, where feces is formed and stored prior to elimination, is approximately five feet in length in adults. The claim that a 26-foot long piece of feces could be produced and extruded in one continuous piece is not merely improbable but anatomically impossible, on the same order as claiming that a human heart could pump blood ten feet outside the body. The claim fails basic anatomical plausibility, which is a useful heuristic for evaluating similar claims. For context, the longest recorded human feces in the medical literature is measured in feet, not tens of feet, and even those extreme cases involve pathological conditions such as severe constipation or megacolon. A 26-foot excretion would require a digestive tract of comparable length, which would make basic human anatomy impossible—there would be no room in the abdominal cavity for the other organs. This kind of basic plausibility check is one of the most powerful tools available to anyone evaluating extraordinary claims online. If a claim requires the violation of a well-established physical or biological constraint, it is almost certainly false, regardless of how many sources appear to support it.

The Google search behavior that drove the 2021 resurgence is documented in the Snopes article. A Twitter user posted a genuine screenshot of search results for "world record longest poop" that included the Hines story, and the image was shared widely without accompanying context. This is a phenomenon worth further study: search engine results pages (SERPs) that include debunked claims alongside verified facts, creating a visual context in which all results appear equally authoritative.

## 6. Verdict

The Snopes fact-check is correct: the claim that Michelle Hines set a world record for a 26-foot-long bowel movement is false. The story originated as an art project, not as a genuine athletic or physiological achievement. The Snopes rating of "False" is accurate and well-supported by the biological evidence, the absence of a Guinness record, and the documented provenance of the art project.

For the Truth Vault's purposes, this entry confirms the Snopes verdict while extending the analysis into the hoax's meaning as an art project, the psychological mechanisms that make such hoaxes successful, and the structural dynamics that allow them to persist across decades and platforms. The claim was false when it was first posted, it was false when Snopes debunked it, and it remains false today. But the reasons for its persistence are worth understanding, because they are the same reasons that more consequential falsehoods persist in domains far removed from bowling alleys and digestive records. The trajectory of this hoax—from conceptual art installation to viral internet misinformation to debunked false claim—illuminates the fragility of context in the digital age. Nothing about the Hines project prepared it for its life on the internet, and nothing about the internet's content distribution systems is designed to preserve the context that makes art legible as art. The hoax's persistence is a testament not to its sophistication but to the structural gap between how meaning is created in the closed ecosystem of the art world and how it is consumed in the open ecosystem of the web.
