## §1 — Claim & Origin

The claim examined in this entry originated not as an organically generated social media post or a piece of journalism, but as a paid online advertisement. The advertisement in question read: "[Gallery] Avoid This Fast Food Chain at All Costs — Scientists Agree It's the Worst."

This headline is a carefully engineered compound of authority invocation ("Scientists Agree") and loss-aversion activation ("Avoid This Fast Food Chain at All Costs"). The framing implies a scientific consensus reaching a definitive verdict — a claim structure that would, if true, be genuinely newsworthy. Readers who clicked the ad were directed to a website called Definition.org, where a 25-page slideshow article bore the headline: "Worst Fast Food Restaurants, Ranked."

The article's opening paragraphs are reproduced substantively in Snopes' coverage, and they make immediately clear that no scientific approach was employed. The text describes fast food as "as American as apple pie," refers to multiple chains as "just terrible," and uses language like "wonderfully unique fast-food establishments" and "scummy worst fast food places." The ranking of chains — beginning with Arby's and ending with McDonald's — was not numbered, making the claimed "ranking" unverifiable even on its own terms. The methodology for determining the order of entries, whether ascending or descending by some quality, was never explained. Each of the 25 pages contained "a review from a single customer who had a bad experience at one location for each restaurant" — essentially one negative Yelp-style anecdote per chain, aggregated without any statistical methodology or systematic sampling.

Crucially, scientists were not mentioned anywhere in the article. The advertisement's headline authority claim — "Scientists Agree" — had no referent within the linked content.

Snopes Senior Reporter Jordan Liles published the fact-check on February 13, 2021, and updated it June 13, 2023. His verdict — False — is straightforward: you cannot "rate a claim as true" when the claim's substantive predicate (scientific agreement) has no corresponding evidence anywhere in the linked content.

## §2 — Verdict & Evidence Base

**Verdict: False.**

The evidence chain for this verdict requires only two steps:

1. The advertisement claimed: "Scientists Agree It's the Worst" [fast food chain].
2. The linked article contained: (a) no scientists; (b) no scientific methodology; (c) no citation of any scientific publication, study, dataset, or researcher; (d) a single negative consumer review per chain, sourced without documented methodology; and (e) no indication of what criteria were used to select chains, order entries, or evaluate "worst-ness."

The verdict is therefore not a contested judgment requiring weighing of competing evidence but a direct factual finding: the claim in the advertisement is not supported by the content to which the advertisement links, and there is no independent scientific consensus document or study corroborating the advertisement's assertion.

The chains mentioned across the 25 pages included Arby's (page 1), Dairy Queen, Subway, Taco Bell, Wendy's, and McDonald's (page 25, the apparent nadir of the ranking), among 20 others. The selection appears comprehensive — it covers most major US QSR chains — but the criteria for inclusion, exclusion, and ordering are opaque. The Snopes article notes that the list appeared on the publication Definition.org in a slideshow format specifically designed to maximize page views (and therefore advertising impressions), with one chain per page requiring 25 clicks to read the full article.

Jordan Liles appended to the fact-check a meta-explanation of the business model driving this category of claim: "Misleading ads often lead to obscure websites that host lengthy slideshow articles with lots of pages. It's called advertising 'arbitrage.' The advertiser's goal is to make more money on ads displayed on the slideshow's pages than it cost to show the initial ad that lured them to it."

This meta-transparency is one of Snopes' more valuable communicative contributions: not merely debunking the claim but expositing the structural incentive system that produced it.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: Advertising Arbitrage as Misinformation Infrastructure

The "scientists agree" fast food chain claim exemplifies a specific and well-understood manipulation of authority heuristics within an advertising-arbitrage business model. Understanding how this works at the level of cognitive science and digital economics is essential for competent information navigation in the contemporary media environment.

**The authority heuristic in advertising**: Cialdini (2001, *Influence: Science and Practice*, Allyn & Bacon) identified authority as one of six core principles of persuasion, documenting that people systematically assign higher credibility and compliance probability to claims endorsed by recognized experts. The "scientists agree" formulation is a minimal authority invocation that activates this heuristic without providing any specific, verifiable scientific source. It is the advertising equivalent of the "one weird trick doctors hate him" format — a phrase structure that signals scientific backing without instantiating it.

Pornpitakpan (2004, *Journal of Advertising Research*) systematically reviewed research on source expertise effects in advertising, finding robust evidence across 25 years of studies that perceived source expertise significantly increased persuasion, even when that expertise was unrelated to the product domain. The "scientists agree" formulation exploits this effect: it is not that the advertisement claims scientists with relevant expertise (food science? nutrition research? public health?) have agreed — it is simply that "scientists," as a generic authority class, are invoked as endorsers.

**The advertising arbitrage model**: Online advertising arbitrage is a well-documented digital-economy practice in which content publishers buy traffic cheaply (through display ads on high-volume platforms) and recoup that cost plus profit through display advertising shown to users who click through to the destination site. For this model to work, the destination content must maximize page views per session — hence the multi-page slideshow format, which requires users to click through 25 pages to consume the full article, generating 25 page impressions and 25 units of advertising inventory per session. The clickbait headline's function is to maximize click-through rate on the initial ad, as click-through rate directly determines the volume of traffic the content publisher can monetize.

This model has been analyzed in detail by Chesney and Citron (2019, *Notre Dame Law Review*), who noted that the economic incentives of advertising arbitrage are structurally misaligned with journalistic accuracy because click-through rate — which drives revenue — is maximized by emotional arousal rather than factual accuracy. A headline claiming "Scientists Agree It's Worst" generates higher click-through than "One Website Compiled Negative Reviews of 25 Fast Food Chains," even though the latter is accurate and the former is not.

**The "doctors hate him" genealogy**: The Snopes article notes that the "scientists agree" framing reminded Jordan Liles of the "doctors hate him" trope — a reference to a genre of internet advertising from the early 2010s that used phrases like "One Weird Trick Doctors Don't Want You to Know" to sell diet supplements, medical devices, and financial products. The Internet meme "Doctors Hate Him" was extensively documented by Know Your Meme and became a symbol of manipulative online advertising. The "scientists agree" formulation in the Definition.org advertisement is formally analogous: it invokes a credentialed authority class ("scientists") in the same syntactically minimal way, without specifying who, what study, what journal, or what consensus body.

What distinguishes the 2021 iteration from the early "doctors hate him" era is the evolution of the delivery infrastructure. The 2010s-era "one weird trick" ads typically appeared as banner advertisements on low-quality web properties. The "scientists agree" fast food ad appeared in standard display advertising slots on mainstream web properties — indicating that advertising arbitrage operations have successfully migrated toward more visible, higher-trust ad inventory as their business model has matured.

The epistemically novel element this case adds is the *content hollowness gap*: the advertisement does not merely exaggerate or distort an underlying true claim — it is not a case where real scientists mentioned fast food as somewhat unhealthy and the ad inflated this to "scientists agreed it's the worst." There is literally no scientific content in the linked article. The authority claim was fabricated entirely from the headline, with the linked content making no attempt whatsoever to substantiate it. This is a pure authority invocation with zero evidentiary backing, detectable through the simplest possible verification (reading the linked article to see if scientists are mentioned).

## §4 — Broader Context & Related Claims

The "scientists agreed on the worst fast food chain" claim belongs to a well-populated category of health and consumer behavior claims that misuse scientific authority framing. Several structurally analogous cases illuminate the pattern:

**"Doctors say this is the healthiest restaurant"** (various iterations): Multiple advertising campaigns have positioned individual fast-food chains as "doctor-approved" or "health-professional-recommended" without citation to any specific professional body, study, or endorsement. The FTC has pursued several enforcement actions against such claims when the implied endorsement was fabricated (FTC, 2020, *Guides Concerning Use of Endorsements and Testimonials in Advertising*).

**"Scientists found the best diet"** (clickbait category): A parallel category of clickbait advertisements promises that scientists have found "the" best diet, eliminating individual variation and scientific uncertainty in service of a simple, shareable claim. Chaiyachati et al. (2019, *JAMA Internal Medicine*) documented that diet claims in mass media systematically overstated certainty and misrepresented single studies as "scientific consensus."

**Definition.org as a content farm**: The specific publishing entity, Definition.org, operates within what industry analysts call a "content farm" model — a website that produces large volumes of low-effort content primarily to generate advertising revenue rather than to inform readers. Tandoc et al. (2018, *Digital Journalism*) developed a typology of "fake news" that distinguishes content farms from disinformation operations: content farms primarily serve economic rather than political or ideological goals, but their outputs can be indistinguishable from misinformation in their effects on audience beliefs.

**The "worst" frame and brand damage**: The specific framing — "scientists agree it's the worst" — raises questions about potential commercial harm to the named chains. If a consumer encountering the ad believed the claim and specifically avoided, say, Arby's or McDonald's as a result, the content farm's content would have produced brand damage without the targeted companies having any knowledge of or recourse against the mechanism. This is structurally analogous to defamation by implication, though the lack of a specific scientific claim makes legal remedy difficult.

The specific mention of Arby's as the "first" (and potentially "worst") chain in the slideshow is interesting in this regard. Arby's has been a perennial target of consumer dissatisfaction in informal rankings and social media commentary — appearing near the bottom of various brand polls (Harris et al., 2019, *Harris Poll EquiTrend Brand Rankings*) — but no peer-reviewed study in the nutritional science, consumer behavior, or food quality literature has identified Arby's as scientifically the "worst" fast food chain by any metric.

## §5 — Counterfactual Analysis & Gaps

**Gap 1: Behavioral impact measurement.** The Snopes fact-check does not cite any data on how many people saw the advertisement, clicked through, and came away with a false belief that scientists had identified a specific worst fast food chain. The claim's virality was not organic (it was a paid ad) but its reach may have been substantial depending on the advertising budget involved and the targeting parameters used. Measuring the belief-formation impact of advertising arbitrage misinformation is methodologically challenging but policy-relevant.

**Gap 2: Platform responsibility.** The advertisement appeared on mainstream advertising networks. Standard advertising guidelines for major platforms (Google's Advertising Policies, Meta's Advertising Standards) prohibit false or misleading claims in ad copy. Whether the "scientists agree" headline in this context constitutes a policy violation — given that the linked article never substantiates the claim — is a question that platforms' ad review systems apparently did not flag. A systematic study of how many clickbait ads employing false authority claims pass platform ad review would be valuable for platform accountability research.

**Gap 3: No actual scientific ranking of fast food chains.** There is, in fact, a genuine scientific and public health literature on fast food nutritional quality, menu composition, and health outcomes by chain. Poti et al. (2014, *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*) analyzed sodium content in US fast-food chain menus; Pereira et al. (2005, *The Lancet*) conducted a prospective cohort study on fast-food consumption patterns and health outcomes; the Center for Science in the Public Interest has published comparative assessments of chain menus. None of these constitutes "scientists agreed on the single worst chain" — but they represent the kind of evidence that would be required to approximate such a claim. The complete absence of any such citation in the Definition.org article is therefore the definitive signature of the hoax.

**Gap 4: FTC enforcement gap.** If the Definition.org advertisement ran on major platforms and falsely claimed scientific endorsement for a consumer-facing claim, it would potentially be subject to FTC enforcement under its guidelines on deceptive advertising. Whether any such enforcement was pursued is not addressed in the Snopes fact-check and represents an unresolved policy question.

**Gap 5: Definition.org's broader content.** The Snopes article limits its analysis to the specific slideshow article reached by the advertisement. A broader audit of Definition.org's content production practices would likely reveal systematic use of fabricated authority claims across multiple consumer domains — a pattern consistent with well-documented content-farm models.

## §6 — Significance & Takeaways

The "scientists agree on the worst fast food chain" claim is, on one level, trivially easy to debunk — the linked article contains zero science. But at the structural level, it represents a mature and economically sustainable form of attention manipulation that operates largely outside the vocabulary of conventional misinformation analysis.

**For media literacy education**, the case demonstrates a technique that students and general readers need to internalize: headings and advertisements can invoke authority (scientists, doctors, researchers, experts) without any corresponding evidentiary content in the linked text. The first question to ask on clicking any authority-invocation claim is: "Where are the scientists? What did they publish? In what journal? With what methodology?" If these questions are unanswerable, the authority claim is empty.

**For advertising regulation**, the case makes visible the regulatory gap between the FTC's prohibition of false endorsements and the practical operation of advertising networks that algorithmically match ads to audiences without reviewing whether specific factual claims in ad copy are substantiated. An ad claiming "scientists agree" creates a false impression that is materially different from what the linked content delivers, which is potentially actionable under current FTC deception standards — but enforcement at the scale of programmatic advertising is practically difficult.

**For platform trust**, the case illustrates how the appearance of mainstream distribution — an advertisement running on standard display inventory on reputable websites — lends unearned credibility to content that fails the most basic verification tests. The fact that users encounter the advertisement in the company of credible editorial content creates an implicit credibility transfer that benefits the content farm at the expense of the hosting publication's audience.

**For the fast food industry and brand protection**, the incident is a reminder that programmatic advertising arbitrage operations can produce brand-damaging content targeting major corporations without any direct relationship with those corporations, making monitoring and response difficult.

The Snopes verdict of **False** is entirely correct: scientists did not agree on the worst fast food chain, no scientists were involved in the production of the linked content, and the advertisement's authority claim was a fabrication deployed to drive click-through revenue. The Jordan Liles fact-check is admirably clear and includes the unusually valuable meta-explanation of the advertising arbitrage business model, which transforms the debunking from a narrow factual correction into a structural literacy intervention.
