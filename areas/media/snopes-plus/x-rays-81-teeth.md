## The Claim

In June 2025, a collage image containing two X-rays — one frontal and one lateral view of a human skull — began circulating widely on Reddit and other platforms. The X-rays were attributed to an 11-year-old patient with a condition called hyperdontia, defined as the presence of more teeth than the normal human complement. The most widely shared version of the claim, appearing on a post to the r/interestingasfuck subreddit on 27 June 2025 (which received more than 67,000 upvotes), stated: "An X-ray of a patient with hyperdontia (the condition of having more teeth than average). Usually adults have 32 teeth. This person had 81."

Earlier iterations of the same image — sometimes with slightly different captions — have been traced by TinEye image search to as far back as December 2013, making this one of the longer-circulating medical curiosity images in social media history. The claim has been restated consistently across twelve-plus years of resharing: the X-rays are of a real person, that person has an extraordinary number of teeth, and the number is 81. The associated images — which circulated alongside the collage in more recent (2024–2025) posts on Instagram and Facebook — included additional photographs from the same case, further cementing the impression of an extensive medical record.

The question is deceptively simple and the answer more textured than it first appears: genuine X-rays of a genuine patient, from a genuine peer-reviewed paper, containing a genuine count that is, on its own terms, correct.

## What's Actually True

The X-ray images are authentic. They were not fabricated, digitally manipulated, or AI-generated. They appeared in a clinical case report published in October 2011 in the *American Journal of Orthodontics and Dentofacial Orthopedics*, the official peer-reviewed journal of the American Association of Orthodontists. The paper is: Pereira, Marilia Nalon, et al., "Multiple Hyperdontia: Report of an Unusual Case," *American Journal of Orthodontics and Dentofacial Orthopedics*, vol. 140, no. 4, October 2011, pp. 580–584, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajodo.2010.02.038.

The paper was authored by six researchers affiliated with the Juiz de Fora Federal University (Universidade Federal de Juiz de Fora, UFJF) in the state of Minas Gerais, Brazil. The case it reports concerns an 11-year-old girl (described in the paper as an "11 years 8 months" patient and identified as a Black girl) who presented for orthodontic consultation. Radiographs — specifically cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), a form of three-dimensional X-ray imaging — revealed 81 teeth in total: 18 deciduous (primary, or "baby") teeth; 32 permanent (adult) teeth; and 31 supernumerary teeth (excess teeth beyond the standard complement). The X-ray images that went viral are taken directly from this paper.

The dental breakdown requires brief clarification. The standard adult human dentition is 32 teeth: 8 incisors, 4 canines, 8 premolars, and 12 molars (including four wisdom teeth). Children in transition between primary and permanent dentition can have both sets present simultaneously, though typically not all 32 of each simultaneously. The 81-tooth count in this patient includes all visible and imaging-identifiable dental structures across both arches (upper and lower jaw), comprising the remaining primary teeth, the erupted and developing permanent teeth, and the numerous supernumerary teeth. The supernumerary teeth were spread across the dental arches in various stages of development — some erupted, some embedded in the jawbone, some incompletely formed.

The patient's clinical team used CBCT imaging because standard two-dimensional periapical and panoramic X-rays are insufficient to characterise complex multi-tooth cases: they cannot reliably distinguish between overlapping structures or accurately localise teeth in three dimensions. CBCT produces a three-dimensional dataset from which two-dimensional cross-sections can be extracted and from which the total tooth count can be systematically determined. The technology was, by 2010–2011, the standard approach for complex supernumerary tooth cases (Betts & Camilleri, 1994, and subsequent CBCT literature).

The paper noted that the primary concern at initial assessment was whether the patient had a syndrome underlying her unusual dental presentation. Hyperdontia of this degree — 31 supernumerary teeth — is not idiopathic in most documented cases but is frequently associated with genetic syndromes, most notably Gardner syndrome (a form of familial adenomatous polyposis characterised by multiple colonic polyps and osteomas, with a well-established association with supernumerary teeth; Gardner, 1951, *American Journal of Human Genetics*, 3: 167–176) and cleidocranial dysplasia (a skeletal dysplasia syndrome characterised by abnormal clavicle development, open skull sutures, and very commonly multiple supernumerary teeth; Jensen, 1990, *Journal of Oral Pathology and Medicine*, 19: 203–208). The patient was referred to a geneticist for evaluation.

The authors described extraction of the supernumerary teeth as both the anticipated treatment and a significant clinical challenge given the extent and distribution of the excess dentition. No follow-up publications documenting the outcome of treatment are available, and the Snopes fact-checker (Kramer, William, Snopes, 23 September 2025) could find no documented outcome.

## Why People Believe This

The 81-tooth X-ray generates consistent and widespread belief — and consistent sharing — because it sits precisely at the intersection of several powerful currents in how people process medically unusual imagery.

**The authority of the radiographic image.** X-rays carry an implicit epistemic authority in popular perception that other medical images may not. They produce a kind of direct access to the interior of a body that feels undeniable — the hard structures of the body made visible without the interpretive layer of a physician's description or a photograph's surface limits. An X-ray that appears to show an extraordinary number of teeth is, to most viewers, harder to dismiss than a photograph or a written claim, because it appears to be a direct structural record rather than a representation. This authority is generally appropriate — X-rays are genuine structural records — but it also means that fake or misidentified X-ray images carry disproportionate credibility per unit of evidence.

In this case, the authority of the image is justified: the X-rays are genuine, from a genuine clinical record, appearing in a peer-reviewed journal. But the mechanism by which viewers trust the image would have operated equally powerfully if the X-rays were fabricated, which is why image verification — tracing images to primary source documents — is essential rather than optional.

**The pleasurable shock of numerical extremity.** The number 81 does specific work in the claim's persuasive structure. It is not a round number. It is specific enough to seem precisely measured rather than estimated or exaggerated. It is large enough relative to the expected 32 to generate a strong cognitive contrast (81 vs. 32: the ratio is 2.53:1). And it is stated with the authority of medical documentation — this is not "lots of teeth" or "many extra teeth" but a specific enumerated count derived from radiographic analysis. The precision reinforces authenticity.

The number is also just small enough to be conceivable as a biological fact, which is important for sustained belief. The claim invites wonder rather than immediate scepticism of the "this can't be real" variety, because 81 teeth — while extraordinary — is not physically impossible in the way that a person having 81 arms would be. The jaw can accommodate additional teeth, imperfectly and pathologically, but structurally. The claim is in the zone of surprising but biologically possible, which is where durable viral curiosities typically live.

**Epistemic novelty: the developmental biology of supernumerary teeth.** The genuinely novel scientific content in this case concerns the question of how 31 supernumerary teeth form in the first place — which is, as of current research, not fully understood at the molecular level. Tooth development in mammals is one of the most extensively studied systems in developmental biology, precisely because teeth are discrete, identifiable units of known morphology and because their developmental program is accessible to molecular genetic analysis. The process is coordinated by signalling pathways that include the Wnt pathway, the sonic hedgehog (Shh) pathway, the FGF (fibroblast growth factor) pathway, and epithelial-mesenchymal interactions regulated by transcription factors including MSX1 and MSX2 (Tucker & Sharpe, 2004, "The Cutting Edge of Mammalian Development: How the Embryo Makes Teeth," *Nature Reviews Genetics*, vol. 5, pp. 499–508).

Supernumerary teeth arise when the dental lamina — the band of ectodermal tissue that gives rise to tooth buds — produces additional tooth germs beyond the standard series. This can happen through localised hyperplasia of the dental lamina (excessive tissue proliferation producing additional structures), through splitting of existing tooth germs (creating paired or multiple teeth from a single developmental unit), or through reactivation of developmental programs that are normally downregulated after the primary and secondary dentitions are established. The precise triggers for any of these events in cases of extreme multiple hyperdontia, particularly non-syndromic cases, are not established. The published literature contains case reports of patients with 3, 4, 6, 10, or more supernumerary teeth, but cases of 20 or more — let alone 31 — are exceptionally rare and the pathways responsible are, at the time of writing, a genuine open question in dental genetics.

The extent to which this patient's hyperdontia was syndromic or idiopathic — a question the paper explicitly raises but does not resolve, since the referral outcome is unrecorded — is therefore not merely a clinical administrative detail. It is a scientifically open question about whether developmental biology can produce this outcome through a known syndrome mechanism, through a novel variant of a known mechanism, or through a genuinely uncharacterised pathway.

**The medical curiosity genre on social media.** The 81-teeth image is a participant in a well-established social media genre: the medical curiosity post. X-rays, medical photographs, and clinical images of unusual conditions circulate persistently on social media in ways that are driven partly by genuine interest in the diversity of human biology, partly by voyeuristic fascination with the pathological, and partly by the genre conventions of platforms that reward striking and unusual imagery. This genre has a complex relationship with the dignity and consent of the individuals whose cases are depicted. Case reports in medical journals typically use anonymised identifiers and minimise identifying information, but even anonymised images can carry personal information, and their unlimited circulation on social media is not something the patients or their families have either anticipated or, in many cases, consented to. The 11-year-old girl documented in the 2011 paper did not consent to her X-rays becoming a twelve-year social media fixture, and the fact that the images are technically in the scientific literature does not fully resolve the ethical complexity of their ongoing circulation.

## Verdict

The X-rays are genuine. The case is real. The number is right. The images are taken directly from a peer-reviewed 2011 case report by Pereira et al. in the *American Journal of Orthodontics and Dentofacial Orthopedics*, documenting a Brazilian 11-year-old with 81 teeth — 18 deciduous, 32 permanent, and 31 supernumerary — assessed and diagnosed via cone-beam computed tomography. Snopes rated the claim "True." The standard adult human dentition is 32 teeth; the standard child dentition in primary phase is 20, with up to 32 permanent developing. This patient's count was not merely twice the typical; it was nearly three times the standard primary count and more than twice the standard permanent.

The verification is straightforward: the source is a peer-reviewed journal publication in the American Association of Orthodontists' official journal, the authors are affiliated with an established Brazilian federal university, and the methodology (CBCT imaging with systematic tooth counting) is standard clinical practice. The images match the published paper. The claim is true.

## The Wider Picture

**Hyperdontia: prevalence, distribution, and clinical significance.** Multiple hyperdontia — defined in most dental literature as the presence of more than one supernumerary tooth — is rare. Single supernumerary teeth (most commonly a mesiodens, or midline incisor supernumerary) occur in approximately 0.1–3.8% of the population in various epidemiological studies (von Arx, 1992, *Australian Orthodontic Journal*, various; meta-analyses vary by method and population). Cases involving large numbers of supernumerary teeth are substantially rarer. The literature contains documented cases with counts of 20 or more supernumerary teeth, many of them associated with Gardner syndrome or cleidocranial dysplasia, some reported as apparently non-syndromic.

Clinically, supernumerary teeth cause multiple problems: they can block or delay the eruption of adjacent teeth, cause displacement and malposition of normal dentition, form cysts, contribute to crowding, and create challenges for orthodontic planning. Management typically involves extraction, but the specific approach — which teeth to remove, in what order, using what surgical technique — depends on the location, development stage, and proximity to normal dental roots. In cases of the scale documented in the Pereira et al. paper, the surgical complexity is substantial.

**CBCT and the evolution of dental imaging.** The use of cone-beam computed tomography in the 2011 case is itself a notable clinical and technological detail. CBCT became available in dentistry in the late 1990s and was not universally adopted in orthodontic practice until the 2000s. Unlike conventional CT scanning (which delivers a higher radiation dose and is designed for whole-body or large-region imaging), CBCT is calibrated for the limited craniofacial field of view relevant to dental and orthodontic diagnosis, producing three-dimensional voxel data at a lower radiation dose with higher resolution for dental structures. By 2010–2011, CBCT was the appropriate technology for characterising complex multi-tooth presentations like this patient's. Its use in this case allowed the clinical team to determine not just how many supernumerary teeth were present but where each one was located in three-dimensional space relative to adjacent normal teeth — information essential for surgical planning. The resulting dataset produced the dramatic cross-sectional images in the paper that subsequently circulated on social media.

**The 526-tooth precedent.** Snopes noted in its fact-check of the 81-teeth images that a previous fact-check had addressed a related case: an Indian child from whom 526 "tooth-like structures" were removed in a surgical procedure documented in 2019. That case involved a different pathology — a compound odontoma, which is a benign tumour of dental tissue that can contain numerous poorly-formed tooth-like elements rather than fully differentiated teeth — but is part of the same cultural environment in which dental curiosity images circulate. The existence of the 526-element case enriched the background context for the 81-teeth images: viewers familiar with the earlier case had a prior reference point for extreme dental anomalies, which may have both facilitated belief in the 81-teeth claim (it's in a range of known extreme cases) and prompted fact-checking attention (extraordinary dental claims have circulated before, sometimes inaccurately).

**The genetics of dental development.** The open question raised by the paper — whether the patient's hyperdontia was syndromic or represented a novel or idiosyncratic developmental event — connects to an active research frontier in mammalian developmental genetics. The BMP/Wnt pathway interactions that govern tooth number and patterning have been extensively studied in mouse models, where targeted mutations in pathway components produce supernumerary teeth, tooth loss, or patterning defects (Harjunmaa et al., "On the Number, Identity and Evolutionary Relationships of Supernumerary Teeth," *PLOS ONE*, 2012, vol. 7: e48509). Translating these mouse models to human clinical genetics is complicated by the different dental formulas between mice (which have only incisors and molars, lacking canines and premolars) and humans, and by the polygenic architecture of most dental trait variation. However, whole-exome and whole-genome sequencing of individuals with extreme hyperdontia — including cases like this — has begun to identify candidate genetic variants, some in known tooth-development pathways and some in previously uncharacterised genes.

The 81-teeth X-ray is, from this perspective, not merely a curiosity but a clinical endpoint of a developmental biology story that begins at the molecular level and plays out over years of embryonic and postnatal tooth development. The extraordinary outcome — a counting anomaly captured in radiographic form — is evidence of a process that was diverted from its normal trajectory at some early developmental decision point, and understanding what that diversion was, and why, is one of the ongoing tasks of dental developmental genetics.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check by William Kramer, published 23 September 2025, is a well-executed example of image-to-primary-source verification in the context of medical imagery.

**Image provenance via reverse search and source tracing.** The fact-checker identified the original source of the X-ray images by following a link provided by Facebook users who had shared the images — the link pointing to the Pereira et al. 2011 paper in the *American Journal of Orthodontics and Dentofacial Orthopedics*. Confirming that the images in viral circulation match those in the published paper is the essential verification step, and it was correctly executed. The TinEye search that traced the images to as far back as December 2013 is additional provenance documentation providing the context that this image has circulated for more than eleven years, which informs both the likely scale of its spread and the longevity of its persuasive credibility.

**Journal verification.** Kramer identified the *American Journal of Orthodontics and Dentofacial Orthopedics* as a peer-reviewed publication and the official journal of the American Association of Orthodontists — both accurate characterisations that establish the publication as a credible primary source for orthodontic case reports. The additional confirmation that the journal is peer-reviewed (referenced in a 2025 editorial by Park, Jae Hyun, *AJODO*, vol. 168, no. 2, pp. 121–122) provides the epistemically relevant context: this is not a predatory or fringe publication but an established venue with editorial standards and peer review.

**Appropriate uncertainty about outcome.** The fact-check correctly notes that no follow-up to the Pereira et al. case report could be found, and that the patient's ultimate outcome is unknown. This is appropriate epistemic honesty. The absence of a follow-up is not unusual for case reports in clinical dentistry — many are published as isolated descriptions of unusual presentations without tracked follow-up — but it does mean that the viral framing ("an 11-year-old with 81 teeth") is a frozen moment in an ongoing clinical history that may have resolved in various ways. Whether the patient received extensive extraction surgery, what the outcome was, and whether her adult dentition was ultimately salvageable are questions the published record does not answer.

**The outreach note.** The fact-checker noted that he reached out to lead author Robert Willer Farinazzo Vitral for additional comment. This is appropriate practice, and the transparency about pending response is responsible. Follow-up contact with paper authors in medical case reports can provide clinical detail not in the paper — including outcome information, clarifications about imaging methodology, and contextual information about the patient's syndrome assessment.

The X-ray of 81 teeth is, on any measure, remarkable. The number is correct, the source is solid, and the biological mechanism — hyperdontia driven by excess tooth germ formation during development — is real and genuinely not fully understood. This is a viral image that deserves its twelve-year circulation lifespan, not because it is disturbing or extreme, but because it accurately represents a genuine and clinically significant anomaly of human developmental biology with unresolved scientific questions at its root.
