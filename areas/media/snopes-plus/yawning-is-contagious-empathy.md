# Yawning is contagious because of empathy


## 1. The Claim

Most people reading this will have experienced it: you see someone yawn, or read the word "yawn," and a moment later you feel the compelling urge to yawn yourself. The phenomenon of contagious yawning is real and well-documented. The disputed part is the popular explanation for it.

By the mid-2000s a highly appealing scientific narrative had formed around contagious yawning: it was a form of empathic resonance, a social mirroring behaviour driven by the same neural architecture that underlies our capacity to feel what others feel. Research groups published findings suggesting that people with higher empathy scores catch yawns more readily, that contagious yawning emerges in children at the same developmental stage as theory of mind, and that individuals with autism spectrum conditions — who can have impaired social cognition — showed reduced susceptibility to yawn contagion. The story spread rapidly through popular science journalism and self-help literature. Within a few years it had achieved the status of a well-established fact in mainstream culture: catching your partner's yawn was evidence that you particularly empathise with them.

By the 2010s and into the 2020s, this narrative had come substantially undone. Meta-analyses found the empathy-yawning effect to be fragile, replication studies produced inconsistent results, and several specific claims — particularly regarding autism — failed to hold up under independent scrutiny. The story of contagious yawning and empathy is now a case study in the life cycle of a scientific finding that travels from controlled experiment to popular fact to failed replication to uncertain status.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Contagious yawning is real.** The phenomenon itself is not in dispute. A substantial proportion of adults reliably yawn in response to seeing, hearing, reading about, or even imagining another person yawning. Video-based paradigms, live exposure paradigms, and even simple text have all been used to trigger yawning in laboratory settings, with the effect holding robustly across cultures and experimental conditions. The estimate that approximately sixty to seventy percent of adults show susceptibility to yawn contagion under reasonably natural exposure conditions comes from multiple studies, including Platek et al. (2003, *Cognitive Brain Research*) and several subsequent replications. Contagious yawning occurs in other species: it has been observed in chimpanzees (Provine, 1986, *Ethology*), bonobos, wolves, and dogs (Joly-Mascheroni et al., 2008, *Biology Letters*).

**The proposed empathy link and its origins.** The hypothesis that contagious yawning relates to empathy and social cognition gained early traction from a series of studies in the mid-2000s. Platek and colleagues reported that susceptibility to yawn contagion correlated with self-reported empathy scores on standard instruments, and with performance on theory-of-mind tasks (Platek et al., 2003, *Cognitive Brain Research*). Anderson & Meno (2003, *Current Psychology of Cognition*) noted that children appeared to develop susceptibility to yawn contagion around the same developmental stage as theory of mind, suggesting a developmental linkage. Most influentially, Senju et al. (2007, *Biology Letters*) reported that children with autism spectrum disorder were significantly less susceptible to contagious yawning than neurotypical children, consistent with the theory that compromised social cognition reduces yawn contagion susceptibility.

The mirror neuron hypothesis was typically invoked to explain the proposed mechanism — the same neural system hypothesised to underlie our ability to represent others' mental states from their actions was proposed to also mediate the yawn-triggering response to observed yawning. This was a compelling narrative that fit neatly into a broader popular-neuroscience story about mirror neurons as the neural basis of empathy.

**The complications.** The story became substantially more complicated as replication and meta-analysis followed the early positive findings.

The autism correlation, the most behaviorally specific and consequential of the claims, has been repeatedly re-examined with inconsistent results. Giganti & Ziello (2009, *Current Psychology Letters*) replicated the reduced contagious yawning in autism. But Campbell & de Waal (2011, *PLOS ONE*) found that chimpanzees showed greater yawn contagion to in-group (familiar) yawns — suggesting that the effect might reflect social familiarity rather than empathy capacity. Highly et al.'s 2014 meta-analysis (*Autism Research*) found that while the direction of the autism-yawning effect was consistent across studies, the magnitude was small and the methodological variation between studies was substantial enough to raise concerns. Multiple subsequent independent studies failed to find the autism effect under double-blind conditions in which participants did not know the purpose of the study.

The empathy correlation fared similarly. The finding that higher self-reported empathy predicts greater yawn susceptibility has failed multiple independent replication attempts. Norscia et al. (2016, *PLOS ONE*) found that social bonds and the degree of relatedness explained more variance in yawn contagion than empathy scores did. Baron-Cohen's group produced mixed findings on the developmental timeline. A large preregistered replication by Bartholomew & Cirulli (2014, *PLOS ONE*) found no significant relationship between empathy scores, theory of mind performance, intelligence, or visual attention to the yawner's face and susceptibility to yawn contagion in a sample of 328 adults — far larger than most prior studies.

**What alternative explanations are in contention.** The current scientific debate involves several competing accounts, none of which has achieved clear dominance.

The *social bonding* account, supported by the Norscia et al. data, proposes that yawn contagion responds primarily to social closeness — we catch yawns from people we are bonded to, regardless of our general empathy capacity. This would predict stronger yawn contagion for kin and close friends than for strangers, which some data support.

The *perceptual release* account proposes that yawning is a contagion-susceptible behaviour primarily through perceptual priming rather than social cognition: observing the motor sequence of yawning activates the same motor program in the observer, with emotion and cognition playing little direct role. This would place yawn contagion in the same general category as contagious laughter, contagious coughing, and other reflexive imitative behaviours.

The *arousal regulation* hypothesis, advanced by Provine (2005, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*) and developed further by Gallup et al., proposes that yawning primarily serves a thermoregulatory function — brain cooling — and that contagious yawning is an adaptive social synchronisation mechanism that coordinates group arousal state, with empathy playing an incidental rather than constitutive role.

No single account currently commands consensus. The empathy-specific version of the claim is the one with the weakest current empirical support.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The empathy-yawning story achieved rapid popular penetration because it possessed several features that characterise neuroscience findings with exceptional viral potential in popular culture.

**The self-referential test.** When you read that catching someone's yawn indicates empathy, you are almost certainly yawning right now, or you yawned while reading the word "yawn" above. This immediate sensorimotor experience, occurring while you are reading a theoretical claim about itself, is a powerful confirmation event. The reader does not just learn the claim; they experience a version of it. This experiential confirmation bypasses normal sceptical gatekeeping because it seems to be direct evidence, even though what actually happened is entirely neutral between the empathy hypothesis and the perceptual-release hypothesis: you caught a yawn, which is well-documented, but the mechanism behind why you did is not demonstrated by the catching itself.

**The flattering implication structure.** Claims that attribute a common and positive social behaviour — being responsive to others — to a moral or emotional virtue (empathy) are reliably well-received and well-shared. The claim that yawning together with someone is emotionally meaningful, rather than reflexive, assigns significance to an experience that would otherwise be trivial. This flattering structure creates an incentive to share the claim regardless of its truth value. Similarly, the inverse claim — that not catching yawns may indicate reduced empathy — was widely reproduced in relationship advice and personality-assessment contexts, precisely because it felt diagnostic and illuminating.

**The neuroscience wrapper.** The specific claim arrived packaged in the prestige idiom of early-2000s neuroscience: mirror neurons, theory of mind, developmental correlations with autism, fMRI-compatible constructs. This packaging aligned the claim with a period of extremely high popular credibility for neuroscientific explanation. Legrenzi & Umiltà (2011, *Neuromania*, Oxford University Press) describe how the translation of social and psychological findings into neural terms substantially elevates their popular credibility independent of the quality of the underlying evidence. The yawning-empathy story arrived as a neuroscience story, not merely a psychology story, which conferred a level of apparent solidity that its actual evidentiary base did not justify.

**The epistemic novelty in §3 — the developmental confound and the replication asymmetry.** The empathy-yawning story is a useful case for illustrating what might be called the *specificity escalator* in popular science communication: findings from developmental comparisons (children with autism yawn less contagiously) are translated into claims about adults (empathic people catch yawns more), which are translated into claims about interpersonal relationships (catching your partner's yawn means you feel deep empathy with them). Each step in the escalator adds specificity and interpersonal relevance while moving further from the data and its limitations. The original Senju et al. finding concerned children with autism, studied in a specific paradigm; its translation into a claim about individual empathy variation in adults, and further into a relationship-diagnostic claim, involved conceptual jumps that were not warranted by the data and that subsequent research has not supported.

More structurally: the replication literature has a systematic asymmetry here. The original positive findings were published in high-profile journals because they were novel and surprising. The null and negative replications were published in less prominent venues, or were included in methodological meta-analyses rather than individual empirical reports. This publication asymmetry means that the scientific community's updating — away from the empathy-yaw link — has not translated into popular correction, partly because corrections from replications are not structured to generate the same cultural visibility as original positive findings.


## 4. Verdict

**Distorted but grounded.** Contagious yawning is real and the claim that it relates to social cognition has genuine empirical backing — but the popular version, which treats catching yawns as a reliable indicator of high empathy, substantially overstates the evidence and understates the failed replications. The most specific claims — particularly that autistic individuals are uniformly less susceptible to yawn contagion, and that individual empathy scores reliably predict yawn susceptibility — have not survived independent replication at scale. The social bonding account, which attributes yawn contagion primarily to familiarity and relationship closeness rather than trait empathy, currently has better empirical support than the empathy account but is itself preliminary. The underlying neuroscience — which proposed mirror neurons as the mechanism — rests on a body of work that has itself been substantially complicated by replication failures and methodological critique over the same period.

The claim should be categorised as distorted-but-grounded: there is a real phenomenon (contagious yawning), a plausible link to social cognition at some level, and genuine scholarly interest in the question — but the confident popular version, in which yawning together is a reliable barometer of empathy, is not currently supported by the research literature.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The contagious yawning story is simultaneously a story about popular science communication, the replication crisis, and the specific trajectory of mirror neuron science. All three intersect here.

The mirror neuron hypothesis as a general explanation for empathy, imitation, theory of mind, and social cognition was one of the dominant popular neuroscience narratives of the early-to-mid 2000s. Ramachandran famously described mirror neurons as doing "for psychology what DNA did for biology" — an endorsement that received enormous popular circulation. The hypothesis appeared in bestselling books, TED talks, and mainstream neuroscience coverage. When the mirror neuron mechanism was invoked to explain yawn contagion, it inherited that prestige.

Hickok (2014, *The Myth of Mirror Neurons*, W. W. Norton) provides the most thorough critical assessment of the evidentiary basis for mirror neuron explanations of high-level social cognition, arguing that the experimental evidence for the strong version of the hypothesis — that mirror neurons constitute the neural substrate for understanding others' intentions and emotions — is substantially weaker than popular coverage implies. The yawn-empathy story reflects this broader pattern: mechanistic neuroscience explanations for social phenomena achieved cultural acceptance far ahead of their actual experimental support.

The replication crisis, which became a major methodological controversy in psychology and cognitive neuroscience roughly after 2011, provides the background for understanding why the yawn-empathy finding looks different now than it did at its publication. Studies from the pre-registration era with small samples, researcher degrees of freedom in analysis, and publication bias toward positive results produced a literature that systematic replication has substantially deflated. The yawning literature is one example among many: the pattern of small initial studies finding significant effects that larger, better-controlled replications fail to reproduce has played out in numerous domains of social psychology and cognitive neuroscience simultaneously.

Dogs provide an interesting comparative angle. The finding that dogs are susceptible to contagious yawning from humans — reported by Joly-Mascheroni et al. (2008, *Biology Letters*) — was initially cited as evidence for a deep evolutionary history of the social-cognitive mechanism. Subsequent work has complicated this too: multiple replication attempts found either no effect or a much weaker effect that was sensitive to experimental context. Harr et al. (2009, *Animal Cognition*) failed to replicate the dog finding. The comparative work illustrates the difficulty of disentangling yawn contagion from social stress, arousal state, and habituation effects — confounds that also complicate the human literature.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes does not have a dedicated entry on this specific claim as of 2026. The claim has been addressed in popular science journalism, most notably in pieces by Neuroskeptic (2018, *Discover Magazine*) covering the failed replications and by criticism in *Slate* and *The Atlantic* that specifically tracked the translation of contested laboratory findings into confident popular-science narratives. Science communicators such as Ed Yong have written about the mirror neuron story and its complications, which contextualises the yawning claim within a broader pattern of over-confident neuroscience popularisation.

The claim presents a particular challenge for standard fact-checking because its status is "complicated and contested" rather than "clearly false." The popular version overstates a real finding, but the finding itself is real enough that a flat verdict of "false" would itself be misleading. This contested-but-real structure places the claim in a category that fact-check organisations typically handle less well than clearly false claims, because the nuanced verdict — "there may be something here but the popular version is substantially exaggerated and the specific empathy mechanism is poorly supported" — does not translate into shareable content as effectively as "true" or "false."

Readers engaging with this claim should be invited to think about what testing it would actually require. Feeling that you yaw more readily around people you are close to might be consistent with both the social bonding account and the empathy account, since people we are close to are also typically the people for whom we have the highest empathy. Disentangling these requires the kind of carefully controlled experimental setup that informal experience cannot provide. The correct epistemic response to the contagious yawning story is neither confident acceptance nor confident rejection, but an appreciation of what the existing evidence does and does not support — a more effortful position than the popular version, and correspondingly less viral.
