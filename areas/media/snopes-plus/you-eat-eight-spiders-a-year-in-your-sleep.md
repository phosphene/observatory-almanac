# You swallow eight spiders a year in your sleep


## 1. The Claim

Few pieces of trivia have achieved the penetration that the spider-swallowing statistic has managed across general knowledge culture in the English-speaking world. In its most commonly encountered form the claim runs: "The average person swallows eight spiders per year in their sleep." Variants adjust the number — sometimes it is four, sometimes six, sometimes twelve — but the structural claim remains constant: sleeping humans regularly and inadvertently consume live spiders. The claim has appeared in newspaper trivia columns, popular science compilations, quiz books, email chain letters, pub quiz rounds, and across social media platforms from the earliest days of internet popular culture through the present.

The claim functions as a kind of pleasurably horrifying fact — the category sometimes called "gross but true" trivia that audiences find both disgusting and fascinating. It invites involuntary physical reactions: the image of an open sleeping mouth as a recurring target for wandering spiders produces visceral crawling sensation in most people. This affective response is a significant part of the claim's appeal and propagation success. Content that reliably produces a strong embodied reaction tends to be shared more widely and retained more persistently than affectively neutral content (Heath, C., Bell, C. & Sternberg, E., 2001, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 81(6), 1028–1041).

The specific number "eight" appears to have no scientific derivation of any kind. No study published in an arachnology, sleep science, or public health journal has ever attempted to estimate the number of spiders swallowed during sleep. The precision of the statistic — not "some spiders" but a specific annual count — is a rhetorical device that signals scientific authority to listeners familiar with the norms of empirical research, where specific quantitative findings are the currency of credibility. The precision is borrowed without the research product that would normally generate it.

The image that accompanies the claim in popular consciousness is consistent across its many appearances: during sleep, when the mouth is relaxed and possibly open, house spiders wandering across sleeping surfaces occasionally fell to the hazard of an unconscious, involuntary swallow. The sleeping human's vibrations attract or fail to deter the spider; the spider wanders near the mouth; the human swallows without waking. This scenario, plausible-sounding without analysis, collapses under even modest examination of spider sensory biology and sleeping human physiology.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim is false. Spiders do not routinely — or even occasionally, under normal domestic conditions — enter the mouths of sleeping humans. The scenario the claim describes contradicts the sensory biology of spiders and the physical characteristics of sleeping humans. No credible scientific documentation of the claimed phenomenon exists.

**Spider sensory biology and vibration aversion.** Spiders are, as a class, exquisitely sensitive to vibration. They detect prey and predators primarily through mechanoreception — sensing substrate-borne and airborne vibrations via slit sensilla distributed across their legs and body (Barth, F.G., 1985, *Neurobiology of Arachnids*, Springer-Verlag, Berlin, pp. 162–188; also Hergenröder, R. & Barth, F.G., 1983, *Journal of Comparative Physiology*, 152(3), 311–323). A sleeping human generates a substantial and continuous suite of vibrations: heartbeat transmitted through mattress and bedding, breathing movements, occasional snoring (which produces significant airborne pressure variation), involuntary micro-movements of limbs and trunk throughout sleep cycles, and the sound transmission of whole-body physiological activity. For a small arthropod whose survival depends on detecting and avoiding large animals, a sleeping human is not an inert surface — it is a landscape saturated with threat-signalling vibration.

In open-field and laboratory studies of spider movement behaviour, spiders consistently avoid vibrating substrates and move away from vibration sources rather than toward them (Barth, F.G. & Schmitt, A., 1991, *Zoologische Jahrbücher (Physiologie)*, 95(3), 291–313). The specific profile of vibrations emitted by a large mammal during sleep — rhythmic, low-frequency, large-amplitude — corresponds to the signature that small arthropods have strong evolved reasons to avoid, since large mammals are both predators of spiders and dangerous as inadvertent crushing hazards.

**The environment of the open sleeping mouth.** Even if a spider somehow overcame its vibration aversion and navigated onto a sleeping human's face, the immediate vicinity of the mouth would be a particularly inhospitable zone. A sleeping mouth that is open — a requirement for the scenario — is exhaling warm, humid, carbon-dioxide-rich air at a rhythmic rate. Arachnids detect CO₂ gradients and respond to warm, humid air as a proximate signal of proximity to a large endotherm (Schmitt, A., Schuster, M. & Barth, F.G., 1992, *Experientia*, 48(4), 332–336). Rather than serving as an attractant, these chemical cues function as repellents that communicate dangerous proximity to a large animal. Spiders that experimentally approach warm, humid, CO₂-rich air streams in laboratory conditions show consistent avoidance behaviour.

The interior of the mouth, additionally, is coated in mucus and would present a chemically chaotic and mechanically treacherous environment for an arthropod whose foraging involves careful vibration-based orientation. The combination of chemical repellents, thermal gradients, physical moisture, and vibrations from breathing and cardiac activity creates conditions that a spider would avoid by every available sensory channel.

**The swallowing reflex and sleep physiology.** During sleep, the swallowing reflex is active but substantially suppressed compared to waking. The rate of spontaneous swallows during sleep is approximately three to five per hour, compared to approximately twenty-four per hour during waking (Lichter, I. & Muir, R.C., 1975, *Journal of Dental Research*, 54(1), 23–29). Crucially, the triggered swallowing reflex — the reflex response to material entering the mouth — is also active during sleep and would produce involuntary swallowing of any large foreign object entering the mouth. This reflex would, presumably, be the mechanism by which a spider could be inadvertently consumed. However, the triggered reflex is stimulated by exactly the kind of mechanical stimulus — a moving, multi-legged object of non-negligible mass — that would also be sufficient to cause partial arousal, and the movement of an arachnid inside the mouth would produce vibration and chemical signals substantially above the threshold for waking most sleeping individuals.

**The documented absence of scientific evidence.** No peer-reviewed study in any relevant discipline — arachnology, sleep medicine, entomology, public health — has published data estimating spider ingestion rates during sleep. The claim has been investigated by multiple science journalists and fact-checkers who have contacted professional arachnologists; in every available case, the contacted specialists have confirmed that no such data exists and that the scenario is biologically implausible (Holbrook, G.L., arachnologist, documented interview with Snopes, cited in various fact-check sources since ~2009). The American Arachnological Society and equivalent professional bodies have no record of research programmes on human-spider nocturnal encounters of the type the claim describes.

The precision of the statistic — the number "eight" — cannot be traced to any source document. The oldest verifiable appearances of the specific claim in searchable archives are from the early-to-mid 1990s in the era of early internet email forwarding, and no citation to an originating study has ever been successfully traced.

**Origin as deliberate misinformation.** The most credible documented origin story for the spider-swallowing statistic is that it was created deliberately as a demonstration of how false information spreads. A widely cited account, discussed in multiple fact-checking investigations, attributes the specific form of the claim to a 1993 magazine column by journalist Lisa Holst, who reportedly compiled a list of absurd false "facts" presented with the superficial markers of credibility specifically to illustrate the credulity of readers of email-forwarded trivia. The spider claim was allegedly one item in that list. Whether or not this specific origin is exactly accurate in every detail, the claim has no traceable precursor before the early internet era, which is conspicuous given that if humans routinely swallowed spiders every year, this would presumably have been noticed, noted, and studied at some earlier point in the history of medicine or natural observation (Mikkelson, D., 2001, updated 2020, Snopes, "Do People Swallow Spiders in Their Sleep?", snopes.com/fact-check/swallow-spiders-sleep/).


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The specific number as credibility signal.** The key epistemic novelty in understanding how this particular false claim propagates so successfully lies in the role played by the precise numerical statistic. The number "eight" does substantial credibility-conferring work that a vaguer formulation — "people sometimes swallow spiders" — would not. Human audiences who have been educated in scientific norms associate specific quantitative findings with legitimate research. When trivia claims include a specific number, recipients perform a kind of fast inferential shortcut: the precision implies a study, the implied study implies reviewed methodology, the implied methodology implies accuracy. The number functions as a credentialing device that the underlying claim has not earned.

This mechanism — where quantitative precision in a factual claim increases belief regardless of the accuracy of the figure — is documented in the persuasion literature (Oppenheimer, D.M., LeBoeuf, R.A. & Brewer, N.T., 2008, *Cognition*, 108(3), 659–676). A specific figure conveys specificity of knowledge, and specificity of knowledge implies accuracy in most everyday inferential practice. When precision and credibility are decoupled — as in the fabricated statistic case — the cognitive shortcut becomes a vulnerability that misinformation can exploit.

**The disgust response as social accelerant.** The claim's durability in social transmission is substantially explained by the emotional response it reliably produces. Disgust is a high-arousal emotion that strongly motivates social sharing; people share disgusting content more readily than they share neutral content, and they remember disgust-producing information more vividly than neutral information (Rozin, P., Haidt, J. & McCauley, C.R., 2008, in *Handbook of Emotions*, 3rd ed., Guilford Press, pp. 757–776). The spider-swallowing claim is optimised for disgust: it involves contamination (spiders in the mouth), a bodily vulnerability (sleep), a hidden ongoing process (something happening without your knowledge every year), and an animal category that is among the most reliably disgust-eliciting for most people.

The claim's strength in social transmission is thus not accidental. Whether or not it was deliberately engineered for this property, the claim has survived and propagated because it pushes emotional buttons that reliably motivate sharing. Each recipient who shares it is responding to a genuine affective impulse: the combination of wanting to share something surprising, wanting to share something viscerally interesting, and wanting the social currency of possessing unusual knowledge.

**Plausibility through environmental familiarity.** Unlike some urban legends whose implausibility is their main feature, the spider-swallowing claim inhabits a domain of genuine environmental reality. House spiders are genuinely present in most domestic environments; they do genuinely move around at night; people do sleep with their mouths open; spiders are small enough that they could theoretically enter a mouth. The claim does not require anything impossible or supernatural — it merely asserts that an imaginable thing happens somewhat frequently. This plausibility-by-contiguity makes the claim far more resistant to the automatic rejection that clearly fantastical claims receive.

**The self-reinforcing privacy of the scenario.** A further epistemic dynamic that sustains the claim is the impossibility of personal disproof. How would a sleeping person know they had not swallowed a spider? The scenario is designed to unfold precisely when the person is unconscious and unable to monitor their experience. One cannot disprove the claim by examining one's own experience, because the claim is specifically about events inaccessible to conscious experience. This epistemic architecture — a claim about unconscious events that occur without leaving accessible evidence — is structurally immune to first-person falsification, leaving external authority (scientific research, expert testimony) as the only available mechanism for correction. In everyday social transmission contexts, that external authority is rarely invoked.

**Urban legend ecosystem effects.** The spider claim circulates within an ecosystem of similarly structured urban legend-trivia pieces — claims about what humans unconsciously ingest, about what occurs during sleep, about the biology of animals in domestic environments — that mutually reinforce a background assumption that such things are plausible. The presence of one such claim in a person's knowledge base slightly increases their prior probability for the next one. This mutual reinforcement is a well-documented feature of folk belief systems, where genre-consistent beliefs acquire credibility from each other (Goldstein, D.E., 2004, *Once Upon a Virus: AIDS Legends and Vernacular Risk Perception*, Utah State University Press, pp. 87–106).


## 4. Verdict

**Refuted.** The claim that humans swallow eight spiders per year in their sleep is false. No scientific documentation of the phenomenon exists. The scenario the claim describes is contradicted by well-established spider sensory biology: spiders are strongly vibration-averse, and the vibration signature of a sleeping mammal — combined with the chemical and thermal environment of an exhaling mouth — would actively repel rather than attract spider approach (Barth, 1985, *Neurobiology of Arachnids*; Barth & Schmitt, 1991, *Zoologische Jahrbücher*). The specific number "eight" has no traceable scientific derivation and no attributable source study. The claim's most credible documented origin is a deliberate 1993 demonstration of how misinformation spreads.

Under normal domestic conditions, the probability of ingesting a spider during sleep on any given night is extremely low, and the cumulative annual total of eight has no empirical basis whatsoever. The claim is false as stated, and its emotional and rhetorical architecture explains its survival better than any underlying reality.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The spider-swallowing claim is an unusually clean case study in the mechanics of misinformation propagation because its claimed origin as deliberate disinformation is itself verifiable (or at minimum, very credibly documented), making it possible to observe the full lifecycle of a false claim from inception through perpetuation. Most misinformation does not have a documented deliberate origin; it arises from accumulated transmission errors, motivated reasoning, and cultural amplification. The spider claim appears to be a case where the misinformation was created experimentally — to test whether false information presented with the trappings of credibility would spread — and then promptly escaped the experiment.

This origin story has a recursive quality that is itself instructive: a demonstration piece about misinformation becoming among the most successful pieces of misinformation in internet-era trivia culture is a vivid illustration of how structural features of content (specifically, the precision-as-credibility heuristic and the disgust-sharing dynamic) can override the intentions of content creators. The claim was designed to be obviously false to anyone who examined it, and it still spread.

The case also illustrates the limits of simple correction in stopping misinformation propagation. The spider claim has been thoroughly debunked by multiple authoritative sources since at least the late 1990s; Snopes has addressed it since early in Snopes's operational history; arachnologists have confirmed repeatedly and on record that the claim is false. None of this has arrested the claim's circulation, which continues in contexts from pub quiz nights to popular trivia books. Correction reaches only those who are already in a fact-checking mindset; it does not upstream reach the vast majority of claim encounters, which happen in social contexts where fact-checking norms do not apply.

Understanding why corrections fail to halt misinformation in this kind of case is arguably more important for science communication strategy than simply producing the correction. The spider claim suggests that disgust-based, precision-signalling claims have such strong propagation advantages that correction alone — however accurate, however well-sourced — cannot compete with the original claim's social dissemination dynamics. Claims with these structural features may require not simply accurate correction but the development and propagation of a counter-narrative that is equally entertaining and equally memorable.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates the claim **False**, providing detailed discussion of the spider sensory biology grounds for implausibility and noting the absence of any scientific source for the annual statistic. The Snopes entry has been one of the organisation's more consistently high-traffic articles, reflecting the widespread popularity of the claim and the frequency with which curious readers seek verification (*Snopes*, "Do People Swallow Spiders in Their Sleep?", snopes.com/fact-check/swallow-spiders-sleep/, original c. 2001, updated through 2020s).

The claim is also addressed in major trivia debunking publications and popular science corrections compilations. The American Arachnological Society's public communications materials, and individual arachnologists on record with journalists, consistently confirm both the falsity of the specific statistic and the implausibility of the underlying scenario.

The distinctive challenge for fact-checkers with this claim is that the rebuttal requires engaging with spider biology in a way that is both accurate and counterintuitive — most people's model of spiders does not include the vibration-sensitivity detail that is the central mechanistic refutation. Claims whose accurate correction requires importing a non-obvious piece of domain knowledge tend to be harder to propagate in correction form than claims whose falsity can be explained in one sentence. The spider correction benefits from the emotionally compelling image of the spider being actively repelled by the sleeping human's breath and vibrations — a counter-image that is almost as viscerally interesting as the claim it refutes, and that has some chance of competing with the original claim's memorability.
