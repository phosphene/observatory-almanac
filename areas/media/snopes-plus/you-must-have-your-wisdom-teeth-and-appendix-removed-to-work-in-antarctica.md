## 1. The Claim

Anyone going to Antarctica is required — as a condition of deployment — to have their wisdom teeth removed and their appendix removed before they can work there. The medical logic, as typically given in popular accounts, is that there are no surgeons at Antarctic stations, emergency evacuation is impossible for much of the year due to weather and darkness, and an infected wisdom tooth or a ruptured appendix in the middle of a six-month polar winter would be fatal or medically catastrophic. Therefore, the policy is: remove any organ or structure that could cause emergency problems before you go. The appendix and wisdom teeth are the most commonly cited, but some versions of the claim extend the prophylactic removal logic to other structures that could cause emergency conditions.

This claim is frequently cited as one of the more striking and plausible-sounding "weird facts" about Antarctica — a category of claim that includes the size of the continent, the intensity of its cold, its treaty status, and the extreme conditions endured by researchers. It circulates in popular science media, in travel discussions, and online as an example of extraordinary requirements placed on Antarctic workers.

The claim has enough truth in it to resist a simple false verdict, but enough imprecision to require careful unpacking. The reality is more interesting and more historically specific than the clean universal statement most versions present.

## 2. What's Actually True

**The medical logic is sound.** Before evaluating the specific claim, the underlying medical reasoning deserves to be taken seriously. Antarctica presents genuinely exceptional medical challenges. Specifically, for overwinter personnel at stations where emergency medical evacuation is impossible:

The Antarctic winter lasts approximately six months (April to October in the Ross Sea area, corresponding to the Southern Hemisphere winter). During this period, the extreme cold — regularly below -40°C and occasionally approaching -80°C at inland stations — combined with perpetual darkness, ice conditions, and storm frequency makes air evacuation not merely difficult but functionally impossible for weeks or months at a time. The distance from medical facilities is extraordinary: the nearest major surgical hospital from McMurdo Sound is in Christchurch, New Zealand — a flight of approximately 5 hours under favorable conditions, but inaccessible for significant periods. From the South Pole (Amundsen-Scott Station), the situation is more extreme. From Russian Vostok Station, even more so.

Against this background, the prophylactic removal of tissue that could cause acute emergency conditions has clear medical logic. Appendicitis — inflammation of the vermiform appendix, typically caused by obstruction — is the most compelling case: it affects approximately 7–8 per 10,000 people per year; it progresses rapidly from inflammation to perforation, peritonitis, and death without surgical intervention (typically within 24–72 hours of perforation); and it cannot be reliably managed with antibiotics alone in all cases, though evidence on antibiotic management has evolved significantly (Salminen, P. et al., 2015, "Antibiotic therapy vs. appendectomy for treatment of uncomplicated acute appendicitis," *JAMA*). If a researcher at an overwinter station develops appendicitis in July, and evacuation is impossible, medical management is the only option — and in the absence of a surgeon and surgical facilities, that management is purely pharmaceutical and extremely high-risk.

Wisdom teeth present a somewhat different but related risk: impacted or partially erupted wisdom teeth are prone to pericoronitis (infection around the crown), abscess formation, and other dental emergencies. Dental emergencies in an environment without a dentist or oral surgeon could rapidly become serious — particularly abscesses, which can spread to the jaw, neck, and potentially the airway. Prophylactic removal of problematic wisdom teeth reduces that risk.

**What the actual policies are.** The specific requirements vary considerably by national Antarctic program and by role:

*Soviet and Russian Antarctic programs*: The most documented and stringent requirements historically have come from the Soviet and later Russian Antarctic programs. According to multiple accounts, Soviet station doctors performing overwinter assignments were required to have their appendixes removed before deployment. This appears to have been an explicit medical screening requirement, not merely a recommendation. Vladislav Rogozov, the surgeon who famously performed his own appendectomy at Novolazarevskaya station in 1961 when he developed appendicitis during the winter, is the most celebrated case in polar medical history — and is often cited as the event that reinforced the Soviet policy of prophylactic appendectomy for overwinter personnel (Miles, S., 2009, "Nik Prokopov tells the story of Leonid Rogozov," *Polar Record*). Rogozov's operation succeeded, but the extreme need it demonstrated was exactly what the policy aimed to prevent.

*United States Antarctic Program (USAP)*: The United States Antarctic Program, administered by the National Science Foundation, has a medical screening process that is detailed and rigorous but does not mandate prophylactic appendectomy for all personnel. Instead, the USAP medical screening looks for existing conditions that could cause emergencies in Antarctica — including problematic wisdom teeth and any appendix-related history — and may recommend or require treatment of those conditions before deployment. The policy has changed over time and differs by role: overwinter personnel at remote sites face stricter screening than summer-only personnel at McMurdo Station (which maintains 24/7 medical coverage during the summer season). NSF's medical screening guidelines are not fully public in their current form, but documents and accounts from participants confirm that dental clearance (including wisdom tooth evaluation) and general medical fitness screening are mandatory, and that conditions identified as deployment risks may need to be resolved before clearance is granted.

*British, German, Australian, New Zealand, and other programs*: Similar risk-tiered approaches apply across many national programs. The British Antarctic Survey (BAS) is known for thorough medical and dental screening, with documented requirements that include addressing impacted wisdom teeth and any other conditions that could require emergency intervention. BAS medical guidelines available through their website explicitly note that dental treatment including impacted wisdom tooth extraction may be required prior to deployment to remote sites.

*McMurdo Station vs. remote sites*: A key distinction that popular accounts collapse is the difference between McMurdo (the US's main Antarctic hub, with a hospital, medical officers, and summer-season evacuation access) and the interior stations — South Pole, Vostok, Concordia, and smaller remote camps. The medical risk for a summer-only researcher at McMurdo is substantially different from an overwinter researcher at the South Pole. The prophylactic requirements track this risk gradient, but many popular accounts describe the requirement as universal rather than role- and program-dependent.

**The Rogozov case.** The 1961 self-appendectomy performed by Leonid Rogozov is historically significant and relevant here. Rogozov was the only physician at Novolazarevskaya station when he developed appendicitis in April 1961. Weather made evacuation impossible. He performed his own appendectomy under local anesthesia with two non-medical assistants holding instruments and a small mirror for reflection. He survived. The operation took approximately one hour and 45 minutes. His report was published in *Soviet Antarctica* and later in English medical literature (Miles, S., 2009, *Polar Record*; Rogozov, L., 1964, "I did it myself," *Antarctic* [first English account]). The case is real, documented, and remarkable — and it is exactly the scenario that prophylactic appendectomy policies aim to prevent.

**Current state of the appendicitis treatment question.** One layer of complexity the claim ignores is the evolving medical evidence on whether appendectomy is always necessary for appendicitis. A series of high-quality randomized controlled trials over the past decade have tested antibiotic-first management of uncomplicated appendicitis against surgery. The APPAC trial (Salminen et al., 2015, *JAMA*) found that among patients with CT-confirmed uncomplicated appendicitis, 73% of the antibiotic group were successfully managed without surgery at five years, but a significant minority progressed and required surgery. Subsequent meta-analyses have confirmed that antibiotics are a viable management option for uncomplicated appendicitis in reliable healthcare settings but that surgery remains the standard for complicated or pediatric appendicitis. In the Antarctic context, the caveat "in reliable healthcare settings" is critical — a remote Antarctic station does not have imaging capability to distinguish complicated from uncomplicated presentations. This uncertainty maintains the logic of prophylactic removal for highest-risk deployments even in the era of antibiotic-first management.

## 3. Why People Believe This

**The claim has a genuine evidential basis.** Unlike many urban legends, the wisdom-teeth-and-appendix claim is not invented. It is a simplification — specifically, a universalization — of a real policy that applies in some contexts to some personnel. This is an unusual epistemic position for a claim that receives a "mixture" verdict: the underlying facts are true and well-documented; what is wrong is the scope and universality of the popular statement.

**Epistemic novelty: Antarctica as an epistemological mirror.** Antarctica has an unusual epistemic status in popular culture: it is genuinely alien in ways that most places are not. The scale of its cold, the duration of its darkness, the completeness of its isolation, and the treaty structures that govern it are all surprising to most people. When people hear unusual facts about Antarctica, those facts have unusually high prior credibility because the context is itself unusual. A fact that would seem implausible about New Zealand — "you need to have your appendix removed before working there" — is immediately plausible about Antarctica because the general category of "strange Antarctic requirements" is well-established. The claim benefits from a valid epistemic general rule applied to a specific instance that is partially, but not universally, true.

This is the epistemic novelty of the Antarctic claim: it demonstrates how the *plausibility prior* for a claim is context-dependent. The same mechanism that allows us to correctly believe extraordinary things about extraordinary places ("Antarctica is cold enough to freeze-dry unprotected skin in minutes") also allows us to over-extend the plausibility benefit to claims that are true in limited form but false in their universal version. We are correctly calibrated on "Antarctica is extreme" and incorrectly calibrated on "therefore all Antarctic requirements are maximally extreme."

**The Rogozov effect.** The Rogozov self-appendectomy is one of the most compelling medical stories of the twentieth century and has been widely circulated. It appears in polar history books, medical ethics literature, discussions of human endurance, and popular science journalism. The story is haunting — a man performing surgery on himself in the Antarctic darkness — and it is precisely the kind of story that embeds itself in cultural memory. Once people know the Rogozov case, the claim that Antarctica requires prophylactic appendectomy becomes not merely plausible but *explained*: the requirement exists because of cases like this. The causal story is so compelling that it anchors belief in the specific policy claim even without independent verification of the policy's universal scope.

**The collapsing of programs and roles.** Antarctica has many overlapping national programs with different policies, significant variation between summer and winter deployment, and major differences between stations based on remoteness and medical facility availability. Most people who encounter the claim do not know this institutional landscape. The phrase "working in Antarctica" is treated as a single category, and whatever the most extreme version of the Antarctic employment requirement is gets attributed to the category as a whole. This is a form of reference class collapse: "the Antarctic requirement" — taken as a unified, universal policy — is constructed from the most stringent version of the most demanding programs and attributed to the whole.

**Media framing as "weird facts."** The wisdom-teeth-and-appendix claim circulates primarily as a "weird facts" type item — it appears in listicles, trivia programs, and casual conversation precisely because it is surprising and striking. This framing creates a characteristic type of misencoding: the claim is received as an interesting curiosity rather than as a claim requiring source scrutiny. Weird facts are processed differently from claims with high personal stakes. People do not apply the same skepticism to a trivia item about Antarctica that they would apply to a medical claim about their own health. The social function of the claim — to be interesting and shareable — conflicts with the epistemic work required to evaluate it carefully.

## 4. Verdict

**Mixture** — historically real for specific roles and programs, not universally required.

The claim that all Antarctica workers must prophylactically remove their wisdom teeth and appendix before deployment is an oversimplification of a real policy that has applied — and to some degree continues to apply — to overwinter personnel at remote Antarctic stations, most consistently in the Soviet and Russian Antarctic programs. The medical logic is sound. The most dramatic illustrating case (Rogozov) is documented and real.

The claim is false as a universal requirement for all Antarctic workers today. Summer-only personnel at well-staffed stations like McMurdo do not face mandatory prophylactic appendectomy requirements. Many national programs approach the issue through risk-based screening rather than mandatory removal — identifying and treating conditions that pose elevated risk, not universally removing tissue from all deployees.

The honest characterization: the prophylactic removal requirement is real, documented, historically grounded, medically reasonable, and currently in force for at least some categories of Antarctic personnel. It is folklore to say it applies to all Antarctic workers in all contexts. The kernel of truth is substantial and interesting on its own terms.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**Polar medicine as a field.** The medical challenges of polar research stations have generated a genuine specialized field: expedition and polar medicine. The literature includes detailed protocols for managing surgical emergencies at remote sites, pharmacological alternatives to surgery, telemedicine consultation frameworks, and case studies of medical emergencies at Antarctic stations. The *Wilderness & Environmental Medicine* journal and the *International Journal of Circumpolar Health* carry relevant research. Major Antarctic programs publish their medical screening requirements, and the British Antarctic Survey has done notable work on the psychosocial challenges of overwinter expeditions (Lugg, D. J., 2000, "Behavioral health in Antarctica: implications for long-duration space missions," *Aviation, Space, and Environmental Medicine*; Stuster, J., 2010, "Behavioral issues associated with long-duration space expeditions," NASA Technical Report).

The appendectomy requirement sits within a broader framework of pre-deployment medical screening that addresses a range of conditions: cardiovascular disease, dental health, psychiatric fitness, sleep disorders, and visual acuity are all evaluated because the Antarctic environment makes management of these conditions difficult and evacuation in crisis potentially impossible. The wisdom teeth and appendix are not exceptional within this framework — they are salient examples of a systematic risk-reduction approach.

**The counterfactual: what happens if you don't.** The logic of prophylactic removal is strengthened by examining cases where it did not apply. Rogozov's case is the most famous. There are others. A 1999 case involved Jerri Nielsen, an American physician overwinter at the South Pole who discovered during the winter that she had breast cancer, required chemotherapy supplies to be airdropped during extreme conditions, and ultimately required winter evacuation — a genuinely life-threatening operation that involved aircraft landing at the South Pole in conditions near the operational limits (Nielsen, J., 2001, *Ice Bound*, Hyperion). That case does not involve appendectomy or dental surgery, but it illustrates the same underlying dynamic: when medical emergencies emerge during Antarctic winter, the response options are severely constrained, dangerous, and expensive.

The rational risk reduction logic of prophylactic removal is also interesting from a bioethics perspective: it involves inducing a medical procedure (surgery) on a healthy person to reduce the probability of a future emergency. This is prophylactic surgery — a category with a complex ethical history, including debates about prophylactic mastectomies for BRCA carriers and prophylactic colectomies in familial adenomatous polyposis. In the Antarctic context, the asymmetry between surgical risk (low for appendectomy and wisdom tooth extraction under normal hospital conditions) and emergency risk (potentially fatal in an Antarctic winter with no surgical facilities) makes the calculus straightforwardly favorable to prophylaxis. What is a minor elective procedure in a hospital becomes a risk-elimination decision in the context of extreme isolation.

**Antarctica as laboratory for extreme-environment medicine.** The medical challenges of Antarctic deployment have become a reference model for long-duration space missions, particularly missions to Mars, which will involve even more complete isolation from emergency medical support for even longer periods. NASA and international space agencies have examined Antarctic medical protocols explicitly when designing medical requirements for long-duration missions (Stuster, 2010; Lugg, 2000). The Antarctic prophylactic surgery requirement has influenced thinking about what pre-mission medical preparations might be required for Mars missions — where a medical evacuation to Earth would take months, not hours or days. In this context, the Antarctic baseline becomes a minimum for the space medicine challenge.

**Appendicitis epidemiology and remote populations.** The lifetime risk of appendicitis in developed countries is approximately 8–9% (Addiss, D. G. et al., 1990, "The epidemiology of appendicitis and appendectomy in the United States," *American Journal of Epidemiology*). For an overwinter expedition team of 10–20 people staying for six months, the statistical probability of one member developing appendicitis is not negligible — in rough terms, approximately 0.4–0.8% per person per six months, or somewhere in the range of 4–16% chance that at least one person in a 10-person team will develop appendicitis during an 18-month rotation. This is a known risk that informed deployment of prophylactic policy, and the probability is high enough — given the potentially fatal consequence of an untreated case — that the precautionary logic is medically defensible.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates this claim as a "mixture," which is the correct verdict at the level of policy accuracy. The Snopes entry distinguishes between historical Russian requirements (where the appendectomy policy is documented) and current universal requirements (not accurate), and notes that dental clearance, potentially including wisdom tooth evaluation, is part of Antarctic screening in most programs.

Popular science outlets including *Popular Science*, *Smithsonian Magazine*, and *Atlas Obscura* have covered the claim in the context of "strange facts about Antarctica" and most have reached the same nuanced conclusion: the claim is true enough to be interesting but requires qualification about which programs, which roles, and which time periods.

What is generally underemphasized in popular fact-checking is the specific institutional variation — the difference between the Russian and US programs historically, the difference between overwinter and summer-only deployment, and the role of the Rogozov case in shaping Soviet policy specifically. The forensic quality of the historical record — who required what, when, on whose orders, and with what documented justification — is available in the polar medicine literature but rarely synthesized in popular treatments.

The claim also serves as a good case study in the "mixture" verdict category more broadly: claims that are true in a specific historical or contextual scope but presented as universal truths are some of the most epistemically interesting cases for fact-checkers, because simple true/false evaluation misses the structure of what is actually being claimed and what is actually accurate. The Antarctica claim invites reflection on how we evaluate scope-dependent truths — claims that are accurate under certain conditions but not others — and how those conditions should be specified in the claim itself.
