# Your Brain's Built-In Biases Insulate Your Beliefs from Contradictory Facts

*A Truth Vault analysis of a Snopes/Conversation article about cognitive bias — and the recursive problem of writing about the limits of human reasoning while being subject to those same limits.*

## The Claim

In December 2020, at the confluence of a pandemic and a contested presidential election, Snopes republished an article from The Conversation by Jay Maddock, a professor of public health at Texas A&M University. The article's central argument is structural and sweeping: the human brain possesses built-in cognitive biases — availability bias, confirmation bias, cognitive dissonance — that systematically protect existing beliefs from contradictory evidence. These biases don't operate in isolation. They are amplified by group affiliation, reinforced by media echo chambers, and deepened by the Dunning-Kruger effect, which leaves people overconfident in the very knowledge that's most deficient.

Maddock illustrates the argument with personal experience. As chair of the Hawaii Board of Health, he was privy to the verification of Barack Obama's birth certificate, yet the birther movement persisted. On a podcast appearance, an anti-vaccine caller told him flatly that no amount of scientific evidence could change her mind. These anecdotes anchor a familiar litany of psychological mechanisms: Leon Festinger's cognitive dissonance, Keith Stanovich's concept of "my-side bias" (sometimes rendered "myside bias"), the availability heuristic described by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, and the filter bubbles created by algorithmically curated media diets.

The article's prescription is modest: practice arguing the opposing side, cultivate intellectual humility, seek out contradictory information. Its implicit claim is larger: the reason people resist facts is fundamentally cognitive. The architecture of the brain, not the architecture of society, is the primary obstacle to rational belief updating.

This is a claim worth examining with precision — not because it's wrong, but because an article about cognitive bias is a uniquely demanding test case for any epistemological framework. The article is, in a sense, about itself. And so are we.

## What's Actually True

Let us begin with what the science actually supports, which is considerable.

**Cognitive dissonance** is among the most replicated findings in social psychology. Festinger's original 1957 formulation — that holding contradictory cognitions produces psychological discomfort, motivating people to reduce the inconsistency — has survived six decades of empirical scrutiny. The forced-compliance paradigm (Festinger & Carlsmith, 1959), in which participants paid a trivial sum to lie about a boring task subsequently rated the task more enjoyable than those paid generously, has been replicated across cultures and experimental designs. Meta-analyses (e.g., Hinojosa et al., 2017) confirm the basic effect, even as the precise mechanism remains debated. Whether dissonance is fundamentally about self-consistency (Aronson, 1969), self-affirmation (Steele, 1988), or action-based cognitive reorientation (Harmon-Jones & Harmon-Jones, 2002) matters for theory but not for the practical observation: people do experience discomfort when confronted with belief-contradicting evidence, and they do sometimes resolve that discomfort by rejecting the evidence rather than updating the belief.

**Confirmation bias** has an even deeper empirical foundation. Peter Wason's classic selection task (1968) demonstrated that people test hypotheses by seeking confirmatory rather than disconfirmatory evidence. Subsequent work has shown this tendency operates across domains: legal reasoning (Ask & Granhag, 2005), medical diagnosis (Mendel et al., 2011), scientific hypothesis testing (Mynatt, Doherty & Tweney, 1977), and political information processing (Taber & Lodge, 2006). The effect is robust. People preferentially seek, interpret, and remember information that supports their existing beliefs. Stanovich's "myside bias" concept sharpens this further: unlike most cognitive biases, myside bias shows essentially no correlation with intelligence. Smart people are no better at reasoning about topics where they have existing commitments. In some studies, they're worse — better reasoning skills simply enable more sophisticated rationalization.

**The availability heuristic** (Tversky & Kahneman, 1973) is foundational to behavioral economics and well-supported. People estimate the frequency and probability of events based on how easily examples come to mind, which systematically distorts risk perception. The Dunning-Kruger effect (Kruger & Dunning, 1999) — the finding that people with low competence in a domain tend to overestimate their ability disproportionately — has been replicated, though its interpretation has been contested (more on which below).

So Maddock's article builds on solid empirical ground. The cognitive biases he describes are real, well-documented, and consequential.

But "well-documented" is not the same as "well-characterized," and this is where the article's popular framing departs from the science in ways that matter.

**The Bayesian counterargument.** A sophisticated epistemological tradition, rooted in Bayesian probability theory, argues that not all evidence-discounting is irrational. When someone encounters a single anecdote or study that contradicts a well-established belief, discounting that evidence can be the correct Bayesian response. Prior probability matters. If you have strong prior evidence for a claim (accumulated from decades of personal experience, education, and reliable testimony), a single contradictory data point should not — rationally — overturn that belief. It should update it incrementally. The distinction between updating incrementally and refusing to update at all is crucial, and it maps precisely onto the difference between rational conservatism and pathological belief persistence. Maddock's article collapses this distinction entirely.

**Motivated reasoning versus rational discounting.** Dan Kahan's work on cultural cognition (Kahan et al., 2011) demonstrates that people's evaluation of empirical evidence is filtered through cultural worldviews. But Kahan himself has been careful to note that this doesn't necessarily mean people are irrational — it may mean they are rationally weighing the social costs of belief change. If accepting climate science means alienating your community, your livelihood, and your identity, then the "bias" against accepting it isn't purely cognitive. It's a rational response to real social incentives. This doesn't make the belief correct, but it reframes the problem from "broken brains" to "competing rationalities."

**The replication crisis.** Some bias research has not fared well in the replication crisis that has swept psychology since 2011. The Dunning-Kruger effect, in particular, has been the subject of serious methodological critique. Gignac and Zajenkowski (2020) argued that much of the apparent effect is a statistical artifact of regression to the mean and bounded scales. The core observation — that people with low competence overestimate their ability — survives, but in attenuated form, and the dramatic visualizations commonly attached to the concept (the famous "Mount Stupid" graph) overstate the evidence. Priming effects, ego depletion, and several other bias-adjacent findings have proven difficult to replicate. This doesn't invalidate the field, but it counsels epistemic caution about any popular article treating cognitive bias research as settled and monolithic.

**Debiasing evidence.** Perhaps the most consequential omission in Maddock's article is the more optimistic debiasing literature. Research by Lilienfeld, Ammirati, and David (2012) catalogued conditions under which people can and do overcome cognitive biases. Structured analytical techniques, adversarial collaboration, prediction markets, and explicit training in probabilistic reasoning have all demonstrated measurable effects. The picture is messier and more hopeful than "your brain is broken." Some biases are remarkably tractable. Others are resistant. The conditions matter enormously. An article that presents bias as an architectural feature of the brain — unyielding without Herculean effort — understates the evidence for cognitive flexibility and trainability.

**The bias blind spot.** The most recursive problem in Maddock's framing is the one he doesn't address: the bias blind spot (Pronin, Lin & Ross, 2002). People are substantially better at identifying cognitive bias in others than in themselves. This asymmetry is itself a cognitive bias, and it is directly relevant to any article that explains why *other people* resist facts. The article implicitly positions its reader as a rational agent observing irrational others — precisely the cognitive posture that bias blind spot research identifies as itself biased. This is not a minor omission. It is the central epistemological problem of writing about bias for a popular audience.

## Why People Believe This

The "your brain is broken" frame is enormously appealing, and understanding why requires the same psychological tools the article deploys.

**Explanatory comfort.** Cognitive bias provides a satisfyingly mechanistic explanation for why other people disagree with you. It converts a genuinely difficult problem — "this person has access to the same evidence I do and reaches a different conclusion; might they have reasons I haven't considered?" — into a simple one: "their cognitive machinery is malfunctioning." This is comforting. It preserves your own epistemic position while explaining away the opposition. It is also, as noted above, a textbook demonstration of myside bias — the very phenomenon the article describes.

**The asymmetric deployment of bias awareness.** Cognitive bias research has become a cultural resource deployed asymmetrically in political and social discourse. In practice, people rarely use bias awareness reflexively ("I might be exhibiting confirmation bias here"). They use it attributionally ("You're exhibiting confirmation bias"). This asymmetry is not accidental. It follows directly from the bias blind spot: we see our own reasoning as objective and others' as distorted. The popular framing of bias research — including Maddock's article — facilitates this asymmetric deployment by presenting bias as something that happens to people (implicitly, other people) rather than as a universal condition that includes the reader, the author, and the institution publishing the article.

**The political moment.** Maddock's article was published on December 1, 2020 — three weeks after the presidential election, in the midst of unprecedented election denial. The article does not explicitly address this context, but its timing makes the subtext unmistakable. It functioned, in the discourse ecosystem, as an explanation for why Trump supporters refused to accept the election results. This is not to say the explanation is wrong — cognitive bias research is indeed relevant to understanding election denial. But the timing reveals the article's rhetorical function: it serves the cognitive needs of one political cohort (explaining the other side's intransigence) while ostensibly presenting neutral science. The article's popularity — it was widely shared on social media — correlates with its utility as a socially acceptable way to say "they're irrational and we're not."

**The appeal to psychologists.** It is worth noting that articles about cognitive bias are also self-serving for the field that produces them. Public engagement through popular science writing is increasingly expected of academics, and cognitive bias offers a uniquely satisfying genre: the research is accessible, counterintuitive enough to be interesting, and flattering to the reader (who is implicitly positioned as someone sophisticated enough to understand their own cognitive limitations — or, more commonly, other people's). This doesn't make the research wrong, but it does mean the selection of which findings get popular treatment and how they're framed is itself subject to professional incentive structures that bias research is uniquely positioned to illuminate but rarely does.

**Belief identity fusion.** Maddock correctly identifies that beliefs central to identity are hardest to change. But the mechanism he describes — that self-concept anchors beliefs — is itself part of a larger phenomenon. In a culture where political beliefs function as primary identity markers (what Lilliana Mason calls "mega-identities"), the social cost of belief change extends far beyond cognitive discomfort. It can mean community exile, family rupture, and professional consequence. This social reality means that what looks like cognitive stubbornness from the outside may be, from the inside, a rational assessment of what belief change would cost. The brain is not simply "protecting" the belief. It is protecting the social infrastructure that surrounds the belief, which is a more complex and arguably more rational process than Maddock's framing suggests.

## Verdict

The core science is sound. Cognitive biases are real, well-documented, and consequential. The availability heuristic, confirmation bias, cognitive dissonance, and the Dunning-Kruger effect are established phenomena in the psychological literature, though their boundaries, mechanisms, and magnitudes are debated more actively than popular treatments suggest.

But the popular framing introduces its own distortions, and in an article about distorted reasoning, those distortions carry particular weight:

**It's more deterministic than the evidence warrants.** The article presents cognitive bias as an architectural feature of the brain — something you're stuck with, amenable only to the modest interventions of arguing the other side and practicing humility. The debiasing literature is substantially more optimistic. Under the right conditions — structured incentives, diverse teams, explicit probability training, skin-in-the-game accountability — people can and do update beliefs in response to evidence. The variability matters as much as the tendency.

**It skips rational reasons for belief persistence.** Not all evidence-discounting is bias. Sometimes prior evidence legitimately outweighs a single contradictory finding. The Bayesian framework provides rigorous tools for distinguishing rational conservatism from pathological resistance, but the article makes no such distinction. This omission matters because it implies that anyone who doesn't immediately update their beliefs in response to new evidence is cognitively deficient — a characterization that is itself epistemologically unsound.

**It can be weaponized to dismiss legitimate disagreement.** When "you're exhibiting cognitive bias" becomes a conversational move, it functions identically to "you're irrational" — it delegitimizes the other person's reasoning without engaging their actual arguments. This weaponization is not a misuse of the research; it's a predictable consequence of the popular framing. An article that explains how other people's biases prevent them from accepting facts, without equally exploring how the reader's biases shape their interpretation of the same evidence, is handing its audience a cognitive weapon while calling it a mirror.

**It demonstrates the bias blind spot it doesn't discuss.** The article's most significant epistemological failure is the one it can't see: it positions itself outside the phenomena it describes. The author is the rational observer; the birthers and anti-vaxxers are the biased subjects. This is precisely the asymmetry that the bias blind spot literature predicts and documents. An article about cognitive bias that doesn't address its own susceptibility to bias is, at minimum, incomplete. At maximum, it is performatively self-refuting.

**Our rating: TRUE but INCOMPLETE in ways that matter epistemologically.** The factual claims about cognitive bias are well-supported. The framing is reductive. And the omissions — Bayesian rational conservatism, the debiasing literature, the bias blind spot, the social rationality of belief persistence — are not minor gaps but structural weaknesses that transform a science communication piece into something closer to a partisan epistemological tool. The article is factually accurate and epistemologically shallow, which is itself a kind of truth failure that fact-checking frameworks are poorly equipped to classify.

## The Wider Picture

This article sits at the center of a recursive epistemological problem that the Truth Vault exists to explore: can knowing about the limits of cognition actually help us think better? The answer, drawn from several decades of research, is: *it depends, and less than you'd hope*.

### The Socratic Paradox of Bias

There is a venerable philosophical tradition — stretching from Socrates' assertion that recognizing your own ignorance is the beginning of wisdom, through Kant's critiques of pure reason, to contemporary metacognition research — that suggests self-knowledge is epistemically empowering. If you know that you're prone to confirmation bias, you should be able to correct for it. This is the implicit promise of every popular article about cognitive bias: awareness leads to correction.

The evidence is more ambiguous. Pronin and colleagues (2002, 2004) have shown repeatedly that people can learn to identify biases in abstract scenarios while remaining blind to identical biases in their own reasoning. The specific problem is what psychologists call the "introspection illusion" — the belief that you can access the processes that produce your judgments. Even after training in bias recognition, people tend to conclude that they've corrected for their own biases (based on introspective confidence) while others haven't. The net effect of bias education, in some experimental conditions, is not reduced bias but increased confidence in one's own objectivity — precisely the opposite of the intended outcome.

This doesn't mean bias education is worthless. It means that the popular model — learn about bias, then compensate for it through willpower — is insufficiently structural. The interventions that work (prediction markets, adversarial collaboration, pre-commitment to decision rules, structured analytic techniques) work precisely because they don't rely on introspective correction. They change the architecture of the decision environment rather than asking individuals to override their cognitive architecture through sheer awareness. The article's recommended solutions — argue the other side, practice humility — are closer to the introspective model that the evidence suggests is weakest. This is a case where the prescription undermines the diagnosis.

### Prior Beliefs and Bayesian Rationality

The deepest challenge to the "bias as deficiency" model comes from Bayesian epistemology. In a Bayesian framework, rational belief updating depends on three things: the prior probability you assign to a hypothesis, the likelihood of observing the evidence if the hypothesis is true, and the likelihood of observing the evidence if the hypothesis is false. A critical implication: if your priors are strong (based on extensive prior evidence), a single contradictory data point should produce only a small update. And if the source of the contradictory evidence is unreliable by your assessment, the rational update may be negligible.

This framework provides a rigorous account of why reasonable people can examine the same evidence and reach different conclusions: they start with different priors, based on different life experiences, different evidentiary histories, and different assessments of source reliability. What looks like confirmation bias from the outside may, from the inside, be perfectly rational Bayesian conservatism. The challenge, naturally, is distinguishing the two — and neither Maddock's article nor most popular treatments of cognitive bias provide the tools to do so.

Consider Maddock's anti-vaccine caller. She said no amount of evidence would change her mind. This is, on its face, irrational — it's a prior probability of 1.0, which cannot be updated by Bayes' theorem. But most people who say "nothing could change my mind" don't literally mean it. They mean: "within the range of evidence I consider credible and the sources I consider reliable, I don't expect to encounter anything that would shift my view substantially." This is a statement about the expected information environment, not about logical impossibility. It may still be wrong — the caller may be discounting genuinely probative evidence — but it's a more nuanced kind of wrong than "her brain is broken."

### The Replication Crisis and Epistemic Humility

There is a deep irony in citing cognitive bias research as settled science during a period when psychology is undergoing a painful reckoning with its own methodological biases. The replication crisis has revealed that publication bias (the tendency to publish positive results), p-hacking (the practice of manipulating analyses until statistically significant results emerge), and HARKing (hypothesizing after results are known) have distorted the published literature in ways that are still being quantified. Some landmark findings in social psychology — ego depletion, social priming, power posing — have failed to replicate.

The cognitive bias findings cited by Maddock are among the more robust in the literature. Confirmation bias, cognitive dissonance, and the availability heuristic have survived replication attempts reasonably well, though effect sizes are often smaller than originally reported. The Dunning-Kruger effect is more contested — Gignac and Zajenkowski (2020) demonstrated that much of the apparent effect can be explained by regression to the mean combined with bounded measurement scales, and Nuhfer and colleagues (2016, 2017) found that self-assessment accuracy was substantially better than the original formulation suggested when measurement issues were addressed.

The epistemically honest position is: cognitive biases are real but their magnitudes are uncertain, their boundary conditions are poorly mapped, and the popular representations typically overstate both their severity and their universality. This uncertainty doesn't help the popular science genre, which thrives on clean narratives and dramatic findings. But for an epistemological framework concerned with truth, the uncertainty is the point. Presenting cognitive bias research with more confidence than the evidence warrants is itself a form of the overconfidence bias documented in the same literature.

### Filter Bubbles: More Complicated Than Advertised

Maddock cites the decline of mass media and the rise of social media as creating filter bubbles that insulate people from contradictory information. This claim, popularized by Eli Pariser's 2011 book *The Filter Bubble*, has been substantially complicated by subsequent research. Guess and colleagues (2023) found that exposure to ideologically cross-cutting content on Facebook had minimal effects on political attitudes, and in some conditions actually increased polarization. Barberá and colleagues (2015) found that social media users are exposed to more ideologically diverse content than popular accounts suggest — the issue is not lack of exposure but selective attention and motivated interpretation.

The filter bubble thesis also underestimates the role of active choice versus algorithmic curation. People have always sought out information that confirms their beliefs — they chose their newspapers, their neighborhoods, their friends accordingly. What's changed is not the impulse but the efficiency with which it can be satisfied. This distinction matters because it shifts the diagnosis from "technology is breaking our brains" to "technology is making existing cognitive tendencies easier to satisfy" — a framing that implies different interventions and a less deterministic outlook.

### The Weaponization of Bias Awareness

Perhaps the most consequential development since Maddock's article has been the weaponization of cognitive bias language in political and cultural discourse. "That's just your confirmation bias" has become a conversational move that functions like older dismissals — "you're brainwashed," "you've been propagandized" — while carrying the authority of science. This weaponization is particularly insidious because it's technically true: the person probably is exhibiting some degree of confirmation bias. But so is the person making the accusation. The asymmetric deployment of bias language — always attributional, rarely reflexive — is itself a bias that bias research predicts but popular treatments rarely acknowledge.

This weaponization has real epistemological consequences. It degrades the conversational norms necessary for genuine belief updating. When "you're biased" replaces "here's why I think you're wrong," the conversation moves from evidence to diagnosis. The other person is no longer a rational agent with potentially valid reasons for disagreement — they're a patient exhibiting symptoms. This pathologization of disagreement is one of the most corrosive effects of popularized bias research, and it operates in direct opposition to the article's stated goal of promoting open-minded inquiry.

### Can We Think About Thinking?

The ultimate question raised by this article — and by the entire project of popular cognitive bias communication — is whether second-order cognition (thinking about thinking) can actually modify first-order cognition (the thinking itself). The philosophical tradition is divided. Rationalists from Descartes onward have assumed that reflective awareness enables cognitive correction. Humean skeptics have argued that reason is the "slave of the passions" and that reflective awareness of bias doesn't necessarily confer the ability to override it. Contemporary dual-process theory (Kahneman's own System 1 and System 2) occupies an uneasy middle ground: System 2 can sometimes override System 1, but it's slow, effortful, and dependent on motivation and cognitive resources that are themselves subject to bias.

The honest answer, as best we can extract it from the literature, seems to be: metacognition helps, but less than metacognition thinks it does. Structural interventions (changing decision environments) are more reliable than dispositional ones (asking people to be less biased). And the most important cognitive skill may not be bias awareness but something more fundamental: the capacity to genuinely entertain the possibility that you are wrong about something important to you. Not as an abstract principle — most people will readily agree with the abstract principle — but as a lived epistemological practice that changes what you read, who you trust, how you argue, and what you're willing to give up.

This is harder than any popular article about cognitive bias can convey. It's harder, in fact, than this article can convey — because we, too, are writing from within the constraints we describe, subject to the biases we catalogue, unable to fully see the distortions in our own framing. The best we can do is acknowledge the recursion openly and proceed with what rigor we can manage.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes' editorial decision to republish this article reveals a tension at the heart of modern fact-checking. The article is not a fact-check. It makes no specific claim verification. It offers no rating. It is a science communication piece — an opinion-adjacent analysis from The Conversation, an academic media platform — that Snopes published under its "News" vertical rather than its fact-checking apparatus.

This distinction matters because **it exposes the expanding scope of fact-checking organizations** beyond their core competency. Snopes built its reputation on specific, verifiable claims: Did this event happen? Was this quote authentic? Is this photo real? The cognitive bias article occupies a different territory entirely — it's a theoretical framework for understanding why people resist fact-checks, published on a fact-checking platform. The institutional effect is to leverage Snopes' credibility for epistemic authority that extends beyond empirical verification into psychological explanation.

The editorial choice to republish without additional framing is itself revealing. When Snopes publishes a traditional fact-check, it applies a structured methodology — source identification, evidence evaluation, contextual analysis, rating. None of these structures are applied to Maddock's article. It arrives on the Snopes platform with the implicit endorsement of appearing there, but without the epistemic rigor that Snopes applies to its actual fact-checks. This creates a credibility transfer — readers may attribute to the article the same evidential standing as a Snopes fact-check, when it is actually an academic's opinion piece about why fact-resistance happens.

The deeper problem is **structural**. Fact-checking organizations face an existential challenge: their work depends on the assumption that providing verified facts changes beliefs, yet the psychological research they republish and implicitly endorse suggests that it often doesn't. This creates what we might call the "fact-checker's paradox" — the better the evidence that facts don't change minds, the weaker the rationale for the fact-checking enterprise itself. Republishing an article about cognitive bias on a fact-checking platform is, in this light, an exercise in institutional cognitive dissonance. The article explains why Snopes' core product (verified facts) may not work, and Snopes publishes it as content.

This is not a criticism of Snopes specifically. It's a structural observation about what happens when fact-checking organizations expand from empirical verification into explanatory psychology. The editorial standards are different. The epistemic status is different. And the risk of institutional bias — specifically, the risk that a fact-checking organization will preferentially publish psychological explanations that attribute resistance to its own fact-checks to cognitive deficiency rather than to legitimate disagreement — is real and unexamined.

The most responsible editorial approach would be to publish such articles with explicit framing: this is not a fact-check; these are well-supported but debated psychological theories; the evidence base has strengths and limitations; and the reader should apply the same critical scrutiny to this article about bias that the article recommends applying to their own beliefs. Snopes did not provide this framing. The article appeared, and was widely shared, as another entry in the Snopes content stream — inheriting the platform's credibility without earning it through the platform's methodology.

For the Truth Vault, this case is instructive. An article about epistemology published on a fact-checking platform demands **more** scrutiny than a standard fact-check, not less — precisely because its claims are harder to verify, its framing is more consequential, and its recursive relationship to the platform's mission creates conflicts of interest that are invisible to casual readers but central to any serious epistemological analysis. The most important biases to examine are not always the ones the article names. Sometimes they are the ones the institution embodies.
