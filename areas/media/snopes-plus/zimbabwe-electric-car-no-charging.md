## 1. The Claim

Since approximately 2015, social media posts and viral news articles circulating across Africa, the African diaspora, and wider English-speaking online communities have proclaimed that a self-taught inventor from Zimbabwe named Sangulani Maxwell Chikumbutso has cracked one of the fundamental puzzles of modern transportation: an electric car that never needs to be plugged in or recharged. The claim, as stated in hundreds of reuploads and shares, is simple and intoxicating — Chikumbutso's car uses a proprietary system to draw energy from ambient radio frequencies in the air, reconvert that energy into electrical power, and continuously recharge its own battery bank while in motion, producing a vehicle that is essentially self-perpetuating, free from any grid dependency, and immune to the range anxiety that plagues conventional electric vehicles.

The technology at the centre of the story is what Chikumbutso and his company, Saith Technologies, have branded the "Greener Power Machine," or GPM. According to the company's own public materials and dozens of promotional articles that proliferated in the years following a single public demonstration event in Harare, Zimbabwe, the GPM achieves its extraordinary result by exposing an undisclosed substance — described only as a "trade-secreted" compound — to radio wavelengths. Through this unexplained process, the device supposedly amplifies the ambient radio energy it receives, converting minuscule stray transmissions into enough electrical output not only to sustain the machine's own operation, but to power an entire automobile and continuously recharge its battery pack as it drives. Chikumbutso also applied the same claimed principle to a standalone Greener Power Machine generator, reportedly capable of providing household power with no fuel input and no grid connection.

The narrative surrounding Chikumbutso has been amplified by a potent combination of factors: genuine pride in African innovation, frustration with the continent's energy poverty, and the emotional resonance of a story about a self-taught working-class man overturning the assumptions of the global scientific establishment. Images from the 2015 demonstration event — Chikumbutso standing beside what appears to be a modest electric vehicle and various machinery — circulated widely, lending the claims an air of visual authority. Headlines described him as "Africa's Tesla," a genius inventor ignored by Western gatekeepers and funded only by his own tenacity. The story was, in short, exactly the kind of claim that spreads virally not because people are credulous, but because they want it to be true.

## 2. What's Actually True

The documented reality is considerably more modest, and in some key respects, contradicts the viral claims entirely.

What is verifiable is this: a Zimbabwean entrepreneur named Sangulani Maxwell Chikumbutso did hold a public media event in July 2015 at the Bluffhill Industrial Park in Harare, organised under the banner of his company Saith Technologies. The event, called an "Open Day," showcased several projects including a locally assembled drone, road safety products, industrial machines, and a vehicle described as an electric car. Zimbabwean technology news outlet Techzim reported on the event in real-time and noted that they had never heard of the company before receiving an unsolicited media invitation. Footage from South Africa's SABC news channel also appeared, briefly covering the spectacle.

What was not documented at that event — or at any event since — is the technology actually working in any independently verified, controlled, or transparent manner. Every photograph of the car, every video clip, every promotional image in existence appears to trace back to that single choreographed day in July 2015. The Saith Technologies website itself, as of investigative review, was populated almost exclusively with photographs from that one demonstration. No independent engineer, materials scientist, or energy researcher has examined the GPM. No peer-reviewed study or credible technical audit has confirmed its operation. No replication of the device has been commissioned or published.

The scientific principles invoked in Chikumbutso's claims are not entirely invented from whole cloth, which is part of what makes the story epistemically interesting. Radio frequency energy harvesting is a real and actively researched field of applied physics and engineering. The basic idea is sound in a limited sense: because modern civilisation blankets the planet in wireless transmissions — mobile phone signals, Wi-Fi, broadcast radio, satellite downlinks — there is genuinely a small amount of electromagnetic energy diffused through the air at any given moment, and it is theoretically possible to build devices that capture some of this ambient energy and convert it into electrical current. Companies and research groups have developed working proof-of-concept systems that do exactly this.

The critical caveat — and it is a decisive one — is that the amount of energy recoverable through this method is minuscule. The ambient radio frequency energy available in most environments amounts to nanowatts or, in favourable conditions near strong transmitters, microwatts. That is enough, potentially, to trickle-charge a very small sensor, power an RFID tag, or sustain a fitness monitor. It is not enough — not even remotely enough — to power an electric automobile. The energy density is separated from automotive requirements by several orders of magnitude.

For Chikumbutso's GPM to work as described, the device would need to produce more usable energy than it receives from the environment. That is not a technology problem or an engineering problem awaiting a sufficiently clever solution. It is a violation of the first and second laws of thermodynamics — the conservation of energy and the principle of entropy. Every credible physicist assesses perpetual motion or "over-unity" energy machines not as speculative future technology but as categorically impossible under the known laws of the universe. The "trade-secreted substance" allegedly at the core of the amplification process has never been disclosed, tested, synthesised, or observed doing anything. Its invocation reads as the defining marker of pseudoscience: an unexplained, unfalsifiable central component that conveniently cannot be examined.

In 2018, Snopes investigated and rated the claim **False**.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of the Chikumbutso story — still shared widely years after the original 2015 event — reflects a set of deeply understandable human motivations, cognitive patterns, and structural information gaps.

**The appeal of the African genius narrative.** For communities that have spent generations watching Western and East Asian nations dominate technological innovation while the African continent is represented primarily through charitable and deficit framings, a story about a self-taught Zimbabwean inventor solving a problem that has baffled the global energy industry carries enormous emotional weight. The claim is not merely about a car; it is about the credibility, capability, and dignity of an entire continent and its people. Sharing the story is an act of pride and solidarity. Questioning it can feel, in context, like betrayal or internalised scepticism about African achievement — a toxic dynamic that bad actors and careless viral spreaders exploit, sometimes deliberately.

**Motivated reasoning and the underdog frame.** The Chikumbutso story is structured precisely as a David-versus-Goliath narrative: a poor, self-taught man from a country with persistent energy poverty, working in a modest industrial park, challenging the assumptions of the global scientific establishment and the interests of the oil and energy industries that profit from dependency. This framing activates a powerful heuristic many people carry: that disruptive truth-tellers are often initially ridiculed by entrenched establishments. There are real historical examples of this — Semmelweis, Barry Marshall, continental drift — and these examples make the heuristic feel wise rather than naive. When someone is positioned as being suppressed by powerful interests, the absence of mainstream validation can be reinterpreted as further evidence of the story's truth rather than as a warning sign.

**Visual plausibility.** Photographs of Chikumbutso's demonstration event show real objects: a car, machines, people in lab-style settings. The human visual system is poorly equipped to distinguish between a functioning prototype and a non-working model constructed for photographic purposes. The images look like what a working invention would look like, and in the absence of detailed technical scrutiny most viewers are not equipped to identify what is missing from those images — namely, any evidence that the devices do what they claim.

**Information asymmetry and the limits of self-correction in viral media.** The original viral shares of the Chikumbutso story were enthusiastic and widely distributed; the corrections and fact-checks were narrowly distributed and read primarily by audiences already predisposed to scepticism. The result is a persistent information asymmetry where far more people have encountered the "amazing African inventor" framing than have ever encountered the word "thermodynamics" applied to it. This is a structural feature of viral information ecosystems, not a failing unique to this story.

**Distrust of Western fact-checking institutions.** In communities that have legitimate historical reasons to distrust Western media, Western science gatekeepers, and Western-authorised knowledge hierarchies, claims that an African innovation has been debunked by a US-based fact-checking website may actually reinforce belief in the claim rather than diminishing it. The Snopes rating can become additional evidence within a worldview that frames scepticism as geopolitical bias.

**The real technology kernel.** Because radio frequency harvesting is a genuine research field, people who encounter the Chikumbutso claim and then do a quick investigation will find real, credible sources discussing the concept. This creates a false validation: the underlying idea sounds like real science, which raises the plausibility of the specific claim. The gap between "RF harvesting can power a Fitbit" and "RF harvesting can power a car" is not intuitively obvious to non-physicists.

## 4. Verdict

The claim that Sangulani Maxwell Chikumbutso invented an electric car that requires no charging is **false**.

The alleged technology — a Greener Power Machine that amplifies ambient radio frequency energy through a trade-secreted substance — has never been independently tested, verified, or replicated. The sole evidence for its existence consists of photographs and promotional materials from a single day-long media event in 2015. The scientific principles it invokes, insofar as they can be reconstructed from public materials, describe a perpetual motion machine: a device that produces more energy than it receives. Such a device is not merely unproven; it is categorically impossible under the laws of thermodynamics that are among the most thoroughly substantiated principles in the history of natural science.

Radio frequency harvesting is a real technology, and it provides the grain of plausibility around which this claim is built. But the power levels achievable through RF harvesting are separated from automotive requirements by roughly ten orders of magnitude, and no undisclosed substance changes that fact.

The story has caused real harm by spreading scientific misinformation and by exploiting legitimate African pride in technological achievement to propagate a myth. Genuine innovation does emerge from Zimbabwe and from all across Africa — in telecommunications, in mobile finance, in renewable energy deployment, in literature, in biomedical research — and it deserves honest attention. Stories like this one, precisely because they feel good to believe, make it harder to engage critically with the genuine achievements and the genuine challenges of technological development on the continent.

**EARC classification: E (Empirical).** The claim makes testable empirical assertions — specifically, that a device produces more energy output than input — that can be evaluated against the laws of physics. It is not a matter of contested interpretation, rival expert schools, or epistemological complexity. The physics here is settled.

**Gap category: distorted-but-grounded.** The claim is built on a real substrate — radio frequency energy harvesting — but extends it far beyond anything the actual science supports, distorting a niche research area into a claim of impossible over-unity output.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The Chikumbutso story sits within a broader ecosystem of "suppressed genius inventor" claims that surface periodically across global media. These narratives share a structural grammar: a self-taught outsider, a technology that would disrupt incumbent industries, a conspicuous absence of mainstream validation framed as evidence of suppression rather than absence of proof, visual documentation from a single controlled event, and a built-in immune response to scepticism scripted as the bias of gatekeepers.

The energy variant of this pattern is particularly persistent because the global energy system is, in fact, shaped by powerful incumbent interests, and because genuinely disruptive energy technologies have historically faced real institutional inertia. James Lovelock, the independent scientist who developed the Gaia hypothesis, really did work outside institutional frameworks. Solar power genuinely was dismissed by entrenched utility and fossil fuel interests for decades before its economics became undeniable. These real cases create narrative space for stories that mimic their structure without containing their substance.

What distinguishes the Chikumbutso story from genuine disruptive science is not its origin in a small country or its lack of institutional affiliation. It is the absence of any independently witnessed demonstration of the core claimed phenomenon, the invocation of a "trade-secreted substance" that conveniently forecloses examination, and the fundamental physics: you cannot get more energy out of a system than you put in. These are not the fingerprints of neglected truth; they are the fingerprints of either sincere self-delusion or deliberate fraud.

Africa's real energy challenges — persistent electricity poverty, inadequate grid infrastructure, heavy dependence on imported fossil fuels, vast untapped solar and wind potential — deserve serious analytical attention and genuine innovation. Stories about magical over-unity machines distract from the more difficult, more interesting, and ultimately more important work of building real renewable energy capacity. Countries like Morocco, Kenya, and South Africa have made remarkable strides in utility-scale solar and wind deployment using real technology. Rwanda has built significant off-grid solar infrastructure. Ethiopia's Grand Renaissance Dam is an enormous, real, contested, consequential energy intervention. These stories exist, but they are less viral than the story of a man who claimed to have solved physics.

The proliferation of claims like Chikumbutso's also creates a subtle secondary harm: when audiences who trusted the story encounter the debunking, some portion of them generalise their disillusionment not to the specific claim but to the idea of African technological achievement itself — a devastating irony in which the myth's exposure reinforces the deficit narrative its propagation was meant to counter.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes investigation, published in May 2018 and updated in June 2018, by journalist Alex Kasprak, represents the most thorough publicly available fact-check of the Chikumbutso claim. Its methodology is instructive.

Kasprak began by tracing the origin of the story rather than simply examining its most recent iteration. He identified that essentially all photographic evidence for the alleged invention traces back to the single July 2015 "Open Day" media event at Saith Technologies' premises in Harare — a deliberately choreographed marketing occasion for which the company issued unsolicited press invitations. The Techzim reporting from that event, which approached the demonstration with journalistic curiosity rather than either credulity or dismissal, provided a contemporaneous account that neither confirmed nor denied the technology's functionality, and explicitly noted the company was unknown prior to the invitation.

Kasprak examined the physics claims directly, explaining the actual state of radio frequency harvesting technology with reference to real research — including a Telegraph article about Freevolt, a legitimate company working on RF energy harvesting — and contextualising those real capabilities against the orders-of-magnitude gap between what RF harvesting can actually achieve and what would be required to power an automobile. This approach — anchoring to real science in order to measure the distance between legitimate research and the viral claim — is a useful template for evaluating "distorted-but-grounded" claims, where the debunking cannot simply dismiss the underlying scientific domain but must map precisely where the distortion occurs.

The investigation noted the "trade-secreted substance" at the heart of the GPM as a red flag: the device's core mechanism is placed off limits from scrutiny by invocation of commercial confidentiality, which is a standard marker of unverifiable pseudoscientific claims. Legitimate inventors filing patents must disclose their methods in detail — that is the bargain at the heart of the patent system. An inventor who claims performance that would revolutionise global energy but refuses to disclose the mechanism on commercial grounds is making an unfalsifiable claim, which occupies a different epistemic category from a falsifiable but contested one.

Fact-checkers assessing similar claims should look for: (1) traceability of evidence to a single controlled event; (2) absence of independent technical verification; (3) invocation of trade secrets or similar shields against examination as the core mechanism; (4) a physics baseline that establishes the theoretical maximum plausible output and compares it to the claimed output; and (5) the nature of the emotional framing around the claim, which can predict the specific ways audiences will resist correction.

The Snopes verdict — False — is straightforward. What the deeper investigation reveals is not merely a false story about a car but a recurring pattern of viral pseudoscience that exploits legitimate social grievances and genuine emotional desires. Accurate fact-checking of such stories requires not just determining the verdict but understanding and honestly engaging with why the story spread, and what it was trying — however misleadingly — to say.
