# Why Scientists Are Still Puzzled by Consciousness

## Introduction

Consciousness, at its basic level, is an individual's self-awareness, comprising both external and internal phenomena; it may constitute any kind of cognition, experience, feeling, or perception. Awareness can be a continuously changing continuum, or it may shut down or be disrupted temporarily. During sleep, for instance, we are not aware of our environment, yet it can be argued that we remain conscious.

Altered levels of consciousness can also occur due to medical or mental conditions — such as coma, delirium, disorientation, and stupor — that impair, change, or obliterate awareness. But what about consciousness in other species? Are dogs, fish, shrimp, or bees conscious? They have senses and perceptions, but do they possess the same kind of awareness as humans?

The problem with discussing consciousness is that it means different things to different people. Some researchers focus on subjective experiences (what you feel), while others focus on functionality, or on how cognitive processes and behaviors are influenced by consciousness. "Until about 30 years ago, it was taboo to study consciousness, and for good reasons," said Lenore Blum, a theoretical computer scientist at Carnegie Mellon University. According to her, there was a lack of "good techniques to study consciousness in a non-invasive way" back then.

That changed around 1990, with the emergence of new technologies, such as functional magnetic resonance imaging, which allowed researchers to study the brain non-invasively.

The words "conscious" and "consciousness" cover a wide variety of mental phenomena. The self-awareness requirement can be interpreted in many ways. Thomas Nagel proposed a criterion for describing the subjective experience of consciousness, summarized in his famous dictum "what it is like…" — that is, how does a creature experience the world? According to Nagel, bats are conscious because they experience their world through echolocation. He said that "the fact that an organism has conscious experience at all means, basically, that there is something it is like to be that organism."

## The Features of Consciousness

In a paper published in 2000, Peter T. Walling from the Department of Anesthesiology and Pain Management, Baylor University Medical Center, listed seven features of human consciousness:

- Consciousness involves short-term memory.
- Consciousness may occur independently of sensory inputs.
- Consciousness displays steerable attention.
- Consciousness has the capacity for alternative interpretations of complex or ambiguous data.
- Consciousness disappears in deep sleep.
- Consciousness reappears in dreaming, at least in muted or disjointed form.
- Consciousness harbors the contents of several basic sensory modalities within a single unified experience.

The neuroscientist Antonio Damasio argues that consciousness is not possible without feelings. Physical responses to external stimuli involving the regulation of life processes are one thing, but what he terms "core consciousness" requires "autobiographical memory," which "emerges from emotions and feelings."

For Anil Seth, another prominent neuroscientist, consciousness is a "controlled hallucination" because we never experience objective reality, whether externally in the world or within our minds. The brain receives signals from both the external environment and our own mental processes, enabling us to make predictions based on prior experiences.

## Theories of Consciousness

### Global Workspace Theory

Proposed by Bernard Baars and elaborated by Stanislas Dehaene and Jean-Pierre Changeux, global workspace theory posits that consciousness arises when information is broadcast widely across the brain via a "global workspace." In this model, the brain contains many specialized, unconscious processors that operate in parallel. When any one of these processors gains access to the global workspace, its contents become conscious and available to other processes throughout the brain.

### Higher-Order Theories

Higher-order theories hold that a mental state is conscious only when it is the object of a higher-order representation. In other words, you are conscious of a perception or thought only when you have a thought about that perception or thought. David Rosenthal is the most prominent advocate of this view.

### Integrated Information Theory

Proposed by Giulio Tononi, integrated information theory (IIT) argues that consciousness is identical to a certain kind of information processing — specifically, information that is both highly differentiated (many possible states) and highly integrated (the whole cannot be decomposed into independent parts). IIT assigns a numerical value, phi (Φ), to measure this integration. A system is conscious to the degree that it has high Φ.

### Attention Schema Theory

Proposed by Michael Graziano, attention schema theory argues that the brain builds a model of attention — an "attention schema" — and that this model is what we experience as consciousness. Consciousness, on this view, is the brain's simplified, sometimes inaccurate description of its own attentional processes.

### Recurrent Processing Theory

Victor Lamme argues that consciousness arises from recurrent (feedback) processing within sensory cortices, independently of higher cognitive areas. On this view, you can have conscious experience without the involvement of prefrontal cortex or global broadcasting.

## The Hard Problem

David Chalmers coined the phrase "the hard problem of consciousness" to describe the explanatory gap between objective, physical descriptions of the brain and the subjective, first-person character of experience. Why does any physical process give rise to experience at all? Why is there something it is like to be a system with certain physical properties?

The hard problem is distinct from the "easy problems" — explaining how the brain integrates information, focuses attention, or controls behavior. Those problems are hard in practice but tractable in principle: they can be addressed by specifying the right mechanisms. The hard problem, Chalmers argues, is different in kind: even a complete functional explanation would leave open the question of why that function is accompanied by experience.

## The Neural Correlates of Consciousness

Much empirical work on consciousness focuses on identifying the neural correlates of consciousness (NCC) — the minimal neural mechanisms sufficient for a given conscious experience. Researchers use techniques such as fMRI, EEG, and single-unit recording to identify which brain regions and activity patterns are associated with conscious versus unconscious processing.

The prefrontal cortex and parietal cortex have long been implicated. But recent work has complicated the picture. Some experiments suggest that visual consciousness arises in posterior cortical regions without necessarily involving prefrontal cortex, challenging theories that give frontal regions a central role.

## Creature Consciousness and Plant Consciousness

The question of which creatures are conscious — and in what degree — has no settled answer. The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness (2012) affirmed that nonhuman animals, including all mammals, birds, and octopuses, possess the neurological substrates that generate conscious states.

More provocatively, some researchers have raised the question of plant consciousness. Plants lack nervous systems and neurons, but they do engage in information processing, exhibit adaptive behavior, and respond to stimuli in ways that appear purposive. Whether any of this constitutes experience — whether there is something it is like to be a plant — remains deeply contested.

## Consciousness and Artificial Intelligence

As AI systems become more sophisticated, the question of machine consciousness has moved from science fiction toward serious inquiry. IIT would assign Φ values to AI systems; most current architectures would score low. Global workspace theory raises the question of whether large language models have anything analogous to a global broadcast. No consensus view exists.

## Conclusion

Consciousness remains one of the hardest problems in science and philosophy. The proliferation of theories has clarified the conceptual landscape — we know more precisely what the competing positions are, and we have better empirical tools for testing them. But the hard problem persists. Why any physical process gives rise to experience at all may be the deepest question science has ever tried to answer.
