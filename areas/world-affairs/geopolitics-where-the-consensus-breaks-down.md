# World Affairs: Where the Popular Consensus Breaks Down

The popular geopolitics narrative runs on grand simplifications: democracies do not fight each other, free trade prevents war, humanitarian intervention saves lives, sanctions change regime behavior, and the arc of history bends toward liberal democracy. These narratives served as the intellectual infrastructure of the post-Cold War order and are still the default assumptions of Western foreign policy discourse.

The problem is that the real world has been generating evidence against these simplifications for decades, and the gap between the narratives that organize foreign policy thinking and the evidence about what actually works in international relations has become dangerously wide. Getting geopolitics wrong has consequences measured in lives — which makes the evidence-practice gap in this domain uniquely consequential.

## The Weakest Popular Claims

### "Democracies don't go to war with each other" ⚠️ Variable misattribution — the democratic peace may reflect shared alliances and power structures, not regime type

The democratic peace theory — the observation that established democracies rarely fight each other — is one of the most cited findings in international relations. However, Rosato (*American Political Science Review*, 2003) argued that the pattern is better explained by Cold War alliance structures and American hegemony than by the internal characteristics of democratic states. The definition of "democracy" is frequently adjusted retroactively to exclude inconvenient cases (the U.S.-UK War of 1812, India-Pakistan conflicts, Israeli-Lebanese conflicts). Transitional democracies — states undergoing democratization — are actually *more* war-prone than stable autocracies, as Mansfield and Snyder documented in *Electing to Fight* (2005). The theory's strongest form (established democracies do not fight each other) may be correct but trivially so — established democracies in the post-1945 period have mostly been wealthy, trade-interdependent allies within the same security architecture.

### "Sanctions effectively change regime behavior" ⚠️ Effect size inflation — comprehensive studies show sanctions fail to achieve political objectives in most cases

A comprehensive study by Hufbauer, Schott, and Elliott (*Economic Sanctions Reconsidered*, third edition, 2007) examined 204 cases of economic sanctions since World War I and found that sanctions achieved their stated foreign policy goals in only about 34% of cases. A more restrictive analysis by Pape (*International Security*, 1997) argued that the success rate was closer to 5% when cases were properly evaluated. Sanctions against major adversaries (Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Russia) have demonstrably failed to produce regime change or major policy concessions over decades of application. Meanwhile, sanctions consistently impose humanitarian costs on civilian populations: a 2019 study in *The Lancet* estimated that U.S. sanctions on Venezuela contributed to excess mortality. Sanctions may serve signaling and domestic political functions, but the evidence for their effectiveness as instruments of coercive diplomacy is far weaker than the frequency of their use would suggest.

### "Humanitarian intervention saves more lives than it costs" ⚠️ Direction of causality unresolved — evidence is mixed and highly context-dependent

The doctrine of Responsibility to Protect (R2P), formalized at the 2005 UN World Summit, assumes that military intervention to prevent mass atrocities produces net positive humanitarian outcomes. The evidence is more complicated. NATO's intervention in Libya (2011), initially justified as preventing an imminent massacre in Benghazi, resulted in state collapse, ongoing civil war, and the creation of open slave markets — outcomes that the UK Parliament's Foreign Affairs Committee (2016) described as a policy failure based on flawed intelligence and no post-intervention plan. A 2017 study by Kuperman (*International Security*) argued that humanitarian interventions can intensify conflicts by emboldening rebel groups who expect international support, a phenomenon he termed "moral hazard." The Kosovo intervention (1999) is more defensible on humanitarian grounds, but even here the outcomes were contested. The pattern is not that intervention never works, but that the conditions for success are narrow and rarely met.

### "Free trade prevents war" ⚠️ Effect does not transfer — World War I broke out among the most economically interdependent nations in history

The "commercial peace" thesis — that trade interdependence reduces the probability of war — was contradicted by one of the most devastating experiments in history. In 1914, Britain and Germany were each other's largest trading partners, and the European great powers were more economically interconnected than at any previous point in history. A 2010 study by Barbieri (*The Liberal Illusion*) examined 200 years of trade and conflict data and found that economic interdependence had no consistent pacifying effect and in some specifications was associated with increased conflict. Copeland (*Economic Interdependence and War*, 2015) argued that it is not the level of trade but expectations about future trade that matter — when states expect trade to be cut off, interdependence can actually increase the incentive for preemptive war. The popular version conflates a theoretical mechanism with an empirical regularity that does not consistently hold.

### "Foreign aid promotes development" ⚠️ Direction of causality unresolved — decades of aid have not produced consistent development outcomes

Despite over $4.6 trillion in development aid since 1960, the relationship between aid and development remains contested. Easterly (*The White Man's Burden*, 2006) documented extensive evidence that aid had failed to produce sustained growth in recipient countries and often reinforced bad governance by reducing accountability to domestic populations. A 2014 meta-analysis by Doucouliagos and Paldam covering 68 studies found no robust evidence that aid promotes economic growth. Specific types of targeted aid — particularly health interventions like malaria bed nets, oral rehydration therapy, and vaccination campaigns — show clear positive effects (as documented by the randomized evaluations of JPAL and IPA). The problem is with the grand "aid promotes development" narrative, which conflates effective targeted interventions with the broader aid enterprise.

### "The world is becoming more dangerous" ⚠️ Effect size inflation — long-term trends show declining violence despite salient exceptions

Steven Pinker's *The Better Angels of Our Nature* (2011) documented long-term declines in every measurable form of violence — war deaths, homicides, domestic violence, child abuse — using data from multiple sources spanning centuries. The Human Security Report (2013) confirmed that battle deaths per capita have declined dramatically since 1945. This does not mean the world is safe or that current conflicts are not severe, but the popular perception that violence is increasing is contradicted by the long-term data. Short-term reversals (the Syrian civil war, the Russia-Ukraine conflict, rising tensions in multiple regions) are real but do not reverse the long-term trend. The perception of increasing danger is driven by media availability bias — we know about more violence than ever because reporting is more comprehensive, not because there is more violence.

### "Nation-building through military intervention works" ⚠️ Direction of effect reversed — track record is overwhelmingly negative

The post-9/11 nation-building experiments in Afghanistan and Iraq represent the most expensive and sustained attempts at military-led nation-building since the post-World War II occupations of Germany and Japan. Both failed to produce stable democratic governance. A 2019 study by Downes and O'Rourke (*International Security*) examined all cases of U.S.-backed regime change since 1816 and found that imposed democracies were no more likely to remain democratic than other post-conflict states, and that foreign-imposed regime change frequently led to civil war. The post-WWII cases of Germany and Japan succeeded under conditions (total defeat, existing industrial capacity, cultural homogeneity, massive sustained investment, external threat from the Soviet Union) that do not generalize to contemporary intervention targets.

## What Actually Holds Up

**International institutions reduce transaction costs and increase cooperation in specific domains.** The WTO dispute resolution mechanism, arms control verification regimes, and international health coordination (WHO) have documented records of facilitating cooperation in their specific areas of competence, even if they cannot prevent great power conflict.

**Targeted health and education aid produces measurable improvements.** Randomized evaluations of specific interventions — insecticide-treated bed nets, deworming programs, conditional cash transfers — show consistent positive effects. The distinction is between targeted, evidence-evaluated interventions and broad budget support.

**Nuclear deterrence has, empirically, coincided with the absence of great power war since 1945.** This is an empirical observation, not a recommendation. The causal mechanism — that nuclear weapons make the cost of great power war unacceptably high — is consistent with the observation but cannot be definitively tested. The observation coexists with the risk that nuclear deterrence could fail catastrophically even once.

**Trade promotes prosperity, even when it does not prevent conflict.** The welfare gains from trade liberalization are well-documented in the economics literature. The commercial peace thesis overstates the security benefits of trade, but the economic benefits are real and large.

## Practical Framework: Navigating Geopolitical Claims Under Uncertainty

| Risk Tier | Principle | Action |
|-----------|-----------|--------|
| **Tier 1: Empirically supported patterns with clear mechanisms** | These have demonstrated effects across multiple contexts. | Accept as working framework: international institutions facilitate cooperation in specific domains, targeted aid works better than general aid, trade improves welfare, violence has declined long-term. |
| **Tier 2: Theories with some support but important exceptions and contested mechanisms** | The pattern may be real but the causal story is uncertain. | Engage critically: democratic peace theory, commercial peace, deterrence theory. Ask: under what conditions? With what definitions? Over what time horizon? |
| **Tier 3: Grand narratives that serve policy interests more than evidence** | These justify interventions and expenditures that the evidence does not support. | Challenge: "sanctions work," "nation-building through military force," "aid promotes development" (as general claims), "humanitarian intervention saves lives" (without careful case assessment). |
| **General principle** | Geopolitical narratives that justify the use of force or coercion should be held to the highest evidentiary standard, not the lowest. | The consequences of getting these wrong are measured in bodies. Demand evidence proportional to the stakes. |
