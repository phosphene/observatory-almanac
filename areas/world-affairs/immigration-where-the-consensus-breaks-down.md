# Immigration: Where the Popular Consensus Breaks Down

The popular immigration debate is organized around two competing myths. One insists that immigrants threaten jobs, public safety, and cultural cohesion. The other treats immigration as an unqualified economic and cultural good with no trade-offs. Both versions flatten a complex empirical reality into politically convenient narratives, and both versions are contradicted by significant bodies of evidence.

The problem is that immigration is a domain where political identity predicts beliefs more strongly than evidence does, where anecdotes substitute for data, and where the actual research — which shows conditional effects that vary by context, time horizon, and policy design — satisfies neither side. The distance between what the evidence shows and what the debate assumes may be the widest of any major policy issue.

## The Weakest Popular Claims

### "Immigrants increase crime rates" ⚠️ Direction of effect reversed — immigrants commit crimes at lower rates than native-born populations

This is one of the most thoroughly studied questions in immigration research, and the evidence is remarkably consistent. Cato Institute analyses of American Community Survey and census data have repeatedly found that immigrants — including undocumented immigrants — are incarcerated at lower rates than native-born Americans. A 2020 study by Light and Miller in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* analyzed data from all 50 states over 30 years and found that increases in undocumented immigration were not associated with increases in violent crime. Nowrasteh (Cato Institute, 2023) found that undocumented immigrants were 26% less likely to be convicted of homicide, 12% less likely for sexual assault, and 58% less likely for all criminal convictions compared to native-born citizens. Cities with larger immigrant populations tend to have lower crime rates — a finding that holds across multiple countries and time periods.

### "Immigrants take jobs from native-born workers" ⚠️ Variable misattribution — lump of labor fallacy underlies the claim

The "immigrants take our jobs" claim rests on the lump of labor fallacy — the assumption that there is a fixed number of jobs in an economy. Immigrants are not only workers but also consumers, entrepreneurs, and taxpayers; their spending and economic activity create jobs as well as fill them. The National Academies of Sciences (2017) comprehensive report on immigration economics concluded that immigration has an overall positive impact on long-run economic growth and that the impact on native-born workers' wages is small, with effects concentrated among prior immigrants and native-born workers without high school diplomas. The Borjas-Card debate — George Borjas finding modest negative wage effects for low-skill natives, David Card finding negligible effects — reflects genuine methodological disagreements, but even Borjas' estimates show effects far smaller than the popular narrative implies (roughly 2–3% wage decline for the most affected group over a decade).

### "Immigrants are a fiscal burden" ⚠️ Dose mismatch — time horizon dramatically changes the calculation

The NAS 2017 report found that first-generation immigrants cost state and local governments more than they contribute (primarily through education and healthcare costs for their children), but that the children of immigrants (second generation) are among the largest net fiscal contributors of any demographic group. Over a 75-year window, the average immigrant represents a net fiscal benefit of $259,000 in present value under the most comprehensive accounting. The "fiscal burden" claim cherry-picks short-term state and local costs while ignoring federal contributions (Social Security, Medicare taxes paid by immigrants who may never collect benefits) and the long-term fiscal contribution of the second generation. Undocumented immigrants specifically contribute billions to Social Security and Medicare through payroll taxes on false or borrowed Social Security numbers, benefits they cannot legally collect.

### "Strict enforcement deters unauthorized immigration" ⚠️ Effect does not transfer — enforcement spending has increased massively with minimal deterrence effect

U.S. border enforcement spending increased from approximately $1.5 billion in 1993 to over $25 billion by 2024, with the number of Border Patrol agents roughly tripling. A 2016 study by Massey, Durand, and Pren (*American Journal of Sociology*) found that this enforcement buildup did not reduce unauthorized immigration but did transform circular migration (seasonal workers returning home) into permanent settlement — undocumented migrants who previously returned to their home countries stopped doing so because the cost and danger of crossing made repeat trips unviable. The border wall specifically has been found to redirect rather than prevent crossings, increasing deaths in remote desert areas while having minimal effect on total unauthorized entries.

### "Assimilation is failing — today's immigrants don't integrate" ⚠️ Effect size inflation — integration patterns are remarkably similar to historical waves

Research on immigrant integration consistently finds patterns similar to previous immigration waves, despite popular perceptions of decline. A 2015 study by the National Academies found that contemporary immigrants are learning English at rates comparable to or faster than previous generations — by the third generation, the vast majority are English-dominant. Alba and Nee's *Remaking the American Mainstream* (2003) documented that second- and third-generation immigrants show educational, occupational, and income convergence with the native-born population, following trajectories remarkably similar to Italian, Jewish, and Polish immigrants of the early 20th century — who were also accused of being unassimilable at the time.

### "Open borders or closed borders are the only options" ⚠️ Addressing the wrong variable — immigration policy is a design problem, not a binary choice

The popular debate presents immigration as a binary — open or closed — when actual immigration systems exist on a complex spectrum of selection mechanisms, quotas, enforcement strategies, and integration policies. Countries with well-designed immigration systems (Canada, Australia, Germany) demonstrate that it is possible to have high immigration rates with broad public support through transparent selection criteria, efficient processing, and adequate integration infrastructure. The binary framing obscures the policy design questions that actually determine outcomes: which selection criteria, what processing timelines, what integration support, and what labor market protections accompany immigration.

## What Actually Holds Up

**Immigration selection and integration policies matter more than total numbers.** Cross-country comparison (Canada vs. U.S., Germany vs. Italy) shows that how immigrants are selected, processed, and integrated determines outcomes more than the volume of immigration.

**High-skill immigration produces large economic gains with minimal distributional costs.** The evidence for positive effects of high-skill immigration — on innovation (Hunt and Gauthier-Loiselle, 2010), entrepreneurship (Kerr and Lincoln, 2010), and economic growth — is among the strongest in immigration economics.

**Regularization of undocumented immigrants improves economic outcomes for everyone.** Studies of the 1986 Immigration Reform and Control Act (IRCA) amnesty and the 2012 DACA program show that legal status enables immigrants to negotiate better wages, pay more taxes, and invest in skills — improving outcomes for both immigrants and native-born workers in similar occupations.

**Refugee resettlement, while costly initially, produces positive long-term fiscal returns.** A 2017 study by Evans and Fitzgerald in the *American Economic Review* found that refugees who arrived as working-age adults paid more in taxes than they received in benefits over a 20-year period.

## Practical Framework: Navigating Immigration Claims Under Uncertainty

| Risk Tier | Principle | Action |
|-----------|-----------|--------|
| **Tier 1: Well-established empirical findings across multiple studies and countries** | These survive replication and varied contexts. | Accept as framework: immigrants commit less crime than natives, economic effects are net positive over generational time horizons, integration patterns mirror historical waves. |
| **Tier 2: Real effects with contested magnitudes** | Honest researchers disagree on how large the effects are. | Engage with nuance: low-skill immigration may modestly reduce wages for the most directly competing workers; short-term fiscal costs at the local level are real; integration support affects outcomes. Demand specifics about which immigrants, which time horizon, which policy context. |
| **Tier 3: Narratives that serve political mobilization more than evidence** | These persist because they activate identity-based responses. | Challenge: immigrants-as-threat narratives (contradicted by crime data), immigrants-as-burden narratives (contradicted by long-term fiscal data), and "no trade-offs" narratives (contradicted by localized distributional effects). Ask for the data, not the anecdote. |
| **General principle** | Immigration beliefs are among the most identity-driven and least evidence-responsive in the entire policy landscape. | This makes the evidence more important, not less. If your immigration views haven't been updated by data, they probably reflect your political identity rather than the research literature. |
