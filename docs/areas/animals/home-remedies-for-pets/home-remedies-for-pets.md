# Home Remedies for Pets

*Safe, evidence-informed home care for common pet ailments — and when to go to the vet instead.*

> **This guide is for minor ailments only.** It does not replace veterinary diagnosis or treatment. When in doubt, call your vet. Do not delay professional care based on home treatment.

---

## The Core Rule

Home remedies are appropriate for **mild, isolated symptoms** in an otherwise healthy animal showing normal behavior. They are not appropriate when:
- Symptoms are severe or worsening
- The animal is very young, old, or immunocompromised
- More than one symptom is present
- The animal is not eating, drinking, or eliminating normally
- Symptoms persist more than 24–48 hours

## Digestive Issues

### Mild Upset Stomach (Occasional Vomiting or Soft Stool)

**Bland diet protocol:**
1. Fast for 12–24 hours (water only). Do not fast puppies or kittens under 3 months — hypoglycemia risk.
2. Offer small amounts of bland food: plain boiled chicken (no skin, no seasoning) + plain white rice (1:3 ratio). Or plain unseasoned boiled hamburger, drained.
3. Feed in small amounts every 4–6 hours for 1–2 days.
4. Gradually reintroduce normal food over 2–3 days by mixing.

**Ginger:** Ginger biscuits or ginger capsules can help settle a queasy stomach. Give one to two ginger cookies (size-appropriate) about 30 minutes before a meal or car ride. Consult your vet on dosage before using capsules. *(AKC)*

**When to call the vet:** Vomiting more than 2–3 times in a few hours, blood in vomit, bloated abdomen, lethargy, or if the animal is a puppy/kitten.

---

### Car Sickness (Dogs)

Dogs with car sickness typically pant, whine, move around restlessly, and may vomit. For mild cases:

**Ginger:** Give a ginger biscuit or two (size-appropriate) 30 minutes before the ride. Natural herbs including ginger, lavender, and valerian may also help calm the stomach and reduce anxiety during travel — consult your vet on appropriate dosing before using herbal supplements. *(AKC, Treehugger)*

**When to call the vet:** Severe or persistent car sickness may respond to prescription anti-nausea medication.

---

### Constipation (Dogs)

**Try:** Add 1–2 teaspoons of plain canned pumpkin (not pie filling — must be 100% pumpkin, no spices) to food. High in fiber; well-tolerated by most dogs. Can repeat once daily for 2–3 days.

**Also:** Ensure adequate water intake. Add a small amount of low-sodium chicken broth to water bowl.

**When to call the vet:** No bowel movement for more than 2–3 days, straining, crying, lethargy.

---

### Diarrhea (Mild)

**Bland diet** as above. Add a probiotic formulated for pets (FortiFlora, Proviable) if available.

Plain unsweetened yogurt with live cultures can help restore gut flora in dogs — 1 teaspoon for small dogs, 1 tablespoon for large dogs. Do not use yogurt for cats (most are lactose-intolerant).

**Pumpkin puree:** 1–4 tablespoons of plain pumpkin puree (not pie mix) mixed into bland food can help firm up loose stools. Generally safe for all dogs. *(AKC)*

**When to call the vet:** Blood or mucus in stool, more than 24 hours in duration, accompanied by vomiting, or if the animal is a puppy/kitten.

## Skin & Coat

### Minor Skin Irritation, Hot Spots, or Mild Itching

**Oatmeal soak:** Blend plain unflavored oatmeal (not instant) into a fine powder. Dissolve in warm water. Apply to affected area or use as a soak for 5–10 minutes. Pat dry (do not rub). Soothes inflammation and relieves itch. Also effective as a shampoo for pets with seasonal allergy-related skin irritation — look for shampoos containing colloidal oatmeal, which moisturize skin and minimize inflammation from pollen or allergens. *(Animal Humane Society)*

**Diluted apple cider vinegar:** Mix 1 part apple cider vinegar with 1 part water. Apply to affected area with a cloth. Antimicrobial properties; can help with minor yeast overgrowth. **Do not apply to open or raw skin** — it will sting and cause irritation.

**Coconut oil:** Apply a small amount topically to irritated, dry, or cracked skin. Has mild antimicrobial and moisturizing properties. Safe if licked in small amounts. A small amount can also be added to food as a supplement for dogs with seasonal dry skin — most dogs enjoy the taste. Coconut oil addresses situational dryness only; it is not a substitute for veterinary treatment of allergies. *(AKC)*

**When to call the vet:** Wound is deep or infected (pus, foul smell), the animal is constantly licking or chewing an area (may need an e-collar), large area of hair loss, or symptoms persist more than 3 days.

---

### Seasonal Allergens

Regular bathing removes pollen and airborne allergens from your pet's skin and coat, which can meaningfully reduce itching and irritation during allergy season. Use a gentle, oatmeal-based shampoo and rinse thoroughly. *(Animal Humane Society)*

**When to call the vet:** Persistent itching, hair loss, repeated ear infections, or skin that remains inflamed despite bathing — these may indicate environmental or food allergies requiring diagnosis and treatment.

---

### Dry Paw Pads

Apply a small amount of unscented, plain petroleum jelly (Vaseline), shea butter, or a commercial paw balm. Massage in and put a sock on the paw for 10–15 minutes to allow absorption before the pet licks it off. Safe if ingested in small amounts. Petroleum jelly also helps prevent ice balls from forming between paw pads during winter walks. *(AKC)*

**Prevention:** In winter, rinse paws after walks on salted or de-iced surfaces.

---

### Sticky Substances (Tar, Glue, Sap)

If your pet gets into something sticky: *(ASPCA)*

1. Cover the affected area of fur with paper towels or apply baby powder to prevent the substance from spreading further.
2. Remove the paper towels, then massage a small amount of butter, margarine, vegetable oil, olive oil, or mayonnaise into the sticky area. Let sit for 5–10 minutes. Keep the pet supervised — do not allow licking.
3. Once the substance begins to bead or ball up, bathe with a mild liquid dish soap (not dishwasher detergent) and rinse thoroughly. Repeat as needed.

---

### Skunk Spray

Mix 1 quart 3% hydrogen peroxide + ¼ cup baking soda + 1 teaspoon liquid dish soap. Work into wet coat, leave 5 minutes, rinse thoroughly. Repeat if needed. Do not get in eyes. Do not store the mixture — it builds pressure in a sealed container.

## Eyes & Ears

### Eye Discharge (Minor, Clear)

Gently wipe away discharge with a clean, damp cotton ball or gauze — wipe away from the eye, outward. Use a fresh piece for each eye.

**When to call the vet:** Yellow or green discharge, squinting, pawing at eye, red or swollen eye, cloudiness, or change in vision (bumping into things).

---

### Ear Cleaning (Maintenance)

Use a commercial ear cleaning solution or a 50/50 mix of warm water and white vinegar (not for ears with open sores or perforated eardrum). Apply a few drops, gently massage the base of the ear for 20–30 seconds, then let the pet shake its head. Wipe the outer canal with a cotton ball. **Never insert cotton swabs into the ear canal.**

**When to call the vet:** Foul odor, dark discharge, persistent head shaking, scratching at ears, ear appears painful to touch — these suggest infection requiring prescription medication.

---

## Minor Wounds

### Superficial Cuts and Scrapes

1. Rinse with clean water or saline for several minutes.
2. Apply diluted chlorhexidine solution (0.05%): dilute 2% chlorhexidine to 1 part solution in 40 parts water. Or use plain hydrogen peroxide once to clean (not repeatedly — it impairs healing). **Witch hazel** is another gentle option — it is a mild astringent that can clean small abrasions and minor skin injuries while the wound is monitored. *(AKC)*
3. Apply a small amount of plain triple antibiotic ointment (Neosporin without pain relief, which contains pramoxine — avoid pramoxine for pets). Keep the animal from licking the wound.

**When to call the vet:** Wound is more than ½ inch long, deep, gaping, won't stop bleeding, or shows signs of infection after 24 hours (swelling, redness, discharge, odor).

---

### Bleeding Nails (After Clipping)

If you clip a nail too short and hit the quick, pat the area with a small amount of **baking soda** or **corn starch** to help clot the blood and stop bleeding. Apply gentle pressure with a clean cloth for a minute afterward. *(AKC)*

**When to call the vet:** Bleeding that does not stop within a few minutes, or signs of significant pain.

## Fleas

### Environmental Control
Wash all pet bedding in hot water weekly. Vacuum carpets and upholstered furniture thoroughly and empty the vacuum immediately.

### On the Pet
**Dish soap bath:** Dawn or similar liquid dish soap kills fleas on contact by disrupting their exoskeleton. Lather the pet thoroughly, leaving on for 5 minutes before rinsing. This is temporary relief only — it does not prevent re-infestation and can dry out skin with repeated use. *(Treehugger)*

**Rosemary flea dip:** Steep 2 cups of fresh rosemary in boiling water for 30 minutes. Strain, allow to cool to a comfortable lukewarm temperature, and pour over your pet, working through the coat. Allow to dry naturally — do not rinse out. Rosemary is a proven flea repellent. Do not use on cats without veterinary guidance. *(Treehugger)*

**Fine-toothed flea comb:** Dip comb in soapy water after each pass to drown fleas. Most effective on short-coated pets.

**Note:** Home remedies do not address flea eggs, larvae, or pupae in the environment. For established infestations, a vet-prescribed monthly flea prevention product is necessary.

## Stress & Anxiety

### Mild Situational Anxiety (Thunderstorms, Travel, Fireworks)

**Compression wraps:** A snug-fitting shirt or commercial anxiety wrap (Thundershirt) can reduce anxiety symptoms in many dogs via sustained, gentle pressure. Not effective for all animals.

**Calming scents and herbs:** Lavender and chamomile have mild calming effects on some dogs. Apply a small amount of lavender essential oil to a bandana worn loosely around the neck — keep away from nose, mouth, and eyes. Valerian is another herb noted for calming anxious dogs; consult your vet on appropriate form and dosage. **Do not apply essential oils directly to skin or use around cats** — cats metabolize essential oils poorly and can be seriously harmed. *(Treehugger)*

**Safe space:** Provide a covered crate or enclosed space with familiar-smelling bedding. For dogs, this simulates a den. Allow the pet to choose to enter and exit freely.

**When to call the vet:** Anxiety is persistent, severe, or affecting quality of life. Behavioral modification and/or medication may be needed.

## Muscle & Joint Pain

### Sore Muscles or Mild Joint Stiffness (Dogs)

For a dog with a minor limp or muscle soreness while awaiting a vet appointment: *(AKC)*

**Epsom salt soak:** Add ½ cup of unscented Epsom salt to a warm water bath. Soak the affected area or the whole dog for 10–15 minutes, twice daily. For dogs resistant to baths, soak a warm washcloth and apply it gently to the area of discomfort instead.

**When to call the vet:** Lameness that doesn't improve within 24–48 hours, visible swelling, pain on palpation, or any limp in a puppy — these warrant prompt evaluation. Do not delay a scheduled vet visit for a limp, even if the dog seems more comfortable with home treatment.

## Toxic Substance Exposure & Decontamination

If your pet comes into contact with a potentially toxic substance on their skin or coat, prompt decontamination reduces further absorption. *(ASPCA)*

**Before handling your pet:** Put on rubber gloves and old clothes. Many substances transfer easily from fur to skin.

**Bathing:** Use a mild liquid dish soap (not dishwasher detergent) — more effective than pet or human shampoo at cutting through oily or chemical residues. Lather with warm water, scrub gently, avoid eyes and ears, and rinse thoroughly. A single bath is usually sufficient; repeat if needed. Do not use a blow dryer — heat can spread residues or further irritate skin.

**If you suspect ingestion of a toxic substance:** Do not attempt to induce vomiting without speaking to a vet first. Call your vet or the ASPCA Poison Control hotline: **(888) 426-4435**. If a vet instructs you to induce vomiting, use 3% pharmaceutical hydrogen peroxide only — 1 teaspoon per 10 lbs of body weight, given orally. Never induce vomiting if the substance is bleach, drain cleaner, or a petroleum product. *(AKC, ASPCA)*

## Things to Avoid

| Item | Risk |
|------|------|
| Acetaminophen (Tylenol) | Fatal in cats; toxic to dogs |
| Ibuprofen/aspirin | Toxic to both dogs and cats |
| Tea tree oil | Toxic to cats and dogs, especially undiluted |
| Garlic, onion (in any form) | Causes red blood cell destruction in dogs and cats |
| Xylitol (sugar-free sweeteners) | Causes rapid, dangerous hypoglycemia in dogs |
| Hydrogen peroxide (to induce vomiting) | Only use on explicit vet instruction — can cause internal damage |
| Human antifungal creams | Many contain harmful compounds for pets |
| Alcohol on wounds | Kills cells and delays healing |
| Essential oils applied to cats | Cats cannot metabolize many essential oils; can cause serious toxicity |

## When Home Care Is Never Appropriate

Go directly to an emergency vet for:
- Difficulty breathing
- Suspected poisoning
- Collapse or inability to stand
- Suspected broken bones
- Penetrating wounds (eyes, chest, abdomen)
- Seizures
- Repeated vomiting or inability to keep water down
- Swollen or distended abdomen
- Signs of severe pain
- Pale, blue, or yellow gums
