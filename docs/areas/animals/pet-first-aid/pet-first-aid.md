# Pet First Aid

*Emergency care for dogs, cats, and small animals — know this before you need it.*

> **Always call your vet or an emergency animal hospital first when possible.** This guide covers stabilization until professional help is available. First aid is not a substitute for veterinary care — it buys time.
> **ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center:** 888-426-4435 (24/7, fee may apply)
> **Pet Poison Helpline:** 855-764-7661 (24/7, fee may apply)

---

## Preparing Before an Emergency

The best time to prepare is before anything goes wrong. *(AKC, CDC)*

- **Know your nearest 24-hour emergency clinic.** Ask your regular vet now whether they provide after-hours care or partner with an emergency hospital. Keep the name, address, and number on your refrigerator and in your phone.
- **Keep an up-to-date copy of your pet's medical records**, including current medications, vaccination history, and any known conditions. Include this in your first aid kit.
- **Microchip your pet** and keep your contact information current with the chip manufacturer — it's the most reliable way to be reunited if your pet panics and runs during an emergency.
- **Know your pet's normal vital signs** when they are healthy and at rest — heart rate, respiratory rate, temperature, and gum color. You can't spot what's wrong in an emergency if you don't know what's normal.
- **Learn basic skills:** CPR, the Heimlich maneuver, how to muzzle a dog, how to apply a pressure bandage, and how to safely move an injured pet. The Red Cross offers an online dog and cat first aid course. *(AKC)*
- **Prepare a pet disaster kit** and keep it near an exit. Include food and water (3-day minimum), medications (2-week supply), flea/tick/heartworm preventative (1-month supply), vaccination records, a spare leash and collar with ID, a carrier with bedding, and a pet first aid kit. *(CDC)*
- **Create a buddy system** with a trusted neighbor who can evacuate your animals if you're not home. *(CDC)*

## Signs Your Pet Needs Emergency Care

*(ASPCA)*

Seek emergency veterinary care immediately if your pet shows:

- Pale, white, blue, or bright red gums
- Rapid or labored breathing
- Weak or rapid pulse
- Significant change in body temperature
- Difficulty standing or collapse
- Apparent paralysis
- Loss of consciousness
- Seizures
- Excessive or uncontrolled bleeding
- Swollen or painful abdomen
- Blood in vomit, urine, stool, or nasal discharge

## Normal Vital Signs

### Dog Vitals

| Vital | Normal Range |
|-------|-------------|
| Heart rate | 60–140 bpm (smaller dogs = faster) |
| Respiratory rate | 15–30 breaths/min |
| Temperature (rectal) | 101–102.5°F (38.3–39.2°C) |
| Gum color | Pink and moist |
| Capillary refill time | Under 2 seconds |

### Cat Vitals

| Vital | Normal Range |
|-------|-------------|
| Heart rate | 140–220 bpm |
| Respiratory rate | 20–30 breaths/min |
| Temperature (rectal) | 100.5–102.5°F (38–39.2°C) |
| Gum color | Pink and moist |
| Capillary refill time | Under 2 seconds |

**To check capillary refill time:** Press your finger on the gum until it turns white, release, and count seconds until pink returns. Over 2 seconds indicates poor circulation — a veterinary emergency.

**Abnormal gum colors:**
- **Pale/white:** Shock, anemia, internal bleeding
- **Blue/purple:** Oxygen deprivation — emergency
- **Yellow (jaundice):** Liver disease
- **Bright red:** Heatstroke, carbon monoxide, toxin exposure

## Your Safety and Handling an Injured Pet

An injury causes pain, fear, and confusion — even the gentlest pet may bite or scratch. *(AVMA, AKC, ASPCA)*

- **Assess the scene first.** Before approaching, check for hazards — downed power lines, fire, rushing water, traffic, or chemical toxins. Only approach once it is safe. *(AKC)*
- **Avoid hugging an injured pet.** Keep your face away from their mouth. *(AVMA)*
- **Stay calm and move slowly.** Dogs especially match their owner's energy — a calm voice and slow movements reduce aggression. *(AKC)*
- **Muzzle a dog before handling** if it is in severe pain, unless it is vomiting, choking, or having trouble breathing. Even docile dogs bite when hurt. Remove the muzzle when the dog is resting — a muzzled dog cannot pant and will overheat. *(AKC)*
- **Ask for help** whenever possible — moving an injured animal alone increases risk for both of you. *(AVMA)*

### Moving an Injured Dog *(ASPCA, AKC)*
- Approach slowly and calmly. Kneel and say the dog's name. If the dog shows aggression, step back and call for help.
- Fashion a makeshift stretcher using a firm board, sled, or blanket. Gently slide the dog onto it with minimal movement.
- Support the neck and back in case of spinal injury — do not twist or bend the spine.
- For small dogs, use a travel carrier (remove the top rather than forcing the dog through the door).

### Moving an Injured Cat *(ASPCA)*
- Gently drape a blanket or towel over the cat's head to prevent biting.
- Slowly lift the cat and place her into an open-topped carrier or box.
- Support the head and avoid twisting the neck in case of spinal injury.

## CPR

*(AVMA, AKC)*

### When to Start
A pet that is unconscious, not breathing, and has no detectable heartbeat requires CPR. Check for breathing first (observe chest movement; feel for air at nostrils). Check pulse at the femoral artery (inner thigh, high up). Open the mouth, gently grasp the tongue, and pull it forward — quickly check for any foreign object blocking the airway.

### Compression Rate
Compress **100–120 times per minute** — to the beat of *"Stayin' Alive."* Aim to compress the chest by about one-third of its total width. *(AVMA)*

### Dog CPR
1. **Airway:** Lay the dog on its side on a firm surface. Tilt the head back gently to straighten the airway. Remove any visible debris from the mouth.
2. **Rescue breaths:** Hold the dog's mouth closed. Breathe directly into the nose until the chest visibly rises. Give **10 rescue breaths per minute**. For small dogs, cover both nose and mouth.
3. **Compressions by breed type:** *(AVMA)*
   - **Most dogs:** Place heel of hand on the widest part of the chest (behind the elbow). Press down 100–120 times per minute.
   - **Barrel-chested breeds (Bulldogs, Pugs):** Lay on back, compress the sternum.
   - **Keel-chested breeds (Greyhounds, Dobermans):** Place hands just behind the armpit.
4. **Cycle:** If alone — 30 compressions, then 2 rescue breaths, repeat. If help is available — one person gives continuous compressions, the other gives 10 rescue breaths per minute.
5. Continue until the dog breathes independently or a vet takes over.

### Cat CPR
1. **Airway:** Lay the cat on its side. Tilt the head back slightly.
2. **Rescue breaths:** Hold the mouth closed. Cover the entire nose with your mouth. Give small, gentle puffs — cat lungs are small. **One breath every 5 seconds.**
3. **Compressions:** Encircle the bottom of the chest between your thumb and fingers. Compress **100–120 times per minute** to about ½ chest depth.
4. **Cycle:** 30 compressions, 2 breaths.

## Choking

*(AVMA, AKC, ASPCA)*

**Signs:** Pawing at mouth, gagging, difficulty breathing, blue-tinged gums or tongue, distress.

1. If the pet can still breathe, **keep them calm and go directly to a vet.**
2. Open the mouth and look for the object. If visible, try to remove it with two fingers or tweezers. The **flat side of a spoon** may help pull an object closer — do not push it deeper. *(AVMA)*
3. **Do not blindly sweep** your fingers down the throat.
4. **Large dogs — Heimlich maneuver:** Stand behind the dog, wrap arms around the waist, make a fist just below the rib cage, and give 3–5 firm upward thrusts. If lying down, place one hand on the back and squeeze the abdomen upward with the other. *(AVMA, AKC)*
5. **Cats and small dogs:** Pick up by the thighs and gently swing the animal from side to side. If that fails, apply firm forward pressure to the abdomen just behind the ribcage. *(AVMA)* Alternatively, hold upside down (head toward floor) and give 3–5 firm thumps between the shoulder blades. *(AKC)*
6. Check the mouth after each attempt and remove any dislodged objects.
7. **Go to an emergency vet even if the object is dislodged** — internal damage is possible.

## Bleeding

*(AVMA, AKC, ASPCA)*

### External Wounds
1. Apply direct pressure with a clean cloth or gauze. Maintain pressure for **at least 3 minutes** without lifting to check. *(AVMA)*
2. If blood soaks through, **add more material on top — do not remove** existing layers. Removing them disturbs forming clots.
3. Once bleeding slows, secure with a bandage wrap.
4. For paw wounds, elevate the limb above the heart.
5. Do not clean the wound until bleeding has fully stopped. *(AKC)*
6. Seek veterinary care for deep wounds, wounds that won't stop bleeding, or wounds near the face or joints.

### Internal Bleeding
Internal bleeding — from a fall, being struck by a vehicle, or blunt impact — can be more dangerous than external wounds. *(AKC)*

**Signs include:**
- Swollen or painful abdomen
- Pale gums
- Blood in vomit, urine, stools, saliva, or nasal discharge
- Difficulty breathing
- Weakness or collapse

**Action:** Keep the pet still and warm. Do not give food or water. Transport to an emergency vet immediately.

### Tourniquets
Use **only on limbs**, only if bleeding is severe and uncontrollable. Apply 2 inches above the wound. Use a strip of cloth; twist with a stick until bleeding stops. Note the time and do not remove — do not leave in place more than 2 hours.

## Shock

*(AKC)*

Shock can follow severe blood or fluid loss, head injuries, or serious infection.

**Signs:** Rapid heart rate, pale mucous membranes, very low blood pressure, weak pulse, minimal urinary output, collapse.

1. Keep the pet **warm and quiet**.
2. Treat any visible injuries.
3. Do not give food or water.
4. Transport to an emergency vet **immediately** — shock is life-threatening.

---

## Heatstroke

*(AVMA, AKC)*

**High risk situations:** Left in a car, over-exercised in hot weather, flat-faced breeds (Bulldogs, Pugs), elderly or overweight pets, poor kennel ventilation.

**Signs:** Heavy panting or drooling, skin hot to the touch, red or dry/sticky gums, weakness or drowsiness, confusion, vomiting, loss of coordination, collapse.

1. Move the animal to shade or air conditioning **immediately**.
2. Cool with **room-temperature water** — not ice cold, which can cause shock. Apply to the neck, armpits, and groin. Do not submerge. *(AVMA)*
3. Place wet towels on the body; remove and replace as they warm up.
4. If a fan is available, **blow it directly onto the pet.** *(AVMA)*
5. Offer small amounts of cool water once the pet begins to cool down and is conscious. *(AKC)*
6. **Transport to a vet immediately.** Heatstroke causes organ damage that may not be immediately apparent.

---

## Hypothermia

**Signs:** Shivering (then absence of shivering in severe cases), lethargy, cold to the touch, slow heartbeat.

1. Move indoors immediately.
2. Wrap in warm blankets. Use a heating pad set to low, wrapped in a towel — never directly against skin.
3. Do not apply direct heat to extremities — warm the core first.
4. Offer warm (not hot) fluids if conscious.
5. Seek veterinary care — hypothermia can mask other injuries.

## Poisoning

*(AVMA, ASPCA)*

**Common household toxins:**
- **Foods:** Chocolate, xylitol (in gum/peanut butter), grapes/raisins, onions, garlic, macadamia nuts, alcohol, caffeine, raw bread dough
- **Plants:** Lilies (highly toxic to cats), sago palm (all parts, lethal), azalea, rhododendron, foxglove, oleander
- **Medications:** Ibuprofen, acetaminophen, antidepressants, ADHD medications
- **Household:** Antifreeze (ethylene glycol — sweet-smelling, extremely toxic), rat poison, insecticides, cleaning products

**Signs:** Vomiting, drooling, tremors, seizures, lethargy, loss of coordination, pale gums.

**If poisoning is suspected:**
1. **Do not induce vomiting unless specifically instructed** by a vet or poison control. *(AVMA, ASPCA)*
2. Collect the substance — or photograph the label.
3. **Be prepared to provide:** species, breed, age, sex, weight, signs of illness, name of the substance, amount, and when it happened. *(AVMA)*
4. Collect any vomited or chewed material and seal it in a plastic bag to bring to the vet. *(AVMA)*
5. Call ASPCA Poison Control **(888) 426-4435** or your vet immediately. Follow their instructions precisely.

## Broken Bones

*(AVMA)*

1. Keep the animal calm and restrict movement.
2. **Do not attempt to set the bone.**
3. **Do not use antiseptics or ointments on open fractures.** *(AKC)*
4. If a bone is protruding through skin, cover loosely with a clean wet cloth — do not push it back.
5. Muzzle the animal if safe to do so — pain causes biting.
6. Transport with minimal movement — use a board, sled, or blanket as a stretcher. Keep the pet confined to reduce risk of further injury. *(AVMA)*
7. Transport to an emergency vet immediately.

---

## Seizures

*(AVMA)*

1. **Do not restrain the animal or put anything in its mouth.** You will be bitten.
2. Clear the area of furniture, other pets, and objects that could cause injury.
3. **Time the seizure** — most last 1–3 minutes. If possible, **video record it** for your vet. *(AVMA)*
4. Keep the environment quiet and dim.
5. After the seizure (postictal phase), the animal may be confused, blind, or unresponsive for up to 30 minutes. Keep calm and contained.
6. Contact your vet after **any** seizure. **Go to an emergency vet immediately** if: the seizure lasts more than 5 minutes, two or more seizures occur within 24 hours, or the animal does not regain consciousness.

---

## Eye Injuries

1. Do not rub or apply pressure to the eye.
2. If a foreign object is visible and superficial, flush with sterile saline or clean water.
3. For chemical exposure: flush with large amounts of water for 10–15 minutes.
4. Cover loosely with a clean damp cloth if the eye is protruding or severely damaged (do not touch).
5. Seek veterinary care promptly — eye injuries deteriorate quickly.

---

## Burns

1. Cool immediately with cool (not cold) running water for 10–15 minutes. Do not use ice.
2. Do not apply butter, oil, toothpaste, or other home remedies.
3. Cover with a clean, non-stick bandage loosely.
4. Seek veterinary care for any burn covering more than a small area, or any burn on the face, paws, or genitals.

## Disease Risks After Disasters

*(CDC)*

Natural disasters and flooding increase exposure to diseases that can spread between pets and people.

**Rabies**
Transmitted through bites from infected animals or contact with their saliva. After any disaster:
- Report all bite wounds to medical personnel immediately.
- Keep pets on a leash or in a carrier at all times.
- Do not allow pets to interact with wildlife or unfamiliar animals.

**Leptospirosis**
A bacterial disease spread through infected urine and contaminated water, soil, or food. It can cause kidney damage in both pets and people.
- Avoid stagnant or floodwater.
- Do not let pets drink from or play in contaminated water.
- Wash hands after handling pet waste.

**Mosquito, Flea, and Tick-Borne Diseases**
Populations spike after disasters. Includes Lyme disease and West Nile virus.
- Keep pets on regular preventative medications.
- Keep pets away from wildlife and stray animals.

**After returning home:** Check for sharp objects, spilled chemicals, and exposed wiring before letting pets roam freely. Animal behavior may change significantly after a flood or storm — normally calm pets may act fearfully or aggressively.

## Assembling a Pet First Aid Kit

*(AVMA, AKC)*

| Item | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| Gauze pads and rolls | Wound dressing |
| Non-stick bandages / strips of clean cloth | Controlling bleeding, protecting wounds |
| Adhesive bandage wrap (vet wrap) | Securing dressings |
| Self-adhering, non-stick tape | Securing bandages |
| Digital rectal thermometer + sterile lubricant | Temperature checks |
| Sterile saline solution | Eye/wound flushing |
| Hydrogen peroxide 3% | Inducing vomiting (only on vet instruction) |
| Activated charcoal | Absorbing poison (only on vet/poison control instruction) |
| Sterile lubricating jelly | Protecting wounds and eyes |
| Antibiotic first aid ointment | Wound care |
| Styptic powder | Stopping nail/minor bleeding |
| Tweezers | Tick and splinter removal |
| Scissors with blunt end | Cutting bandage materials |
| Small electric clippers | Clearing fur around wounds |
| Disposable gloves | Infection control |
| Flashlight/penlight | Examining mouth, ears, wounds |
| Instant cold pack | Reducing swelling |
| Oral syringe or turkey baster | Giving fluids or flushing wounds |
| Cotton balls or swabs | Cleaning and applying medication |
| Muzzle (correct size for your pet) | Preventing bites during pain |
| Elizabethan collar (cone) | Preventing self-injury |
| Emergency blanket | Shock and hypothermia |
| Spare leash and collar with ID tag | Secure transport |
| Extra carrier or travel crate | Safe transport for small animals |
| Copies of vet records and vaccination history | Documentation |
| Vet and poison control phone numbers | Emergency contacts |
| Pet first aid reference guide | Step-by-step instructions |

> **Check the kit regularly** to make sure nothing has expired. Keep one at home and one in the car if you travel frequently with your pet. *(AKC)*
