# Backyard Wildlife Guide

*Identify and understand the animals that share your outdoor space.*

> **A note on feeding wildlife:** Providing food can attract animals away from their natural diet and make them dependent on humans. It can also lead to conflict. When in doubt, observe from a distance and let them forage naturally.

---

## Birds

### American Robin *(Turdus migratorius)*
**Appearance:** Orange-red breast, dark gray-black back, yellow bill.
**Habitat:** Lawns, gardens, parks. Frequently seen pulling earthworms from soil.
**Diet:** Earthworms, insects, berries, fruit.
**Behavior:** Territorial nesters. Familiar song: a rich, caroling *cheerily, cheer-up, cheerio*.
**Season:** Year-round in most of the US; migrates north in spring.

---

### American Crow *(Corvus brachyrhynchos)*
**Appearance:** All-black body, fan-shaped tail, heavy bill. Larger than a robin; smaller than a raven.
**Habitat:** Ubiquitous — urban, suburban, woodland edges.
**Diet:** Omnivore. Insects, carrion, small animals, eggs, grain, garbage.
**Behavior:** Highly intelligent. Uses tools, remembers human faces, forms complex social groups. Loud *caw* call; flocks in autumn and winter.
**Note:** If crows are mobbing an area noisily, they've likely spotted a predator (hawk, owl, or cat).

---

### Northern Cardinal *(Cardinalis cardinalis)*
**Appearance:** Male: brilliant red with crest and black mask. Female: warm brown with red tinges.
**Habitat:** Woodland edges, shrubs, gardens.
**Diet:** Seeds, berries, insects.
**Behavior:** Does not migrate. Male feeds female during courtship. Aggressive toward window reflections — cover windows with film to reduce collisions.
**Call:** Loud, clear whistles. *What-cheer, cheer, cheer* or *purdy-purdy-purdy.*

---

### House Sparrow *(Passer domesticus)*
**Appearance:** Brown and black streaked back; male has gray crown and black bib.
**Habitat:** Human settlements — walls, gutters, eaves, dense shrubbery.
**Diet:** Seeds, grain, human food scraps.
**Behavior:** Non-native (introduced from Europe). Extremely adaptable. Nests in cavities, can displace native cavity nesters (bluebirds, Tree Swallows).

---

### Red-tailed Hawk *(Buteo jamaicensis)*
**Appearance:** Broad, rounded wings; rust-red tail visible from below in adults. Highly variable plumage.
**Habitat:** Open areas with nearby perch trees — fields, roadsides, parks.
**Diet:** Rodents (primary), rabbits, birds, snakes.
**Behavior:** The quintessential "screaming eagle" — the piercing *kree-eee-ar* call used in almost every film featuring any large bird of prey.
**Role:** Controls rodent populations. A resident hawk is a sign of a healthy ecosystem.

---

## Mammals

### Eastern Gray Squirrel *(Sciurus carolinensis)*
**Appearance:** Gray above, white below; bushy tail. About 18–20 inches tip to tail.
**Habitat:** Deciduous woodlands, urban parks, suburban yards.
**Diet:** Acorns, nuts, seeds, fungi, buds, occasionally bird eggs.
**Behavior:** Scatter-hoards food — buries thousands of nuts, relocates them by smell. Forgotten caches sprout trees. Alarm call: rapid chattering and tail flicking.
**Note:** Non-native on the West Coast (from eastern North America).

---

### Eastern Cottontail *(Sylvilagus floridanus)*
**Appearance:** Brown-gray fur, white fluffy tail, large ears.
**Habitat:** Woodland edges, brushy areas, gardens.
**Diet:** Grasses, clover, garden vegetables, bark, twigs in winter.
**Behavior:** Dawn and dusk active (crepuscular). Does not burrow — nests in shallow depressions in grass. Young are born helpless and left in nests covered with fur and leaves.
**Garden note:** Rabbits will eat young plants. Low fencing (2 ft) prevents most damage.

---

### Virginia Opossum *(Didelphis virginiana)*
**Appearance:** Pale gray, pointed white face, naked tail; about the size of a cat.
**Habitat:** Woodland edges, suburbs, anywhere near food.
**Diet:** True omnivore — insects, carrion, fruit, garbage, small vertebrates.
**Behavior:** North America's only marsupial. Young (joeys) develop in the mother's pouch. "Playing possum" (catatonic defensive response) is involuntary, not a choice. Eats ticks in significant numbers — beneficial to have around. Not a significant rabies vector (low body temperature).

---

### Raccoon *(Procyon lotor)*
**Appearance:** Black facial mask, ringed tail, grayish fur, nimble front paws.
**Habitat:** Deciduous forests, urban and suburban areas near water.
**Diet:** Highly opportunistic — crayfish, frogs, insects, eggs, fruit, garbage.
**Behavior:** Primarily nocturnal. Excellent climbers. Manipulative front paws allow them to open containers and latches. Intelligent and adaptable. **Secure trash cans with bungee cords or locking lids.**
**Note:** Rabies risk is real. A raccoon active in daylight, stumbling, or showing no fear of humans is a concern — call animal control.

---

### White-tailed Deer *(Odocoileus virginianus)*
**Appearance:** Reddish-brown in summer, gray-brown in winter. White underside to tail (the "flag").
**Habitat:** Woodland edges, fields, gardens, suburban areas.
**Diet:** Grasses, leaves, twigs, agricultural crops, ornamental plants.
**Behavior:** Most active at dawn and dusk. Bucks grow and shed antlers annually. Fawns are left alone by mothers for hours — a motionless fawn is not abandoned; do not approach or touch it.
**Garden note:** Deer are persistent garden browsers. Physical fencing (8 ft) is the only reliable deterrent.

---

### Eastern Mole *(Scalopus aquaticus)*
**Appearance:** Velvety gray-brown fur, no external ears, paddle-shaped front feet. Rarely seen above ground.
**Habitat:** Moist, loose soil — lawns, meadows, woodland.
**Diet:** Earthworms (primary), insect larvae, grubs.
**Behavior:** Creates surface ridges (feeding tunnels) and deeper permanent tunnels. **Beneficial:** aerates soil, controls soil insects. **Nuisance:** can damage lawn and dislodge plant roots. Rarely causes serious harm.

---

## Reptiles & Amphibians

### Eastern Garter Snake *(Thamnophis sirtalis)*
**Appearance:** Slender; yellow, blue, or white stripe down dark body. Variable. Up to 4 feet.
**Habitat:** Meadows, woodland edges, gardens, near water.
**Diet:** Earthworms, slugs, frogs, small mice.
**Behavior:** Non-venomous. Beneficial garden predator. Will vibrate tail (mimicking rattlesnake), release musk, or bite if handled — bites are minor. Hibernates communally in winter.

---

### American Toad *(Anaxyrus americanus)*
**Appearance:** Brown, warty skin; distinct parotoid glands (large bumps behind eyes). Rotund.
**Habitat:** Gardens, yards, woodland edges, wherever insects are found.
**Diet:** Insects, slugs, worms — a single toad eats thousands of pest insects per season.
**Behavior:** Largely nocturnal. Will defend itself by secreting mild skin toxin (wash hands after handling). The warts do not cause warts in humans. Highly valuable as a pest controller.
**Note:** Toads in garden beds are a sign of a healthy, chemical-free environment.

## Building a Pollinator-Friendly Backyard

Pollinators — bees, butterflies, moths, hummingbirds, bats, and more — are responsible for one in three bites of food we eat. Their populations are declining worldwide, largely due to habitat loss and pesticide use. You don't need a large yard to help. A balcony, a raised bed, or even a single pot of coneflowers makes a difference.

---

### Choose the Right Location

Pick a spot that gets **full or partial sun** with some wind protection. Most wildflowers and the pollinators that visit them need sun to thrive. Butterflies in particular like to bask — open, warm areas are most productive.

Before planting, assess your **soil type**: sandy and well-drained, or clay-like and moisture-retentive. Your soil will determine which plants succeed. Pollinators do best with **moist, moderately drained soil** — not waterlogged, not bone dry.

---

### Select the Right Plants

**Go native.** Native plants are adapted to your local climate, soil, and pollinators. They require less maintenance once established, tend to be hardier than ornamentals, and are far more useful to local species. Find a nursery that specializes in regional natives.

**Avoid modern hybrid flowers**, especially "doubled" varieties. Plant breeders often inadvertently eliminate pollen, nectar, and fragrance when engineering showier blooms. What looks beautiful to us may be empty to a bee.

**Stagger your bloom times.** Use a mix of early, middle, and late-season species so there is nectar available from early spring through fall. Pollinators need food across their entire active season — a garden that peaks in July and goes quiet in August leaves them short.

**Include larval host plants.** If you want butterflies, you need to feed their caterpillars too. Check a butterfly field guide to identify the plants local species depend on for egg-laying and larval development. Expect some leaf damage — that's the point.

**Include night-blooming species** to support moths and bats, which pollinate after dark and are often overlooked.

**Request plants by their Latin name** at nurseries to ensure you get the right species. Common names vary and can lead to the wrong plant.

**Ask whether plants have been treated with systemic insecticides or neonicotinoids** before purchasing. Treated plants can harm the very pollinators they're meant to attract.

---

### Plant for Density, Not Variety Alone

Plant in **clumps rather than scattered singles**. A mass of the same flower is far easier for pollinators to locate and use than a collection of one-offs. Group three, five, or seven plants of the same species together, then move on to the next.

---

### Provide Water

Add a **shallow dish of water** on a deck, railing, or windowsill. Place several semi-submerged stones in the dish so pollinators have somewhere to land while drinking — without the risk of drowning.

A **backyard pond**, even a small one, benefits damselflies (which help control mosquitoes), butterflies, and bees. It also creates habitat for amphibians.

For butterflies and bees that need minerals, create a **damp salt lick**: place a drip line or bird bath directly on bare soil and mix in a small amount of sea salt or wood ash. Overripe fruit — bananas, oranges — placed in a shallow dish will attract species that forage on fermented material as well as nectar.

---

### Support Nesting

Most people know about honeybees in hives. Most native bees are nothing like that.

**Ground nesters** (the majority of native bee species) need a small patch of bare, well-drained soil — ideally facing south, away from sprinklers. They don't sting aggressively and rarely bother anyone.

**Cavity nesters** use dead wood, hollow stems, and brush piles. If a dead tree or large limb is not a safety hazard, leave it. When pruning shrubs, cut hollow stems (raspberry, elderberry, sumac, goldenrod, coneflower) to about a foot high — they become ready-made nesting tubes.

**Build or install a bee house.** Blocks of untreated wood with holes drilled 3–5 inches deep in varying diameters, or bundled hollow reeds and cardboard tubes, work well. Mount under eaves or on a post. Clean annually to prevent the spread of parasites and disease.

---

### Leave the Wild Alone

**Leave leaf litter in fall.** Dead leaves and plant material are shelter and food for pollinators over winter. Avoid raking your entire yard — even a corner left alone is useful.

**Leave an unmowed zone.** A strip of lawn left to grow, seeded with native grasses and wildflowers, provides foraging and nesting habitat at minimal cost. Add a small sign explaining the purpose if neighbors ask.

**Leave dead wood.** Fallen logs, brush piles, and snags (standing dead trees) provide nesting sites for native bees, beetles, and flies.

---

### Limit Pesticides

Pesticides are one of the primary drivers of pollinator decline. Before using any:

- Try hand-removing pests or mechanical controls first
- Rotate crops and water as needed (not on a schedule)
- Choose plants that have not been pre-treated
- Grow organically where possible — native predators like lacewings and lady beetles will manage many pests

If you must spray, use the **least-toxic material available**, follow the label exactly, and **apply only at night** when pollinators are not active. Never spray when flowers are in bloom.

### Common Pollinators You May See

| Pollinator | What to look for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Bumblebee** *(Bombus spp.)* | Large, fuzzy, black and yellow; slow, low flight | Colonial; nests in ground cavities or dense grass |
| **Eastern Honeybee** *(Apis mellifera)* | Slender, amber-and-black; very common at flowers | Non-native; hive-dwelling; important agricultural pollinator |
| **Monarch Butterfly** *(Danaus plexippus)* | Orange and black; large, slow wingbeats | Requires milkweed for larvae; long-distance migrant |
| **Eastern Tiger Swallowtail** *(Papilio glaucus)* | Yellow with black tiger stripes; large | Visits many garden flowers; larvae on wild cherry and tulip poplar |
| **Ruby-throated Hummingbird** *(Archilochus colubris)* | Iridescent green; rapid wingbeats; hovers | Attracted to red tubular flowers; only hummingbird in eastern US |
| **Sphinx Moth / Clearwing** *(Hemaris spp.)* | Hovers like a hummingbird; often mistaken for one | Active at dusk; important pollinator of deep tubular flowers |
| **Mason Bee** *(Osmia spp.)* | Small, dark, metallic; often blue or green | Solitary ground or cavity nester; among the most efficient pollinators |

## Interpreting What You See

| Sign | Likely culprit |
|------|---------------|
| Lawn ridges, soil mounds | Mole (tunnels) or vole (runways) |
| Excavated flower bulbs | Squirrel or vole |
| Knocked-over bird feeder | Raccoon or bear |
| Clean-cut seedlings at ground level | Rabbit |
| Stripped bark on young trees | Deer (rubbing) or rabbit (gnawing in winter) |
| Scratched compost bin | Raccoon or opossum |
| Half-eaten tomatoes with teeth marks | Squirrel or rat |
| Bird nests in gutters | House sparrow or starling |
